## Sourced claims

### E1. Harris (1954): distribution, and difference of meaning tracks difference of distribution
- Source: `harris1954distributional`
- Location: pp. 146, 155-157.
- Quotes: Definition (p. 146): "The distribution of an element will be understood as the sum of all its environments." Purpose (p. 146): "Here we will discuss how each language can be described in terms of a distributional structure, i.e. in terms of the occurrence of parts (ultimately sounds) relative to other parts, and how this description is complete without intrusion of other features such as history or meaning." The key sentence (p. 156, checked on the page image): "if we consider words or morphemes A and B to be more different in meaning than A and C, then we will often find that the distributions of A and B are more different than the distributions of A and C. In other words, difference of meaning correlates with difference of distribution." The example (p. 156): "If we consider oculist and eye-doctor we find that, as our corpus of actually-occurring utterances grows, these two occur in almost the same environments", while "there are many sentence environments in which oculist occurs but lawyer does not: e.g. I've had my eyes examined by the same oculist for twenty years". Footnote 17 (p. 156): "This particular pair was suggested to me by Y. Bar-Hillel, who however considers that distributional correlates of meaning differences cannot be established." The graded statement (p. 157): "If A and B have almost identical environments except chiefly for sentences which contain both, we say they are synonyms: oculist and eye-doctor. If A and B have some environments in common and some not (e.g. oculist and lawyer) we say that they have different meanings, the amount of meaning difference corresponding roughly to the amount of difference in their environments." Also pp. 155-156: "language is not merely a bag of words but a tool with particular properties which have been fashioned in the course of its use."
- Note: Harris says "often" and "correlates", and his footnote records Bar-Hillel's disagreement. The phrase "distributional hypothesis" does not occur in Harris or Firth (searched; 0 hits); it is a later label (`jurafsky2026embeddings` p. 1 uses it). Do not put it in Harris's mouth. Harris also writes that probabilities are needed for word-level statements (p. 146: "it would be necessary to speak in terms of probability, based on the frequency of that occurrence in a sample"), a link to `prediction`. `jurafsky2026embeddings` (p. 1) quotes the same p. 157 phrase, which cross-checks the page.

### E2. Firth (1957): "You shall know a word by the company it keeps!"
- Source: `firth1957synopsis`
- Location: p. 11 (checked on the page image).
- Quote, with its context: "It follows that a text in such established usage may contain sentences such as ' Don't be such an ass! ', ' You silly ass! ', ' What an ass he is ! ' In these examples, the word ass is in familiar and habitual company, commonly collocated with you silly-, he is a silly-, don't be such an-. You shall know a word by the company it keeps! One of the meanings of ass is its habitual collocation with such other words as those above quoted." (The source prints a dash after "silly" and "an" to mark the slot; spacing around the quotation marks is the source's.) The same paragraph begins "As Wittgenstein says, ' the meaning of words lies in their use.'" (footnote 4: "Philosophical Investigations, pp. 80, 109").
- The next paragraph (p. 11): "From the preceding remarks, it will be seen that collocation is not to be interpreted as context, by which the whole conceptual meaning is implied. Nor is it to be confused with citation." Footnote 5 (p. 11): "See ' Modes of Meaning ', pp. 124-7. In this essay, collocation is first suggested as a technical term."
- Exact wording: the sentence ends with an exclamation mark, and "company" is the word (not "the company it keeps" as a paraphrase of a longer line). The dating: the scan is a 1962 printing; the 1957 date and the publisher (Philological Society special volume) are from `brunila2022company`'s and `jurafsky2026embeddings`'s reference lists. Safe citation: "Firth (1957), p. 11".
- Note: in Firth the "company" is **collocation**, one "mode of meaning" among several, inside his larger theory of "context of situation". See D1. The illustrating word is "ass" (the animal and the insult), which is itself an example of a word with more than one meaning.

### E3. The distributional idea in later accounts
- Sources: `jurafsky2026embeddings`, `brunila2022company`.
- Quotes: Jurafsky and Martin, p. 1: "Words that occur in similar contexts tend to have similar meanings. This link between similarity in how words are distributed and similarity in what they mean is called the distributional hypothesis. The hypothesis was first formulated in the 1950s by linguists like Joos (1950), Harris (1954), and Firth (1957)". p. 22 (Historical Notes): "As Joos (1950) put it, the linguist's "meaning" of a morpheme. . . is by definition the set of conditional probabilities of its occurrence in context with all other morphemes." p. 5: Osgood's 1957 idea "to use a point in three-dimensional space to represent the connotation of a word". Brunila and LaViolette, p. 1: Firth and Harris are "often credited with the invention of "distributional semantics."" and, after a close reading, "we discover two distinct and in many ways divergent theories of meaning. One focuses exclusively on the internal workings of linguistic forms, while the other invites us to consider words in new company". p. 2: the 1990s vector papers such as LSA "generally did not cite Firth or Harris".
- Check made here: none of Deerwester et al. 1990, Bengio et al. 2003, Mikolov et al. 2013a or 2013b, Pennington et al. 2014, or Collobert and Weston 2008 mentions Harris or Firth (text search, 0 hits). Collobert et al. 2011 cites Harris's 1968 *Mathematical Structures of Language* for its ranking criterion (pp. 2515-2516), not the 1954 paper.
- Note: so the line "Harris and Firth, then LSA, then word2vec" is a lineage drawn afterwards by textbooks and surveys, not one the engineers cited at the time. The chapter can tell it as a history of ideas while saying that the 1990s and 2010s papers mostly did not cite the linguists. Joos (1950) is a third name the textbook gives; not fetched.

### E4. One-hot vectors: every word equally far from every other
- Sources: `goodfellow2016deep`, `lecun2015deep`, `mikolov2013efficient`, `bengio2003neural`, `elman1990finding` (back-reference).
- Quotes: Goodfellow et al., ch. 12, p. 459: "In this interpretation, we view the raw symbols as points in a space of dimension equal to the vocabulary size. The word representations embed those points in a feature space of lower dimension. In the original space, every word is represented by a one-hot vector, so every pair of words is at Euclidean distance sqrt(2) from each other. In the embedding space, words that frequently appear in similar contexts (or any pair of words sharing some "features" learned by the model) are close to each other." (The source prints a square-root sign.) LeCun et al., p. 441: "Each word in the context is presented to the network as a one-of-N vector, that is, one component has a value of 1 and the rest are 0." Also p. 441: "N-grams treat each word as an atomic unit, so they cannot generalize across semantically related sequences of words, whereas neural language models can because they associate each word with a vector of real valued features, and semantically related words end up close to each other in that vector space". Mikolov et al. 2013a, p. 1: "Many current NLP systems and techniques treat words as atomic units - there is no notion of similarity between words, as these are represented as indices in a vocabulary." p. 3: "N previous words are encoded using 1-of-V coding, where V is size of the vocabulary." Bengio et al. 2003, p. 1138: when discrete variables take many values, "most observed objects are almost maximally far from each other in hamming distance."
- Note: the reader met this in `structure` (Elman's 31-bit vectors "orthogonal to every other vector", S9 of that ledger). Terms: "one-hot", "one-of-N", "1-of-V" are synonyms; pick "one-hot" and mention the others once at most.

### E5. Distributed representations (the 1986 precedent)
- Sources: `rumelhart1986learning`, `lecun2015deep`, `bengio2003neural`.
- Quotes: Rumelhart, Hinton, Williams 1986, Fig. 3 caption (p. 534): the network has "24 input units on the left for representing (person 1)" and each input group "is totally connected to its own group of 6 units in the second layer. These groups learn to encode people and relationships as distributed patterns of activity." Fig. 4 caption (p. 535, checked on the page image): "The weights from the 24 input units that represent people to the 6 units in the second layer that learn distributed representations of people." "Unit 1 is primarily concerned with the distinction between English and Italian ... This means that the representation of an English person is very similar to the representation of their Italian equivalent." "Unit 2 encodes which generation a person belongs to, and unit 6 encodes which branch of the family they come from. The features captured by the hidden units are not at all explicit in the input and output encodings, since these use a separate unit for each person." Text (p. 535): the network was "trained on 100 of the 104 possible triples". LeCun et al. 2015, p. 441: "The network learns word vectors that contain many active components each of which can be interpreted as a separate feature of the word, as was first demonstrated27 in the context of learning distributed representations for symbols" (ref. 27 is Rumelhart, Hinton, Williams 1986). Also p. 441: "Such representations are called distributed representations because their elements (the features) are not mutually exclusive and their many configurations correspond to the variations seen in the observed data." Bengio et al. 2003, p. 1140, credit the "(old) idea of learning a distributed representation for symbolic data, that was advocated in the early days of connectionism (Hinton, 1986, Elman, 1990)".
- Note: the family-tree network is the cleanest pre-word example of the chapter's central idea: one-hot in, learned dense code in the middle, similar things get similar codes as a side effect of the task. It links back to `backprop`, which already cites `rumelhart1986learning`. "Distributed" here means the meaning is spread over many units; contrast it with one-hot (each word its own unit).

### E6. Latent semantic analysis (Deerwester et al., 1990)
- Source: `deerwester1990indexing`
- Location: pp. 391-397, 399-405.
- Quotes and numbers:
  - Abstract (p. 391): "The particular technique used is singular-value decomposition, in which a large term by document matrix is decomposed into a set of ca. 100 orthogonal factors from which the original matrix can be approximated by linear combination. Documents are represented by ca. 100 item vectors of factor weights. Queries are represented as pseudo-document vectors formed from weighted combinations of terms, and documents with supra-threshold cosine values are returned."
  - The problem (pp. 391-392): "There are actually two sides to the issue; we will call them broadly synonymy and polysemy." "two people choose the same main key word for a single well-known object less than 20% of the time (Furnas, Landauer, Gomez, & Dumais, 1987)" (p. 392). Footnote 1 (p. 391): "By "semantic structure" we mean here only the correlation structure in the way in which individual words appear in documents".
  - The method is two-mode factor analysis via SVD (pp. 394-395); "the dot product or cosine between vectors representing two documents corresponds to their estimated similarity" (p. 395). On dimensions (p. 396): "we are not interested in being able to visualize the space or understand it" and "What we mean by "works best" is not (as is customary in some other fields) what reproduces the greatest amount of variance in the original matrix, but what will give the best retrieval effectiveness."
  - Toy example (p. 396, Table 2, checked on the page image): nine Bellcore technical memo titles, five about human-computer interaction (c1-c5) and four about graphs (m1-m4); twelve terms that occur in more than one title; cell entries are raw counts. Fig. 1 (p. 397) plots them in two dimensions: "In this reduced space, even documents c3 and c5 which share no terms with the query are near it." The appendix (pp. 405-406) prints the SVD: singular values 3.34, 2.54, 2.35, 1.64, 1.50, 1.31, 0.85, 0.56, 0.36, and the rank-2 reconstruction.
  - MED (pp. 400-402): 1033 medical abstracts, 30 queries, 5823 index terms; "A 100-factor SVD of the 5823 term by 1033 document matrix". "The average difference in precision between the LSI and the term matching method is .06 (.51 vs. .45), which represents a 13% improvement over raw term matching." Fig. 5 (p. 402): "Precision more than doubles (from about .20 to .50) as the number of factors is increased from 10 to 100." (The text, p. 402, says "from .25 to .52".)
  - CISI (pp. 402-403): 1460 documents, 35 queries, 5135 terms; "Average precision is .11 for both LSI and term matching"; "SMART, on the other hand, results in reliably better performance than LSI".
- Note: LSA was built for **document retrieval**, and the 1990 paper reports a mixed result: 13% better than term matching on MED, no better on CISI, and worse than SMART on CISI. "About 100 dimensions" is the 1990 paper's number; `jurafsky2026embeddings` (p. 23) says later LSA practice used "the first 300 dimensions" with log and entropy weighting. The paper's own name is "latent semantic indexing (LSI)"; LSA became the usual name (J&M p. 23: "latent semantic indexing (LSI) model (Deerwester et al., 1988) recast as LSA"). The bridge for the reader: this is the SVD they know from PCA, applied to a word-by-document count table instead of a centred data matrix. Deerwester et al. call it "two-mode factor analysis", not PCA; the PCA link is the chapter's (and standard), and the honest wording is "the same matrix factorization (SVD) that underlies PCA".

### E7. LSA's own statement of the one-point-per-word weakness
- Source: `deerwester1990indexing`
- Location: p. 405.
- Quote: "While the LSI method deals nicely with the synonymy problem, it offers only a partial solution to the polysemy problem. ... The failure comes in the fact that every term is represented as just one point in the space. That is, a word with more than one entirely different meaning (e.g., "bank"), is represented as a weighted average of the different meanings. If none of the real meanings is like the average meaning, this may create a serious distortion." And: "What is needed is some way to detect the fact that a particular term has several distinct meanings and to subcategorize it and place it in several points in the space. We have not yet found a satisfactory way to do that".
- Note: this is the outline's "one vector per word as a weakness", stated in 1990 by the people who built the first word vectors. It applies unchanged to word2vec and GloVe (E21, E22 preview: the 50-dimensional GloVe "bank" has only financial neighbours). Hand-off: contextual vectors (different vector per occurrence) belong to later chapters; `jurafsky2026embeddings` p. 11: "Word2vec embeddings are static embeddings, meaning that the method learns one fixed embedding for each word in the vocabulary."

### E8. Bengio et al. (2003): the curse of dimensionality and the fix
- Source: `bengio2003neural`
- Location: pp. 1137-1140.
- Quotes: Abstract (p. 1137): "A goal of statistical language modeling is to learn the joint probability function of sequences of words in a language. This is intrinsically difficult because of the curse of dimensionality: a word sequence on which the model will be tested is likely to be different from all the word sequences seen during training." "We propose to fight the curse of dimensionality by learning a distributed representation for words which allows each training sentence to inform the model about an exponential number of semantically neighboring sentences." "Generalization is obtained because a sequence of words that has never been seen before gets high probability if it is made of words that are similar (in the sense of having a nearby representation) to words forming an already seen sentence." The count (p. 1137): "if one wants to model the joint distribution of 10 consecutive words in a natural language with a vocabulary V of size 100,000, there are potentially 100 000^10 - 1 = 10^50 - 1 free parameters." The example (pp. 1139-1140): "having seen the sentence "The cat is walking in the bedroom" in the training corpus should help us generalize to make the sentence "A dog was running in a room" almost as likely, simply because "dog" and "cat" (resp. "the" and "a", "room" and "bedroom", etc...) have similar semantic and grammatical roles." The recipe (p. 1139): "1. associate with each word in the vocabulary a distributed word feature vector (a real-valued vector in R^m), 2. express the joint probability function of word sequences in terms of the feature vectors of these words in the sequence, and 3. learn simultaneously the word feature vectors and the parameters of that probability function." Sizes (p. 1139): "The number of features (e.g. m = 30, 60 or 100 in the experiments) is much smaller than the size of the vocabulary (e.g. 17,000)." Why it works (p. 1140): "similar" words are expected to have a similar feature vector, and because the probability function is a smooth function of these feature values, a small change in the features will induce a small change in the probability."
- Note: the reader's bridge: n-gram smoothing shares probability between contexts by backing off to shorter contexts (p. 1138, "gluing" very short pieces); the neural model shares it between *similar words*. Hand-off with `prediction`, which lists "the curse of dimensionality" among its concepts: `prediction` should state the problem, this chapter the fix. Agree who quotes the 10^50 sentence.

### E9. The embedding table: a lookup, shared by all positions, learned with everything else
- Sources: `bengio2003neural`, `collobert2008unified`, `goodfellow2016deep`.
- Quotes: Bengio et al., p. 1141: "A mapping C from any element i of V to a real vector C(i) in R^m. It represents the distributed feature vectors associated with each word in the vocabulary. In practice, C is represented by a |V| x m matrix of free parameters." and "with C being shared across all the words in the context". Figure 1 (p. 1142) labels it "Table look-up in C". Parameter count (p. 1143): "The number of free parameters is |V|(1 + nm + h) + h(1 + (n - 1)m). The dominating factor is |V|(nm + h)." Also p. 1142: "the number of free parameters only scales linearly with V, the number of words in the vocabulary." Collobert and Weston, PDF p. 2: "Each word i in D is embedded into a d-dimensional space using a lookup table LT_W(.): LT_W(i) = W_i, where W in R^(d x |D|) is a matrix of parameters to be learnt". Goodfellow et al., p. 459: "We sometimes call these word representations word embeddings."
- Note: this is exactly the "embedding table" of `destination` (one row per token, random at the start, moved by training). The chapter can close that loop. "x" in the quotes stands for the multiplication sign.

### E10. Learning the vectors jointly beat fixing them from co-occurrence PCA (Bengio et al.)
- Source: `bengio2003neural`
- Location: p. 1141.
- Quote: "The idea of using a vector-space representation for words has been well exploited in the area of information retrieval (for example see work by Schutze, 1993), where feature vectors for words are learned on the basis of their probability of co-occurring in the same documents (Latent Semantic Indexing, see Deerwester et al., 1990). An important difference is that here we look for a representation for words that is helpful in representing compactly the probability distribution of word sequences from natural language text. Experiments suggest that learning jointly the representation (word features) and the model is very useful. We tried (unsuccessfully) using as fixed word features for each word w the first principal components of the co-occurrence frequencies of w with the words occurring in text around the occurrence of w. This is similar to what has been done with documents for information retrieval with LSI."
- Note: the bridge sentence the outline wants, in the authors' own words: LSA/PCA vectors versus vectors learned for prediction. Contrast with the 2000 conference version, which initialized its best Brown model's "30 word features" "with the SVD method" (`bengio2000neural`, PDF p. 6), while the 2003 paper says "Random initialization of the word features was done" (p. 1148). Do not over-generalize the "unsuccessfully" remark: it is about using PCA features *fixed*, in their model.

### E11. Bengio et al. (2003): corpora and results against n-grams
- Source: `bengio2003neural`

### E16. word2vec, part 2 (Mikolov et al. 2013b): negative sampling, subsampling, phrases
- Source: `mikolov2013distributed`
- Location: pp. 1, 3-7.
- Quotes and numbers: p. 1: "an optimized single-machine implementation can train on more than 100 billion words in one day." p. 1: "Somewhat surprisingly, many of these patterns can be represented as linear translations. For example, the result of a vector calculation vec("Madrid") - vec("Spain") + vec("France") is closer to vec("Paris") than to any other word vector [9, 8]." p. 1 (abstract): "An inherent limitation of word representations is their indifference to word order and their inability to represent idiomatic phrases. Phrases (pp. 6-7): a phrase analogy set of 3218 examples; "a dataset with about 33 billion words", 1000 dimensions, "resulted in a model that reached an accuracy of 72%", "lower accuracy 66% when we reduced the size of the training dataset to 6B words". Why addition works (p. 7): "As the word vectors are trained to predict the surrounding words in the sentence, the vectors can be seen as representing the distribution of the context in which a word appears."

Negative sampling turns the prediction into many small yes/no logistic regressions, a direct bridge to the reader's logistic regression; Collobert and Weston's real-versus-corrupted window (E12) is the ancestor the paper itself cites (p. 3: "This is similar to hinge loss used by Collobert and Weston [2] who trained the models by ranking the data above noise"). ### E18. Prediction versus counting: three positions
- Sources: `baroni2014dont`, `levy2014neural`, `levy2015improving` (and `pennington2014glove`, E17).
- Quotes: Baroni et al., abstract (p. 238): "the results, to our own surprise, show that the buzz is fully justified, as the context-predicting models obtain a thorough and resounding victory against their count-based counterparts." Levy and Goldberg 2014 (NIPS), abstract (p. 1): skip-gram with negative sampling "is implicitly factorizing a word-context matrix, whose cells are the pointwise mutual information (PMI) of the respective word and context pairs, shifted by a global constant" and "When dense low-dimensional vectors are preferred, exact factorization with SVD can achieve solutions that are at least as good as SGNS's solutions for word similarity tasks. On analogy questions SGNS remains superior to SVD." Levy, Goldberg, Dagan 2015, abstract (p. 211): "We reveal that much of the performance gains of word embeddings are due to certain system design choices and hyperparameter optimizations, rather than the embedding algorithms themselves. - Note: this is the strongest support for the outline's bridge ("LSA is PCA on a word-document matrix; embeddings are the learned, predictive version"): by 2014-2015 it was shown that word2vec is, in effect, factorizing a (weighted, shifted) word-context count matrix. One correction to the bridge: word2vec and GloVe use **word-by-context-word** counts in a small window, LSA used **word-by-document** counts; that difference matters (`jurafsky2026embeddings` p. 24 credits Schutze 1992 with SVD on the term-term matrix). ### E19. Analogies, part 1: the input words must be excluded
- Sources: `mikolov2013efficient`, `mikolov2013distributed`, `levy2014linguistic`, `linzen2016issues`, `rogers2017too`, `nissim2020fair`, `jurafsky2026embeddings`.
- Quotes: Mikolov et al. 2013a p. 5: "(we discard the input question words during this search)". 2013b p. 5: "(we discard the input words from the search)". Levy and Goldberg 2014 (CoNLL) p. 173: the analogy is solved by maximizing similarity over "V ... the vocabulary excluding the question words b, a and a*". Linzen p. 14: "The x* obtained from Equation 2 is often trivial (typically equal to b). In practice, most studies exclude a, a* and b from consideration". Linzen p. 16 (results, SGNS vectors): "When these words were not excluded, the nearest neighbor of a* - a + b was b in 93% of the cases and a* in 5% of the cases (it was never a)." Rogers et al. p. 137, Fig. 2: "if we do not exclude the source vectors, b is the most likely to be predicted". Nissim et al. p. 491: "In the default implementation of word2vec (Mikolov et al. 2013), gensim (Rehurek and Sojka 2010) as well as the code from Bolukbasi et al. (2016), the input terms of the analogy query are not allowed to be returned." Allowing them, macro-accuracy on Mikolov's test set drops "from 0.71 to 0.21" for the standard method (3CosAdd) and "0.73 to 0.45" for 3CosMul; "In most cases, this is because the second term is returned as answer (man is to king as woman is to king, D == B)". Jurafsky and Martin p. 19: "the closest value returned by the parallelogram algorithm in word2vec or GloVe embedding spaces is usually not in fact b* but one of the 3 input words or their morphological variants ... so these must be explicitly excluded."
- Note: the plain statement "king - man + woman = queen" is false as written for the vectors in common use: the nearest vector to the sum is usually "king" itself, and "queen" is the nearest *other* word. The research preview on GloVe 50d (E22) shows exactly this. The 1990s-style "parallelogram model" of analogy predates all of this (`jurafsky2026embeddings` p. 17 credits Rumelhart and Abrahamson 1973; not fetched).

### E20. Analogies, part 2: what the test measures, and how far it reaches
- Sources: `linzen2016issues`, `levy2014linguistic`, `rogers2017too`.
- Quotes: Linzen, abstract (p. 13): "We show that the method's reliance on cosine similarity conflates offset consistency with largely irrelevant neighborhood structure". Linzen p. 15: accuracy varied across categories "ranging from .13 to .90"; "The most striking case is the plurals category, where the accuracy of ONLY-B reached .70" (ONLY-B ignores a and a* and returns the nearest neighbour of b). Linzen p. 16: "Accuracy decreased in most categories when the direction of the analogy was reversed (mean difference -0.11)". Levy and Goldberg (CoNLL), abstract (p. 171): the offset method "is equivalent to searching for a word that maximizes a linear combination of three pairwise word similarities" and "analogy recovery is not restricted to neural word embeddings, and that a similar amount of relational similarities can be recovered from traditional distributional word representations." p. 175: "in man:woman, king:?, we are likely to recover feminine entities, but not necessarily royal ones" and "king is more royal than it is masculine". Rogers et al., abstract (p. 135): "We argue against such "linguistic regularities" as a model for linguistic relations in vector space models and as a benchmark". p. 136: "even the best-performing GloVe model scores under 30% on the more challenging Bigger Analogy Test Set (BATS)" and the Google test set "only contains 15 types of linguistic relations, and these happen to be the easier ones".
- Note: the honest summary: the offset trick works well for some relations (capitals, plurals, verb forms) partly because the answer is already a near neighbour of the third word; it works poorly for others; and count-based vectors show it too. Do not present analogies as proof that the vectors "understand" relations.

### E21. Analogies, part 3: bias, and the dispute over measuring it with analogies
- Sources: `bolukbasi2016man`, `nissim2020fair`, `jurafsky2026embeddings`.
- Quotes: Bolukbasi et al., abstract (p. 1): "We show that even word embeddings trained on Google News articles exhibit female/male gender stereotypes to a disturbing extent." p. 3: the same system "will offensively answer "man is to computer programmer as woman is to x" with x=homemaker. The vectors (p. 3): "the popular publicly-available word2vec [24, 25] embedding trained on a corpus of Google News texts consisting of 3 million English words and terms into 300 dimensions". Abstract (p. 1): "gender bias is first shown to be captured by a direction in the word embedding." Nissim et al., abstract (p. 487): "Recent work has shown that analogies are in fact not an accurate diagnostic for bias" and "We stand by the truth that human biases are present in word embeddings, and, of course, the need to address them. Nissim et al. p. 492, Table 1: with the standard constrained method, man:doctor :: woman:X gives "gynecologist", he:doctor :: she:X gives "nurse", man:computer programmer :: woman:X gives "homemaker"; unconstrained, all three return the second term ("doctor", "doctor", "computer programmer"). p. 492: "using the original analogy code, it is impossible to obtain man is to doctor as woman is to doctor (where D == B). - Note: both claims are true at once and the chapter should say so: the vectors do encode stereotyped associations from their training text (Bolukbasi et al.'s gender direction, and J&M's other examples), and the famous analogy outputs overstate it because the method cannot return the input word. See D4. Date-stamp the "open problem" remark (as of 2026-08, J&M draft).

### E23. Similarity as closeness: cosine
- Sources: `deerwester1990indexing`, `mikolov2013linguistic`, `mikolov2013efficient`, `pennington2014glove`, `levy2014linguistic`, `jurafsky2026embeddings`.
- Quotes: Deerwester p. 396: documents "with the highest cosines, that is the nearest vectors, are returned", and "(We are concerned with the issue of whether the cosine measure is the best indication of similarity to predict human relevance judgments, but we have not yet systematically explored any alternatives". Mikolov et al. 2013c p. 748: search "for the word whose embedding vector has the greatest cosine similarity to y". Levy and Goldberg (CoNLL) p. 173: "Since cosine is inverse to the angle, high cosine similarity (close to 1) means that the vectors share a very similar direction. Note that this metric normalizes (and thus ignores) the vectors' lengths, unlike the Euclidean" distance. GloVe project page: "The Euclidean distance (or cosine similarity) between two word vectors provides an effective method for measuring the linguistic or semantic similarity of the corresponding words." J&M p. 22: "The cosine of two vectors (a normalized dot product) is the most popular of such metrics." (The source sets "a normalized dot product" off with em-dashes; written here with parentheses.)
- Note: cosine ignores length, which matters because raw count vectors of frequent words are long. The reader knows the dot product and may know cosine from document retrieval; define it with the standard formula [D-002]. Collobert et al. used Euclidean distance "chosen arbitrarily" (2011, p. 2514); the chapter can mention that both are used.

## Disputed or inconsistent points

### D1. What Firth meant by "the company it keeps"
- **The common reading:** the quote stands for the distributional hypothesis, paired with Harris (`jurafsky2026embeddings` pp. 1, 5, 22; `brunila2022company` p. 1 notes it is "cited in most major textbooks").
- **The close reading:** `brunila2022company`: Firth and Harris held "two distinct and in many ways divergent theories of meaning" (p. 1); for Firth, analysis "always started with the "context of situation" and necessarily accounted for non-verbal actors and objects" (p. 1, citing Firth 1957c, p. 9); the famous phrase "concerned a particular "mode of meaning": "meaning by collocation"" (p. 5). Firth himself says on the same page (p. 11) that "collocation is not to be interpreted as context, by which the whole conceptual meaning is implied" (E2).
- Suggested wording: "Firth's line is about collocation, the words a word habitually appears next to; he treated it as one layer of meaning among several. Modern writers often use it as a slogan for the whole distributional idea, which is closer to Harris." Quote Firth once, with the exclamation mark, from p. 11.

### D2. Who gets credit for neural word embeddings
Present the positions; do not pick a winner.
- **Bengio et al.:** `lecun2015deep` p. 441 and ref. 71 annotation: Bengio, Ducharme, Vincent (NIPS 2000, published 2001) "introduced neural language models, which learn to convert a word symbol into a word vector or word embedding composed of learned semantic features in order to predict the next word in a sequence." `goodfellow2016deep` p. 458: neural language models "overcome the curse of dimensionality problem ... by using a distributed representation of words (Bengio et al., 2001)". `jurafsky2026embeddings` p. 24: "Bengio et al. (2003) and Bengio et al. (2006) showed that neural language models could also be used to develop embeddings as part of the task of word prediction." `mikolov2013efficient` p. 2: the "very popular" NNLM "was proposed in [1]" (Bengio et al. 2003).
- **Earlier roots:** Bengio et al. 2003 themselves (p. 1140) cite Hinton (1986) and Elman (1990) for distributed representations of symbols, Miikkulainen and Dyer (1991) for neural networks in language modelling, Schmidhuber (1996) for neural next-character prediction, and Xu and Rudnicky (2000), who "independently proposed" a neural language model "with networks without hidden units and a single input word". `lecun2015deep` p. 441 says distributed representations of symbols were "first demonstrated" in ref. 27 (Rumelhart, Hinton, Williams 1986; E5).
- **Collobert and Weston's view:** the earlier language-model authors "were never able to obtain a good embedding of their words" (2008, PDF p. 6); "neither work leads to significant word embeddings being reported" (2011, p. 2512). `jurafsky2026embeddings` p. 24 credits them with showing "that embeddings could be used to represent word meanings for a number of NLP tasks".
- **Mikolov et al.:** present word2vec as a simplification of the NNLM line ("we directly extend this architecture", 2013a p. 2) whose contribution is cost and scale (E15); they credit [13, 14] (Mikolov's own earlier work) for learning the vectors in a first step.
- Facts that bear on it: Bengio's 2000/2003 papers learn and use word vectors and state the similarity argument (E8) but report no nearest-neighbour lists or analogy tests (the 2003 text has no table of word neighbours; "neighbor" occurs only for neighbouring sentences, pp. 1137-1140); Collobert and Weston (2008) show neighbour tables (E12); the king/queen offset is from Mikolov, Yih, Zweig 2013 with RNN vectors (E14).
- Suggested wording: "Bengio and colleagues introduced the neural language model with a learned vector for each word (2000, journal version 2003). Collobert and Weston (2008) made the vectors the goal and showed they were useful features. Mikolov and colleagues (2013) made them cheap enough to train on billions of words, and showed the offsets."

### D3. Is word2vec "the learned, predictive version" of LSA, or the same thing?
- **Different and better:** `baroni2014dont` p. 238 ("a thorough and resounding victory" for predict models); `mikolov2013linguistic` Table 2 (RNN 39.6% versus LSA 16.5% on its syntactic test, p. 749); `mikolov2013efficient` p. 2 (neural vectors "perform significantly better than LSA for preserving linear regularities").
- **Much the same underneath:** `levy2014neural` p. 1 (SGNS implicitly factorizes a shifted PMI word-context matrix); `levy2015improving` p. 211 (gains due mostly to "system design choices and hyperparameter optimizations"); `pennington2014glove` p. 1541 (both families "probe the underlying co-occurrence statistics"); GloVe Table 2 (SVD-L 60.1 versus SG 69.1 on 6B, p. 1537).
- Suggested wording for the bridge: "LSA factorizes a table of counts; word2vec learns vectors by predicting neighbours; later analysis showed that the second is, in effect, a weighted factorization of a similar table of word-neighbour counts. What changed in practice was scale, the choice of context (nearby words rather than whole documents), and many careful details." Keep the outline's bridge but add "turned out to be closer than it looked".

### D4. Do the analogy results show bias, or produce it?
See E21. Bolukbasi et al.: the vectors exhibit gender stereotypes "to a disturbing extent", shown with analogies and with a gender direction. Nissim et al.: analogies "are not an accurate tool" for this, because the method cannot return an input word; they also "stand by the truth that human biases are present in word embeddings". Present both; the reader should leave knowing that bias in vectors is real and that the most quoted demonstrations exaggerate it.

### D5. Small inconsistencies in primary sources
- `bengio2003neural`: "about 8% on AP News" (pp. 1148-1149), but Table 2's numbers give 117/109 = 1.073, about 7% (KN 5-gram, best n-gram on validation) or 119/109 = 1.09 (4-gram). The Brown figures reproduce (312/252 = 1.24, 336/252 = 1.33). Quote "about 8%" as the paper's figure, or give the two perplexities and let the script compute the ratio.
- `deerwester1990indexing`: text says mean precision rises "from .25 to .52" (p. 402), the Fig. 5 caption "from about .20 to .50". Use "more than doubles".
- `pennington2014glove` Table 1: printed ratios 8.9 and 0.96 differ from ratios of the printed probabilities (8.6 and 0.94); the others reproduce (8.5e-2, 1.36). If the chapter uses the table, compute ratios from the printed probabilities and say they are rounded.
- `firth1957synopsis`: the fetched scan is a 1962 printing; the 1957 date rests on later reference lists (E2).
- `levy2014linguistic`: the paper calls Mikolov's network "a recursive neural network (RNN)" (p. 171); it was a recurrent network (`mikolov2013linguistic` p. 748). Do not repeat.
- `mikolov2013linguistic` versus `mikolov2013efficient`: the 2013c formula does not mention excluding the question words; 2013a and 2013b do (E14, E19).

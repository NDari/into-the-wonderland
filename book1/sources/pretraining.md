## Sourced claims

### PT1. The 2000s assumption: train and test come from the same task and distribution
- Source: `pan2010survey`
- Location: p. 1 (abstract and introduction).
- Quotes: Abstract: "A major assumption in many machine learning and data mining algorithms is that the training and future data must be in the same feature space and have the same distribution. However, in many real-world applications, this assumption may not hold." "In such cases, knowledge transfer, if done successfully, would greatly improve the performance of learning by avoiding much expensive data labeling efforts." Introduction: "many machine learning methods work well only under a common assumption: the training and test data are drawn from the same feature space and the same distribution. When the distribution changes, most statistical models need to be rebuilt from scratch using newly collected training data."
- Note: this is the bridge for the reader's "you need labeled data for your task". A survey written in 2009-2010, in the reader's own era; it frames transfer learning for classification, regression, and clustering, not neural networks. Date-stamp: "as of 2010".

### PT2. Transfer learning, defined in the deep-learning textbook (as of 2016)
- Source: `goodfellow2016deep`
- Location: pp. 533, 534, 536.
- Quotes: p. 534: "Transfer learning and domain adaptation refer to the situation where what has been learned in one setting (e.g., distribution P1) is exploited to improve generalization in another setting (say, distribution P2)." p. 534: "The idea of pretraining has been generalized to supervised pretraining ... as a very common approach for transfer learning. Supervised pretraining for transfer learning is popular (Oquab et al., 2014; Yosinski et al., 2014) for use with convolutional networks pretrained on the ImageNet dataset. Practitioners publish the parameters of these trained networks for this purpose, just as pretrained word vectors are published for natural language tasks (Collobert et al., 2011a; Mikolov et al., 2013a)." p. 533 (the passage `deep-turn` already quotes): "Today, unsupervised pretraining has been largely abandoned, except in the field of natural language processing, where the natural representation of words as one-hot vectors conveys no similarity information and where very large unlabeled sets are available. In that case, the advantage of pretraining is that one can pretrain once on a huge unlabeled set (for example with a corpus containing billions of words), learn a good representation (typically of words, but also of sentences), and then use this representation or fine-tune it for a supervised task for which the training set contains substantially fewer examples. This approach was pioneered by Collobert and Weston (2008b), Turian et al. (2010), and Collobert et al. (2011a) and remains in common use today." p. 536: "Two extreme forms of transfer learning are one-shot learning and zero-shot learning ... Only one labeled example of the transfer task is given for one-shot learning, while no labeled examples are given at all for the zero-shot learning task."
- Note: `deep-turn` ends on the p. 533 sentence ("That exception is where this book is going"). This chapter should pick it up, not re-quote it at length. The p. 533 continuation ("pretrain once ... fine-tune it for a supervised task ... substantially fewer examples") is the chapter's thesis in a 2016 textbook's words. "Zero-shot" is defined here in its 2016 sense; `scale` will use it for prompting.

### PT3. Vision, 2013-2014: features from an ImageNet network reused with a linear classifier (DeCAF)
- Source: `donahue2014decaf`
- Location: PDF pp. 1, 2, 5, 7.
- Quotes: Abstract (p. 1): "We evaluate whether features extracted from the activation of a deep convolutional network trained in a fully supervised fashion on a large, fixed set of object recognition tasks can be repurposed to novel generic tasks. Our generic tasks may differ significantly from the originally trained tasks and there may be insufficient labeled or unlabeled data to conventionally train or adapt a deep architecture to the new tasks." p. 1: "With limited training data, however, fully-supervised deep architectures with the representational capacity of (Krizhevsky et al., 2012) will generally dramatically overfit the training data." p. 1: "Our model can either be considered as a deep architecture for transfer learning based on a supervised pre-training phase, or simply as a new visual feature DeCAF defined by the convolutional network weights learned on a set of pre-defined object recognition tasks." p. 5: "all of its weights are frozen to those learned on the Berg et al. (2012) dataset"; "Our top-performing method (based on validation accuracy) trains a linear SVM on DeCAF6 with dropout, with test set accuracy of 86.9%" (Caltech-101, 30 training images per class); "Our one-shot learning results (e.g., 33.0% for SVM) suggest that with sufficiently strong representations like DeCAF, useful models of visual categories can often be learned from just a single positive example." p. 7 (Discussion): "by leveraging an auxiliary large labeled object database to train a deep convolutional architecture, we can learn features that have sufficient representational power and generalization ability to perform semantic visual discrimination tasks using simple linear classifiers".
- Note: a strong bridge. The reader knows linear SVMs and logistic regression; DeCAF is "keep the network fixed, feed its 4096-number activations to an SVM". The pretraining here is supervised (ImageNet labels), unlike NLP's (PT8, PT50). The Berg et al. (2012) dataset is the ILSVRC 2012 data of `deep-turn`.

### PT5. How transferable are features? General early layers, specific late layers; frozen vs fine-tuned (2014)
- Source: `yosinski2014transferable`
- Location: pp. 1-2.
- Quotes: Abstract (p. 1): "Many deep neural networks trained on natural images exhibit a curious phenomenon in common: on the first layer they learn features similar to Gabor filters and color blobs. Such first-layer features appear not to be specific to a particular dataset or task, but general in that they are applicable to many datasets and tasks. Features must eventually transition from general to specific by the last layer of the network". "We also document that the transferability of features decreases as the distance between the base task and target task increases, but that transferring features even from distant tasks can be better than using random features. A final surprising result is that initializing a network with transferred features from almost any number of layers can produce a boost to generalization that lingers even after fine-tuning to the target dataset." p. 2: "In transfer learning, we first train a base network on a base dataset and task, and then we repurpose the learned features, or transfer them, to a second target network to be trained on a target dataset and task." "The usual transfer learning approach is to train a base network and then copy its first n layers to the first n layers of a target network. The remaining layers of the target network are then randomly initialized and trained toward the target task. One can choose to backpropagate the errors from the new task into the base (copied) features to fine-tune them to the new task, or the transferred feature layers can be left frozen, meaning that they do not change during training on the new task." "If the target dataset is small and the number of parameters is large, fine-tuning may result in overfitting, so the features are often left frozen. ... Of course, if the target dataset is very large, there would be little need to transfer because the lower level filters could just be learned from scratch on the target dataset."
- Note: the cleanest definitions of "transfer", "fine-tune", and "frozen" for the glossary. The overfitting trade-off is in the reader's vocabulary. ULMFiT builds its gradual unfreezing on this paper's general-to-specific finding (PT19).

### PT6. Vision, 2014: "supervised pre-training ... followed by domain-specific fine-tuning" (R-CNN)
- Source: `girshick2014rich`
- Location: PDF pp. 1, 2, 3.
- Quotes: Abstract (p. 1): "when labeled training data is scarce, supervised pre-training for an auxiliary task, followed by domain-specific fine-tuning, yields a significant performance boost." p. 2: "A second challenge faced in detection is that labeled data is scarce and the amount currently available is insufficient for training a large CNN. The conventional solution to this problem is to use unsupervised pre-training, followed by supervised fine-tuning (e.g., [29]). The second major contribution of this paper is to show that supervised pre-training on a large auxiliary dataset (ILSVRC), followed by domain-specific fine-tuning on a small dataset (PASCAL), is an effective paradigm for learning high-capacity CNNs when data is scarce. In our experiments, fine-tuning for detection improves mAP performance by 8 percentage points." p. 3: "We discriminatively pre-trained the CNN on a large auxiliary dataset (ILSVRC 2012) with image-level annotations (i.e., no bounding box labels)."
- Note: shows the words "pre-training" and "fine-tuning" changing meaning in front of the reader: in 2013-2014 "the conventional solution" was still the unsupervised pretraining of `deep-turn`; R-CNN replaced it with supervised pretraining on ImageNet. NLP then went back to unlabeled pretraining (PT8). One sentence in the chapter.

### PT7. NLP before 2018 transferred only the first layer
- Sources: `mccann2017learned`, `howard2018universal`, `radford2018improving`, `peters2018deep`
- Location: `mccann2017learned` p. 1; `howard2018universal` p. 328; `radford2018improving` p. 1; `peters2018deep` p. 2228.
- Quotes: McCann et al., abstract (p. 1): "Computer vision has benefited from initializing multiple deep layers with weights pretrained on large supervised training sets like ImageNet. Natural language processing (NLP) typically sees initialization of only the lowest layer of deep models with pretrained word vectors." Howard and Ruder (p. 328): "Inductive transfer learning has greatly impacted computer vision, but existing approaches in NLP still require task-specific modifications and training from scratch." "Applied CV models (including object detection, classification, and segmentation) are rarely trained from scratch, but instead are fine-tuned from models that have been pretrained on ImageNet, MS-COCO, and other datasets". "fine-tuning pretrained word embeddings (Mikolov et al., 2013), a simple transfer technique that only targets a model's first layer, has had a large impact in practice and is used in most state-of-the-art models." Radford et al. (p. 1): "The most compelling evidence for this so far has been the extensive use of pretrained word embeddings ... to improve performance on a range of NLP tasks". Peters et al. (p. 2228): pretrained word vectors "are a standard component of most state-of-the-art NLP architectures ... However, these approaches for learning word vectors only allow a single context-independent representation for each word."
- Note: connects to `embeddings` (static vectors, one per word). The word vectors of `embeddings` are "layer 1"; this chapter's story is moving the transfer up the stack.

### PT8. The labeled-data problem, in the authors' own words
- Sources: `radford2018improving`, `devlin2018open`, `radford2019language`, `raffel2020exploring`
- Location: `radford2018improving` p. 1; `devlin2018open` (first paragraph); `radford2019language` p. 1; `raffel2020exploring` p. 2.
- Quotes: Radford et al. 2018, abstract: "Although large unlabeled text corpora are abundant, labeled data for learning these specific tasks is scarce, making it challenging for discriminatively trained models to perform adequately." Introduction: "Most deep learning methods require substantial amounts of manually labeled data, which restricts their applicability in many domains that suffer from a dearth of annotated resources". Devlin and Chang (blog, 2 Nov 2018): "One of the biggest challenges in natural language processing (NLP) is the shortage of training data. Because NLP is a diversified field with many distinct tasks, most task-specific datasets contain only a few thousand or a few hundred thousand human-labeled training examples." "researchers have developed a variety of techniques for training general purpose language representation models using the enormous amount of unannotated text on the web (known as pre-training). The pre-trained model can then be fine-tuned on small-data NLP tasks like question answering and sentiment analysis, resulting in substantial accuracy improvements compared to training on these datasets from scratch." Radford et al. 2019 (p. 1): "The dominant approach to creating ML systems is to collect a dataset of training examples demonstrating correct behavior for a desired task, train a system to imitate these behaviors, and then test its performance on independent and identically distributed (IID) held-out examples." Raffel et al. (p. 2): "In applications of transfer learning to computer vision ..., pre-training is typically done via supervised learning on a large labeled data set like ImageNet ... In contrast, modern techniques for transfer learning in NLP often pre-train using unsupervised learning on unlabeled data." - Note: Raffel et al.'s vision-vs-NLP sentence is the exact contrast the chapter needs between PT3-PT6 and the rest. Common Crawl figure: "as of 2019".

### PT9. Dai and Le (2015): pretrain an LSTM as a language model or sequence autoencoder, then fine-tune
- Source: `dai2015semi`
- Location: pp. 1-3.
- Quotes: Abstract (p. 1): "We present two approaches to use unlabeled data to improve Sequence Learning with recurrent networks. The first approach is to predict what comes next in a sequence, which is a language model in NLP. The second approach is to use a sequence autoencoder, which reads the input sequence into a vector and predicts the input sequence again. These two algorithms can be used as a "pretraining" algorithm for a later supervised sequence learning algorithm. In other words, the parameters obtained from the pretraining step can then be used as a starting point for other supervised training models." p. 1: "We also find that a simple pretraining step can significantly stabilize the training of LSTMs." "We believe that this semi-supervised approach [1] is superior to other unsupervised sequence learning methods, e.g., Paragraph Vectors [19], because it can allow for easy fine-tuning." p. 2: "Our approach to sequence autoencoding is inspired by the work in sequence to sequence learning (also known as seq2seq) by Sutskever et al. [32]". "The objective is to reconstruct the input sequence itself. That means we replace the output sequence in the seq2seq framework with the input sequence." "We also find that recurrent language models [24] can be used as a pretraining method for LSTMs. This is equivalent to removing the encoder part of the sequence autoencoder in Figure 1." p. 3: "After training the recurrent language model or the sequence autoencoder for roughly 500K steps with a batch size of 128, we use both the word embedding parameters and the LSTM weights to initialize the LSTM for the supervised task. We then train on that task while fine tuning both the embedding parameters and the weights".
- Note: "pretraining" is in quotation marks in their abstract, a sign the word was borrowed from the 2006 sense (`deep-turn`). Ref. [1] is Ando and Zhang (2005), 2000s semi-supervised learning. Hand-off: seq2seq is known from `seq2seq`.

### PT12. ELMo (2018): what a contextual word vector is
- Source: `peters2018deep`
- Location: pp. 2227-2230.
- Quotes: Abstract (p. 2227): "We introduce a new type of deep contextualized word representation that models both (1) complex characteristics of word use (e.g., syntax and semantics), and (2) how these uses vary across linguistic contexts (i.e., to model polysemy). ### PT25. BERT (2018): the argument against left-to-right pretraining
- Source: `devlin2019bert`
- Location: pp. 4171-4172.
- Quotes: Abstract (p. 4171): "We introduce a new language representation model called BERT, which stands for Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers. ### PT27. BERT: the masked language model, 15% and 80/10/10
- Source: `devlin2019bert`
- Location: pp. 4174, 4182-4183, 4186.
- Quotes: p. 4174: "In order to train a deep bidirectional representation, we simply mask some percentage of the input tokens at random, and then predict those masked tokens. ### PT35. BERT's blog: the "bank" example, unidirectional vs bidirectional
- Source: `devlin2018open`
- Location: section "What Makes BERT Different?" and "The Strength of Bidirectionality".
- Quotes: "Pre-trained representations can either be context-free or contextual, and contextual representations can further be unidirectional or bidirectional. Context-free models such as word2vec or GloVe generate a single word embedding representation for each word in the vocabulary. For example, the word "bank" would have the same context-free representation in "bank account" and "bank of the river." Contextual models instead generate a representation of each word that is based on the other words in the sentence. For example, in the sentence "I accessed the bank account," a unidirectional contextual model would represent "bank" based on "I accessed the" but not "account." However, BERT represents "bank" using both its previous and next context - "I accessed the ... account" - starting from the very bottom of a deep neural network, making it deeply bidirectional." "unidirectional models are efficiently trained by predicting each word conditioned on the previous words in the sentence. However, it is not possible to train bidirectional models by simply conditioning each word on its previous and next words, since this would allow the word that's being predicted to indirectly "see itself" in a multi-layer model. To solve this problem, we use the straightforward technique of masking out some of the words in the input and then condition each word bidirectionally to predict the masked words." "While this idea has been around for a very long time, BERT is the first time it was successfully used to pre-train a deep neural network."
- Note: ties straight back to `embeddings` (Deerwester's "bank", GloVe's neighbours of "bank"). A good second figure or a worked sentence: what each kind of model can use when it builds a vector for "bank" (Figures planned, item 2). ### PT43. "Self-supervised learning": the definition, and the term's own history
- Sources: `lecun2021selfsupervised`, `bommasani2021opportunities`, `jurafsky2026transformers`

- Quotes: LeCun and Misra (4 March 2021): "Self-supervised learning obtains supervisory signals from the data itself, often leveraging the underlying structure in the data. "As a result of the supervisory signals that inform self-supervised learning, the term "self-supervised learning" is more accepted than the previously used term "unsupervised learning." ### PT45. Why the two families went separate ways, in the sources' words
- Sources: `devlin2019bert`, `raffel2020exploring`, `brown2020language`, `bommasani2021opportunities`, `jurafsky2026masked`
- Location: BERT pp. 4173-4174 (footnote 4); T5 p. 10; GPT-3 pp. 33, 40; Bommasani p. 83 (footnote 53); J&M ch. 9 p. 1.
- Quotes: BERT footnote 4: the left-context-only version "is referred to as a "Transformer decoder" since it can be used for text generation." T5 p. 10: encoder-only models are "applicable for classification or span prediction tasks but not for generative tasks like translation or abstractive summarization." GPT-3 (p. 33): "We focused on exploring in-context learning behavior in autoregressive language models because it is straightforward to both sample and compute likelihoods with this model class. Thus our design decision comes at the cost of potentially worse performance on tasks which empirically benefit from bidirectionality. Decoders are generative models, meaning that, given input tokens, they generate novel output tokens. The encoder takes as input a sequence of tokens and outputs a vector representation for each token. Encoders are usually masked language models ... ### PTD2. Did next-sentence prediction help?
- **Yes (BERT, 2018):** Table 5: removing NSP lowers QNLI 88.4 to 84.9, MNLI 84.4 to 83.9, SQuAD 88.5 to 87.9 (p. 4178); "very beneficial to both QA and NLI" (p. 4174).
- **No, or not reliably (RoBERTa, 2019):** without NSP, using full-length single-document inputs, results match or improve (Table 2, p. 5); possible explanation that BERT's ablation kept the paired-segment input format and only removed the loss (p. 5). RoBERTa also cites XLM, XLNet, and SpanBERT as questioning NSP.
- **Too easy (ALBERT, 2019):** NSP "conflates topic prediction and coherence prediction"; a random second sentence is usually off-topic, so the task can be solved by topic alone; sentence-order prediction is offered instead (pp. 4-5). A conjecture in the authors' words, backed by their SOP/NSP cross-test.
- **Later practice (J&M, 2026 draft, p. 8):** "Some models, like the RoBERTa model, drop the next sentence prediction objective".
- Suggested wording: "BERT's authors found that a second task, guessing whether one passage follows another, helped on some benchmarks. Within a year, other groups found that dropping it did no harm when the inputs were long passages from one document, and suggested the task had been too easy. Many later models of BERT's family dropped it."

### PTD3. Left-to-right or both directions?
- **Both directions are better for understanding tasks (BERT 2018, T5 2019):** BERT's argument (PT25) and ablation (LTR worse everywhere, much worse on SQuAD and MRPC, PT32); T5: denoising beat language-model objectives "always" in their comparison (p. 19); GPT-3's authors themselves "conjecture ... that a large bidirectional model would be stronger at fine-tuning than GPT-3" (p. 33).
- **Left-to-right is what can generate and score text (BERT footnote 4, T5 p. 10, GPT-3 pp. 33 and 40, J&M ch. 9 p. 1):** see PT45.
- **The prefix LM middle way:** T5's prefix LM with denoising scored 81.82 GLUE against 83.28 for encoder-decoder (Table 2), and the prefix-LM objective "attains similar performance on the translation tasks" (p. 21).
- **As of 2026-08:** decoders are "the architecture used to create large language models like GPT, Claude, Llama, and Mistral"; encoders are "not generative models" and are fine-tuned as classifiers (J&M ch. 9 pp. 1-2).
- Suggested wording: "In 2018 and 2019, for tasks such as classification and question answering after fine-tuning, models that read in both directions scored higher, and their authors' ablations showed it. But a model trained to fill in blanks cannot, by itself, write text one token after another; a model trained to predict the next token can, and it gives the probability of any text directly. Which mattered more depended on what the model was for. By the mid-2020s, the large language models people chat with were all of the left-to-right kind (as of 2026), while BERT-style encoders remained in use for classification and search." The last clause about search should be checked when `tools` is researched (embedding models); J&M p. 2 supports only "classifiers".

## Mechanism claims to check numerically at drafting [D-009]

Suggested script: `code/checks/pretraining.py` (standard library only), imported by `figures/pretraining.py` [D-014]. No external data is needed, so [D-018] does not apply. All items were run once in this session with `python3` (scratch script, not in the repo) and agree with the statements below.

1. **BERT's masking arithmetic on a 512-token input.** 15% of 512 = 76.8 positions chosen for prediction (in expectation); of these, 80% = 61.44 become [MASK], 10% = 7.68 become a random token, 10% = 7.68 stay unchanged. Per token: 12% [MASK], 1.5% random (matches BERT's "1.5% of all tokens", p. 4183), 1.5% unchanged, 85% not predicted. A next-token model on the same 512 tokens makes 512 predictions (one per position; the last token's target lies beyond the window, so 511 within one window if the window is exactly the text): the MLM gets a learning signal from 15% of positions, which is why BERT's authors expected slower convergence (p. 4183). Label the 76.8 as an expectation (BERT does not say whether it rounds).
2. **Tokens seen in pretraining.** BERT: 256 x 512 = 131,072 tokens per batch (paper: "128,000"); 1,000,000 steps all at 512 would be 131.1B token positions, 39.7 passes over 3.3B words (paper: "approximately 40 epochs"); with 90% of steps at 128 tokens: 0.9M x 256 x 128 + 0.1M x 256 x 512 = 29.5B + 13.1B = 42.6B, about 12.9 passes (tokens and words are not the same unit; WordPiece makes more tokens than words, so passes over the text are fewer still). T5's "roughly 137B" for BERT = 2^37 = 137.4B. T5 baseline: 2^19 x 2^16 = 2^35 = 34.36B ("≈ 34B"); one quarter of 2^37; 2.2T / 34.36B = 64 (RoBERTa). T5 final: 2^11 x 512 x 1,000,000 = 1.049 x 10^12 ("about 1 trillion"), 30.5 x the baseline ("about 32x"; exactly 32 if "1 trillion" is read as 2^40). Present at most one or two of these, with the caveats.
3. **Pretraining vs fine-tuning cost (BERT), in chip-hours.** One Cloud TPU = 4 chips (16 chips / 4 Cloud TPUs, p. 4183; derived). BERT-base pretraining 16 chips x 96 h = 1,536 chip-hours; BERT-large 64 x 96 = 6,144. Fine-tuning at most 1 h on one Cloud TPU = at most 4 chip-hours; SQuAD about 30 min = 2 chip-hours.

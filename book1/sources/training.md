### TR2. GPT-3's data: sources, sizes, and a mixture not proportional to size (2020)
- Source: `brown2020language`.
- Location: section 2.2, pp. 8-9; Table 2.2, p. 9.
- Quotes (p. 8): Common Crawl is "constituting nearly a trillion words. This size of dataset is sufficient to train our largest models without ever updating on the same sequence twice. However, we have found that unfiltered or lightly filtered versions of Common Crawl tend to have lower quality than more curated datasets. Therefore, we took 3 steps to improve the average quality of our datasets: (1) we downloaded and filtered a version of CommonCrawl based on similarity to a range of high-quality reference corpora, (2) we performed fuzzy deduplication at the document level, within and across datasets, to prevent redundancy and preserve the integrity of our held-out validation set as an accurate measure of overfitting, and (3) we also added known high-quality reference corpora to the training mix". The third step added "an expanded version of the WebText dataset", "two internet-based books corpora (Books1 and Books2) and English-language Wikipedia".
- Size (p. 8): "The CommonCrawl data was downloaded from 41 shards of monthly CommonCrawl covering 2016 to 2019, constituting 45TB of compressed plaintext before filtering and 570GB after filtering, roughly equivalent to 400 billion byte-pair-encoded tokens."
- Sampling (pp. 8-9): "datasets are not sampled in proportion to their size, but rather datasets we view as higher-quality are sampled more frequently, such that CommonCrawl and Books2 datasets are sampled less than once during training, but the other datasets are sampled 2-3 times. This essentially accepts a small amount of overfitting in exchange for higher quality training data."
- Table 2.2 (p. 9), quantity in tokens / weight in training mix / epochs when training for 300B tokens: Common Crawl (filtered) 410 billion / 60% / 0.44; WebText2 19 billion / 22% / 2.9; Books1 12 billion / 8% / 1.9; Books2 55 billion / 8% / 0.43; Wikipedia 3 billion / 3% / 3.4. Caption: "'Weight in training mix' refers to the fraction of examples during training that are drawn from a given dataset, which we intentionally do not make proportional to the size of the dataset." The printed epochs do not all follow from the weights (TRD3).
- Contamination (p. 9): "we searched for and attempted to remove any overlaps with the development and test sets of all benchmarks studied in this paper. Unfortunately, a bug in the filtering caused us to ignore some overlaps, and due to the cost of training it was not feasible to retrain the model." Hand off to `evaluation`.

### TR3. GPT-3's quality filter is a logistic regression; fuzzy deduplication with MinHash (2020)
- Source: `brown2020language`, Appendix A, p. 43.
- Quotes: "Using the original WebText as a proxy for high-quality documents, we trained a classifier to distinguish these from raw Common Crawl. We then used this classifier to re-sample Common Crawl by prioritizing documents which were predicted by the classifier to be higher quality. The classifier is trained using logistic regression classifier with features from Spark's standard tokenizer and HashingTF." Positives: "a collection of curated datasets such as WebText, Wikiedia [sic], and our web books corpus"; negatives: "unfiltered Common Crawl". A document is kept "iff np.random.pareto(α) > 1 - document_score" with "α = 9 in order to take mostly documents the classifier scored highly, but still include some documents that were out of distribution." Do not print the code-like expression in Book 1 [D-003]; describe it in words ("kept at random, with a probability that rises with the classifier's score").
- Deduplication: "we fuzzily deduplicated documents (i.e. removed documents with high overlap with other documents) within each dataset using Spark's MinHashLSH implementation with 10 hashes, using the same features as were used for classification above. We also fuzzily removed WebText from Common Crawl. Overall this decreased dataset size by an average of 10%."

### TR4. C4's cleaning rules (2019-2020)
- Source: `raffel2020exploring`, section 2.2, pp. 6-7.
- Quotes (p. 6), the heuristics, verbatim: "We only retained lines that ended in a terminal punctuation mark (i.e. a period, exclamation mark, question mark, or end quotation mark)." "We discarded any page with fewer than 5 sentences and only retained lines that contained at least 3 words." "We removed any page that contained any word on the 'List of Dirty, Naughty, Obscene or Otherwise Bad Words'." "we removed any line with the word Javascript." "we removed any page where the phrase 'lorem ipsum' appeared." "Since the curly bracket '{' appears in many programming languages (such as Javascript, widely used on the web) but not in natural text, we removed any pages that contained a curly bracket." "To deduplicate the data set, we discarded all but one of any three-sentence span occurring more than once in the data set." Plus: "we used langdetect to filter out any pages that were not classified as English with a probability of at least 0.99."
- Size (p. 7): "we downloaded the web extracted text from April 2019 and applied the aforementioned filtering. This produces a collection of text that is not only orders of magnitude larger than most data sets used for pre-training (about 750 GB) but also comprises reasonably clean and natural English text. We dub this data set the 'Colossal Clean Crawled Corpus' (or C4 for short)".
- Good worked example: each rule is a plain, inspectable test a reader could apply to a web page by hand.

- Source: `touvron2023llama`, section 2.1 and Table 1, PDF p. 2.

### TR7. Llama 3's data pipeline (2024)
- Source: `grattafiori2024llama`, section 3.1, pp. 4-6.
- Cutoff and scope (p. 4): "We create our dataset for language model pre-training from a variety of data sources containing knowledge until the end of 2023. We apply several de-duplication methods and data cleaning mechanisms on each data source to obtain high-quality tokens. We remove domains that contain large amounts of personally identifiable information (PII), and domains with known adult content."
- Extraction (p. 5): "we build a custom parser that extracts the HTML content and optimizes for precision in boilerplate removal and content recall." "We find markdown is harmful to the performance of a model that is primarily trained on web data compared to plain text, so we remove all markdown markers."
- Deduplication (p. 5): "URL-level de-duplication ... We keep the most recent version for pages corresponding to each URL." "Document-level de-duplication. We perform global MinHash (Broder, 1997) de-duplication across the entire dataset to remove near duplicate documents." "Line-level de-duplication ... We remove lines that appeared more than 6 times in each bucket of 30M documents. Although our manual qualitative analysis showed that the line-level de-duplication removes not only leftover boilerplate from various websites such as navigation menus, cookie warnings, but also frequent high-quality text, our empirical evaluations showed strong improvements."
- Heuristics (p. 5): "duplicated n-gram coverage ratio (Rae et al., 2021) to remove lines that consist of repeated content such as logging or error messages"; "'dirty word' counting (Raffel et al., 2020) to filter out adult websites"; "a token-distribution Kullback-Leibler divergence to filter out documents containing excessive numbers of outlier tokens".
- Model-based quality filtering (p. 5): "fast classifiers such as fasttext (Joulin et al., 2017) trained to recognize if a given text would be referenced by Wikipedia (Touvron et al., 2023a), as well as more compute-intensive Roberta-based classifiers (Liu et al., 2019a) trained on Llama 2 predictions. To train a quality classifier based on Llama 2, we create a training set of cleaned web documents, describe the quality requirements, and instruct Llama 2's chat model to determine if the documents meets these requirements."
- Multilingual (p. 5): "a fasttext-based language identification model to categorize documents into 176 languages."
- Mix (p. 6): "Our final data mix contains roughly 50% of tokens corresponding to general knowledge, 25% of mathematical and reasoning tokens, 17% code tokens, and 8% multilingual tokens." The mix was chosen with a "knowledge classification" classifier used "to downsample data categories that are over-represented on the web, for example, arts and entertainment", and with small-model scaling-law experiments (p. 6; scaling laws belong to `scale`).
- During training (p. 14): "we increased the percentage of non-English data", "upsample mathematical data", "added more recent web data in the later stages of pre-training to advance the model's knowledge cut-off, and we downsampled subsets of the pre-training data that were later identified as being lower quality."
- Annealing (p. 15): "During pre-training on the final 40M tokens, we linearly annealed the learning rate to 0, maintaining a context length of 128K tokens. During this annealing phase, we also adjusted the data mix to upsample data sources of very high quality". The final model is "the average of model checkpoints (Polyak (1991) averaging) during annealing" (p. 15).
- Size: "about 15T multilingual tokens, compared to 1.8T tokens for Llama 2" (p. 1); "405B parameters on 15.6T tokens using a context window of 8K tokens" (p. 3).

### TR8. FineWeb: a documented open pipeline, and a deduplication surprise (2024)
- Source: `penedo2024fineweb`.
- Abstract (p. 1): "FineWeb, a 15-trillion token dataset derived from 96 Common Crawl snapshots"; "FineWeb-Edu, a 1.3-trillion token collection of educational text filtered from FineWeb." Motivation (p. 1): "the pretraining datasets for state-of-the-art open LLMs like Llama 3 and Mixtral are not publicly available and very little is known about how they were created."
- Method (p. 3): choices tested by "data ablation" models, 1.71B parameters, sequence length 2048, "a global batch size of ~2 million tokens", "the GPT2 tokenizer"; "we trained over 70 models on our internal cluster, for an estimated total of 80,000 H100 GPU hours."
- Text extraction (p. 4): WET files "retained too much boilerplate and menu text", so text was extracted from raw WARC files with trafilatura.
- Base filtering (p. 4): a URL blocklist for adult content, "a fastText language classifier ... to keep only English text with a score >= 0.65", and MassiveText quality and repetition filters; this left "roughly 36 trillion tokens of data when tokenized with the GPT-2 tokenizer."
- MinHash (p. 5): "each document's 5-grams ... 112 hash functions in total, split into 14 buckets of 8 hashes each - targeting documents that are at least 75% similar" (em-dash in source).
- The surprise (p. 5): deduplicating all 96 snapshots against each other "removed as much as 90% of the original base filtered data" from the oldest snapshots and left 4 trillion tokens, but "our ablation models showed little improvement over a model trained on the non-deduplicated data". For the 2013-48 snapshot, "the data from it that was kept (10% of the original data) was actually of worse quality than the 90% of data that was removed." Deduplicating each snapshot separately gave 20 trillion tokens and matched RefinedWeb. Hypothesis: "the main improvement gained from deduplication lies in the removal of large clusters of duplicates with hundreds of thousands of documents" (pp. 5-6).
- C4 rules re-tested (p. 6): "the Terminal punctuation filter, by itself, gives the biggest individual boost, but removes around 30% of all tokens"; FineWeb kept the other C4 filters and dropped that one.
- Final pipeline (p. 7): WARC extraction, base filtering, per-snapshot MinHash, selected C4 filters, custom filters; "For the public release of the dataset, we have also applied Personal Identifiable Information (PII) removal, by anonymizing email and public IP addresses."
- FineWeb-Edu (p. 8): Llama-3-70B-Instruct scored "460,000 randomly sampled webpages ... for their educational quality on a scale from 0 to 5"; a linear regression on top of an embedding model was trained on 410,000 of these and applied to all of FineWeb; "Applying the classifier to the 15 trillion tokens of FineWeb required 6,000 H100 GPU hours." The same idea "was notably used in the non-public pretraining datasets of Llama 3 [6] and Phi-3 [8]" (p. 8).
- License (Hugging Face dataset card metadata, read 2026-11-11): ODC-By.

### TR10. Deduplication (Lee et al., 2022)
- Source: `lee2022deduplicating`.
- Abstract (p. 8424): "existing language modeling datasets contain many near-duplicate examples and long repetitive substrings. As a result, over 1% of the unprompted output of language models trained on these datasets is copied verbatim from the training data." "Deduplication allows us to train models that emit memorized text ten times less frequently and require fewer training steps to achieve the same or better accuracy. ### TR13. The training example and the loss: every position is a prediction, and the loss is the average
- Sources: `radford2018improving`; `bigscience2022megatronds`.
- GPT-1, eq. (1), p. 3: "Given an unsupervised corpus of tokens U = {u1, ..., un}, we use a standard language modeling objective to maximize the following likelihood: L1(U) = sum over i of log P(ui | ui-k, ..., ui-1; Θ) where k is the size of the context window, and the conditional probability P is modeled using a neural network with parameters Θ. These parameters are trained using stochastic gradient descent".
- BLOOM's training code: `pretrain_gpt.py`, `loss_func`: the per-token losses are summed over the positions that count and divided by their number (`torch.sum(losses.view(-1) * loss_mask) / loss_mask.sum()`), so the logged "lm loss" is a mean over token positions. `megatron/mpu/cross_entropy.py`: "Loss = log(sum(exp(logits))) - predicted-logit", using `torch.log` and `torch.exp`, i.e. natural logarithms. The logged loss is therefore cross-entropy in nats per token. Do not print the code [D-003]; state the fact.
- Hand-off: `prediction` defines cross-entropy in bits; this chapter must say that training logs usually use natural logarithms and that dividing by ln 2 (0.693) converts to bits (mechanism item 9). Causal masking (why one forward pass predicts every position at once) belongs to `anatomy`; this chapter can say "the model predicts every position of the window in one pass" and point there.

### TR18. Mixed precision (Micikevicius et al., 2018)
- Source: `micikevicius2018mixed`.
- Abstract (p. 1): "We introduce methodology for training deep neural networks using half-precision floating point numbers, without losing model accuracy or having to modify hyperparameters. This nearly halves memory requirements and, on recent GPUs, speeds up arithmetic." Three techniques: "maintaining a single-precision copy of weights that accumulates the gradients after each optimizer step (this copy is rounded to half-precision for the forward- and back-propagation)", "loss-scaling to preserve gradient values with small magnitudes", and "half-precision arithmetic that accumulates into single-precision outputs".
- Why (p. 1): "half-precision math throughput in recent GPUs is 2x to 8x higher than for single-precision" (as of 2017-2018).
- Why a master copy (p. 3): "any value whose magnitude is smaller than 2^-24 becomes zero in FP16"; and the update can vanish when added to a much larger weight: "This can happen when the magnitude of a normalized weight value is at least 2048 times larger that of the weight update. Since FP16 has 10 bits of mantissa".
- Loss scaling (pp. 4-5): choose a factor "so that its product with the maximum absolute gradient value is below 65,504 (the maximum value representable in FP16)"; overflows "will result in infinities and NaNs in the weight gradients which will irreversibly damage the weights after an update."

### TR19. bfloat16
- Sources: `wang2019bfloat16`; `kalamkar2019study`.
- Google (2019-08-23): "This custom floating point format is called 'Brain Floating Point Format,' or 'bfloat16' for short. The name flows from 'Google Brain', which is an artificial intelligence research group at Google where the idea for this format was conceived." "bfloat16 has a greater dynamic range-i.e., number of exponent bits-than FP16. In fact, the dynamic range of bfloat16 is identical to that of FP32." (em-dashes in source). "in our experience, the bfloat16 format works as well as the FP32 format while delivering increased performance and reducing memory usage."
- Kalamkar et al. abstract (p. 1): bfloat16 "is attractive for Deep Learning training for two reasons: the range of values it can represent is the same as that of IEEE 754 floating-point format (FP32) and conversion to/from FP32 is simple." "IEEE 754 compliant half-precision floating point (FP16) requires hyper-parameter tuning." Origin (p. 1): "BFLOAT16 data format was first introduced as part of distributed training frameworks DistBelief [10] and Tensorflow [4] as a low precision storage format used to reduce communication volumes of weights and activations".
- Table 1 (p. 4), bit format (sign, exponent, mantissa) and max normal: FP32 (1, 8, 23), 3.40e38; FP16 (1, 5, 10), 6.55e4; BFLOAT16 (1, 8, 7), 3.38e38. Min normal: FP32 and BFLOAT16 1.17e-38; FP16 6.10e-5. (TRD8 on "8 bits of mantissa".)
- In practice: Llama 3 reports "BF16 MFU" for its 405B run (Table 4, p. 10) and uses "FP32 gradient accumulation during backward computation over multiple micro-batches" and reduces gradients "in FP32" (p. 12). OLMo (PDF p. 4): mixed precision with "bfloat16", with "certain operations like the softmax always run in full precision", and weights and optimizer state "kept in full precision". Date-stamp: practice as of 2022-2024.

### TR22. Splitting the work across GPUs: data, tensor, and pipeline parallelism
- Sources: `shoeybi2019megatron`; `lescao2022bloom`; `grattafiori2024llama`; `brown2020language`.
- Megatron-LM (PDF p. 3): "There are two central paradigms for scaling out deep neural network training to numerous hardware accelerators: data parallelism (Valiant, 1990) where a training minibatch is split across multiple workers, and model parallelism in which the memory usage and computation of a model is distributed across multiple workers." Data parallelism's limit: "the model must fit entirely on one worker." "Within model parallelism, there are two further paradigms: layer-wise pipeline parallelism, and more general distributed tensor computation." Result (PDF p. 1): "converging transformer based models up to 8.3 billion parameters using 512 GPUs. We sustain 15.1 PetaFLOPs across the entire application with 76% scaling efficiency when compared to a strong single GPU baseline that sustains 39 TeraFLOPs, which is 30% of peak FLOPs."
- BLOOM (p. 19), plain definitions: "Data parallelism (DP) replicates the model multiple times, with each replica placed on a different device and fed a slice of the data. The processing is done in parallel and all model replicas are synchronized at the end of each training step." "Tensor parallelism (TP) partitions individual layers of the model across multiple devices." "Pipeline parallelism (PP) splits up the model's layers across multiple GPUs, so that only a fraction of the layers of the model are placed on each GPU." Together "3D parallelism".
- Llama 3 (p. 10): "4D parallelism-a combination of four different types of parallelism methods-to shard the model" (em-dashes in source), adding context parallelism, which "divides the input context into segments, reducing memory bottleneck for very long sequence length inputs." FSDP "shards the model, optimizer, and gradients while implementing data parallelism which processes data in parallel on multiple GPUs and synchronizes after each training step." Table 4 (p. 10): 8,192 GPUs (TP 8, CP 1, PP 16, DP 64, sequence 8,192), 16,384 GPUs (DP 128), and 16,384 GPUs for long context (CP 16, DP 8, sequence 131,072); 16M tokens per batch in each; 430, 400, 380 TFLOPs per GPU; BF16 MFU 43%, 41%, 38%. Placement (p. 11): "The innermost parallelism requires the highest network bandwidth and lowest latency, and hence is usually constrained to within the same server."
- GPT-3 (p. 9): "we use a mixture of model parallelism within each matrix multiply and model parallelism across the layers of the network. All models were trained on V100 GPU's on part of a high-bandwidth cluster provided by Microsoft."

### TR28. How memorization scales (Carlini et al., 2023)
- Source: `carlini2023quantifying`.
- Definition (p. 3): "A string s is extractable with k tokens of context from a model f if there exists a (length-k) string p, such that the concatenation [p || s] is contained in the training data for f, and f produces s when prompted with p using greedy decoding." - Abstract (p. 1): "Memorization significantly grows as we increase (1) the capacity of a model, (2) the number of times an example has been duplicated, and (3) the number of tokens of context used to prompt the model."

## Disputed or inconsistent points

### TRD1. Llama 3 Table 5: the first row does not add up
Table 5 (p. 13, page image checked) prints Faulty GPU as 148 interruptions and 30.1%. 148 of 419 is 35.3%; 30.1% of 419 is 126. The other rows match 419 (72 is 17.2%, 54 is 12.9%, 35 is 8.4%, 32 is 7.6%). The counts sum to 419 while the percentages sum to 94.9%; the GPU rows' percentages sum to 58.7%, matching the text, while their counts give 268 of 419, 64.0%. So either 148 or 30.1% is a misprint, and the paper does not say which. Suggested wording: "faulty GPUs were the largest single cause, and GPU problems of all kinds accounted for 58.7% of the unexpected interruptions". Do not print "148 (30.1%)" as if consistent.

### TRD2. Llama 3's batch-size sentence and step count
p. 14 says "double these values to a batch size of 8M sequences of 8,192 tokens": in context this must mean 8M tokens in sequences of 8,192 tokens (Table 4 gives tokens per batch). Quote it only with that reading, or paraphrase. Separately, 15.6T tokens at 16M tokens per step would be 975,000 steps, but the schedule is stated "over 1,200,000 steps" (p. 14), with smaller batches early. The paper gives no step count for the run; do not compute one.

### TRD3. GPT-3 Table 2.2's epochs do not all follow from the weights
Weight x 300B / size gives Common Crawl 0.44 (printed 0.44), Books2 0.44 (0.43), but WebText2 3.47 (printed 2.9), Books1 2.0 (1.9), Wikipedia 3.0 (3.4). The weights sum to 101% (rounding). The paper does not explain the difference. Quote the table as printed and do not derive one column from another.

### TRD4. Carlini et al. 2023's "0.00000015%"
p. 1 says 600 examples out of GPT-2's 40GB set a lower bound "that at least 0.00000015% of the dataset is memorized". 0.00000015% of 40 GB is 60 bytes, far less than 600 examples; the figure is unclear as printed. Do not quote it. The contrast it was making (a few hundred examples then, "at least 1%" for GPT-J) can be stated with the other numbers.

### TRD5. Is deduplication always good?
- Lee et al. (p. 8425): "no observed disadvantages."
- FineWeb (p. 5): global deduplication across all snapshots gave "little improvement", and for an old snapshot the kept 10% was worse than the removed 90%; per-snapshot deduplication worked better.
- Llama 3 (p. 5): line-level deduplication "removes not only leftover boilerplate ... but also frequent high-quality text", yet "our empirical evaluations showed strong improvements."
- Carlini et al. 2023 (p. 5): deduplication reduces but "will not perfectly prevent" memorization.
- Present all four. These are not contradictory measurements of the same thing (different corpora, models, and kinds of deduplication), but they rule out "more deduplication is always better".

### TRD6. Dodge et al.: 365 million documents or domains?
Table 1 (p. 1287) gives 365 million documents for C4.EN; the text (p. 1286) says "more than 365 million domains". Use the table: 365 million documents (pages).

### TRD7. Common Crawl's own dates
The home page says "founded in 2007", "Free and open corpus since 2007", and "Over 300 billion pages spanning 15 years"; the overview says "regularly collected since 2008". 2007 or 2008 to 2026 is 18 or 19 years. Use "founded in 2007" and avoid the "15 years".

### TRD8. bfloat16's mantissa: 7 or 8 bits?
Kalamkar et al. (p. 3) say "8 bits of mantissa"; their Table 1 (p. 4) lists (1, 8, 7). Both are right if the 8 includes the implicit leading bit. Say "7 stored fraction bits (8 bits of precision counting the hidden bit)", or avoid the bit count and say "the same range as 32-bit numbers, with much less precision".

### TRD9. Packing: mask or no mask (practice differs; not a dispute)
GPT-3 (p. 43) did not mask between packed documents and relied on the end-of-text token; Llama 3 (p. 6) masks attention between documents and found it mattered mainly for long sequences. Present as two choices.

### TRD10. BLOOM's "only one loss spike"
The paper (p. 21) and chronicles agree on one loss spike (iteration 31,219, loss 5.10). The log also shows smaller jumps: at iteration 6,374 (3.19 against a median of 2.87 over the previous 200) and at iterations 40,489-40,490 (2.94 and 2.85 against 2.14). The chronicles also mention "a few grad norm spikes". Say "one large spike" and, if the figure shows the small blips, label them neutrally. The chronicles say recovery took "~30 iterations"; in the log the loss is back to 2.31 by iteration 31,230 and 2.22 by 31,250. Both descriptions fit.

### TRD11. Small defects in primary sources
- `carlini2021extracting` p. 2640 contains the stray sentence "Somwhere say 'We extracted 16 individual's contact information, an additional 46 named (actual) people.'" (an author's note left in the published text). Do not quote it or its numbers.
- `brown2020language` p. 43 spells "Wikiedia". Quote with [sic] or paraphrase.

### TRD12. Llama 3: one GPU count or two?
"trained on up to 16K H100 GPUs" (p. 9); Table 4 (p. 10) shows the first stage on 8,192 GPUs. Say "up to 16,384".

### TRD13. Two kinds of "how much is memorized"
Carlini et al. 2023: at least 1% of the training set is extractable from GPT-J (fraction of data). Llama 3: 1.13% to 3.91% inclusion rate for 405B on sampled prompts (fraction of tests). Lee et al.: about 1.9% of tokens of unprompted output come from training data (fraction of output). Three different denominators; never set them side by side as one statistic.

## Mechanism claims to check numerically at drafting [D-009]

Suggested script: `code/checks/training.py` (standard library plus numpy for the precision items), imported by `figures/training.py` [D-014]. Items 1-7 and 9-12 were run once in this session with `python3` and agree with the statements below. Item 8 was run on the downloaded log.

1. **Tokens per step.** GPT-3 175B: 3.2M tokens per batch / 2,048 per sequence = 1,562.5 sequences (the paper's "3.2M" is rounded; say "about 1,500 sequences of 2,048 tokens"). Pythia: 1,024 x 2,048 = 2,097,152. BLOOM at full batch: 2,048 x 2,048 = 4,194,304. Llama 3 Table 4: 32 x 64 x 8,192 = 16 x 128 x 8,192 = 16 x 8 x 131,072 = 16,777,216 = 16 Mi tokens.
2. **Steps for a token budget.** GPT-3 175B: 300B / 3.2M = 93,750 steps if the batch were full from the start (it was not; ramp over 4-12B tokens), so "roughly 94,000". LLaMA 65B: 1.4T / 4M = 350,000. BLOOM: the log has 95,281 iterations for 366.5B tokens; at the full batch it would be 366.5B / 4,194,304 = 87,390, the difference being the ramp. Do not compute Llama 3's steps (TRD2).
3. **Wall-clock check.** LLaMA 65B: 1.4T tokens / (380 tokens/s/GPU x 2,048 GPUs) = 1.80M s = 20.8 days, matching the paper's "approximately 21 days".
4. **GPT-3 mixture.** Weights sum to 101%. Epochs: weight x 300 / size: CC 0.44, WebText2 3.47, Books1 2.00, Books2 0.44, Wikipedia 3.00 (TRD3). 570 GB out of 45 TB is 1.3%, but the paper calls the 45 TB "compressed plaintext" and does not say whether the 570 GB is compressed, so do not print this as "GPT-3 kept 1.3% of the crawl"; say "45 TB of compressed text went in, 570 GB came out". Likewise 570 GB / 400B tokens = 1.4 bytes per token depends on that unknown; do not print it.
5. **Training memory per parameter (ZeRO's accounting).** 2 + 2 + 4 + 4 + 4 = 16 bytes. 1.5B: 24 GB (matches p. 8). 70B: 1,120 GB, i.e. at least 14 GPUs of 80 GB for model states alone, before activations. 175B: 2,800 GB. 405B: 6,480 GB, at least 81 GPUs of 80 GB. Contrast storage alone: 70B in bf16 = 140 GB, in fp32 = 280 GB (the `destination` table already gives the storage numbers; reuse, do not repeat).
6. **Llama 3 interruptions.** 466 - 47 = 419; 466 / 54 = 8.6 per day; 419 / 54 = 7.8 unexpected per day. Table 5 arithmetic for TRD1: 148/419 = 35.3%; 0.301 x 419 = 126; sum of percentages 94.9%; GPU rows 58.7% by percentage, 64.0% by count.
7. **Weights versus data (reconstruction; label it so).** Llama 3 405B at 2 bytes per parameter: 810 GB. 15.6T tokens x 3.94 characters per token (Llama 3's English figure, p. 7; characters are not bytes outside ASCII, so this is rough) = 61.5 trillion characters, about 61 TB of text: about 76 times the size of the weights. Per training token: 405B x 16 bits / 15.6T = 0.42 bits of weight per token seen. 15.6T / 405B = 38.5 tokens per parameter. GPT-3: 175B x 2 bytes = 350 GB against 300B tokens: 1.7 tokens per parameter. The point: the weights are far too small to hold the corpus verbatim, yet measurable fractions are recoverable (TR27-TR29).
8. **The BLOOM loss curve (from the log).** File checks: 369,323,221 bytes; SHA-256 908ce0dd3cd0c42efa525ebfd94d314861733567f6441c62b98e82bf1d644cf5 (equals Hugging Face's LFS object id). 95,281 iterations, no gaps, 39 backward jumps (restarts), 1,767 repeated iterations. Iteration 1: loss 61.48 at learning rate 6.29e-8, batch 192 sequences (393,216 tokens). The loss falls below ln(250,680) = 12.43 (the loss of uniform guessing over BLOOM's vocabulary) at iteration 16 (12.36). Warmup peak: 6.0e-5 at iteration 910, 375,422,976 tokens (loss 4.60). Batch ramp: 192 sequences at iteration 1, steps of 16 sequences, 2,048 sequences from iteration 12,659 (20.0B tokens; loss 2.45). Loss at 1B tokens 3.56; 10B 2.65; 50B 2.30; 100B 2.21; 200B 2.07; 300B 1.99; 341B (end of the first pass over ROOTS) 1.94; final iteration 95,281 at 366.5B tokens: 1.916 nats. Spike: iteration 31,219 (97.85B tokens), loss 5.098 against 2.20 just before, grad norm 960.35 against about 0.2; loss 2.31 at iteration 31,230 and 2.22 at 31,250. Learning rate at the end 7.46e-6, above the 6e-6 minimum, as the paper says. Medians over 500 iterations: 7.23 (first 500), 4.12 (iterations 1,001-1,500), 2.98 (5,001-5,500), 2.45 (just after the ramp), 2.14 (40,001-40,500), 1.93 (94,001-94,500).
9. **Nats to bits and perplexity.** 1.916 nats / ln 2 = 2.76 bits per token; e^1.916 = 6.80 (perplexity per BLOOM token). Bridge to `prediction` (bits, perplexity) but warn that per-token figures depend on the tokenizer.
10. **Floating point (numpy, float16 and a bfloat16 emulation by rounding the float32 bit pattern).** fp16 max 65,504; smallest normal 6.10e-5; 2^-24 (5.96e-8) is the smallest subnormal and 2^-25 rounds to 0 (Micikevicius p. 3). 1 + 1/1024 is representable in fp16 (1.001), 1 + 1/4096 rounds to exactly 1: a weight of 1 receiving an update of 1/4096 does not move (the "2048 times larger" effect). bfloat16: 1 + 1/256 rounds to 1, 1 + 1/128 = 1.0078125; 3.0e38 and 1e-30 are representable (the fp32 range). Spacing near 1: fp32 1.19e-7, fp16 9.77e-4, bf16 7.81e-3.
11. **Lee et al. ratios.** 1.926 / 0.189 = 10.2; 1.926 / 0.138 = 14.0 (the paper's "~10x").
12. **BLOOM warmup in steps.** 375M tokens / (192 x 2,048) = 954 steps at the starting batch; the log reaches the peak at iteration 910 because the batch grew to 208 and 224 during warmup.

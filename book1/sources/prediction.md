## Sourced claims

### P1. What a language model is, and the probability of a sequence by the chain rule
- Sources: `jurafsky2026speech`, `chen1998empirical`, `brown1990statistical`.
- Quotes: Jurafsky and Martin, p. 1: "A language model is a machine learning model that predicts upcoming words. More formally, a language model assigns a probability to each possible next word, or equivalently gives a probability distribution over possible next words. Language models can also assign a probability to an entire sentence." Also p. 1: "large language models are built just by training them to predict words!!" p. 2: "An n-gram is a sequence of n words: a 2-gram (which we'll call bigram) is a two-word sequence of words like The water, or water of, and a 3-gram (a trigram) is a three-word sequence". p. 3, eq. 3.4: P(w_1:n) = P(w_1) P(w_2|w_1) P(w_3|w_1:2) ... P(w_n|w_1:n-1), and "The chain rule shows the link between computing the joint probability of a sequence and computing the conditional probability of a word given previous words." p. 3: "we can't just estimate by counting the number of times every word occurs following every long string in some corpus, because language is creative and any particular context might have never occurred before!" Chen and Goodman, p. 3: "A language model is usually formulated as a probability distribution p(s) over strings s that attempts to reflect how frequently a string s occurs as a sentence." Also p. 3: "Notice that unlike in linguistics, grammaticality is irrelevant in language modeling; even though the string asbestos gallops gallantly is grammatical, we still assign it a near-zero probability." Brown et al. 1990, p. 80: "we can recast the language modeling problem as one of computing the probability of a single word given all of the words that precede it in a sentence."
- The Markov (n-gram) approximation: Jurafsky and Martin, p. 3: "The intuition of the n-gram model is that instead of computing the probability of a word given its entire history, we can approximate the history by just the last few words." and "The assumption that the probability of a word depends only on the previous word is called a Markov assumption." Brown et al. 1990, p. 80: "Because there are so many histories, we cannot simply treat each of these probabilities as a separate parameter. One way to reduce the number of parameters is to place each of the histories into an equivalence class ... In an n-gram model, two histories are equivalent if they agree in their final n - 1 words."
- Counting estimates (maximum likelihood): Jurafsky and Martin, p. 4, eq. 3.11: P(w_n|w_n-1) = C(w_n-1 w_n) / C(w_n-1), with the worked corpus "<s> I am Sam </s> / <s> Sam I am </s> / <s> I do not like green eggs and ham </s>" and "P(I|<s>) = 2/3 = 0.67 ... P(am|I) = 2/3 = 0.67 ... P(do|I) = 1/3 = 0.33". p. 4, footnote 1: the end symbol is needed "to make the bigram grammar a true probability distribution".
- Note: `destination` already wrote the chain rule with invented probabilities and cited `radford2019language` p. 2, which credits Jelinek and Mercer (1980) and Bengio et al. (2003). This chapter should refer back to that table rather than re-derive it. The reader knows this counting from naive Bayes; Jurafsky and Martin p. 14 note that add-one smoothing "is also a practical smoothing algorithm for other tasks like text classification", which is the honest bridge.

### P2. Shannon (1948): a source of text is a stochastic process
- Source: `shannon1948mathematical`
- Location: pp. 379, 385.
- Quotes: p. 379: "The fundamental problem of communication is that of reproducing at one point either exactly or approximately a message selected at another point. Frequently the messages have meaning ... These semantic aspects of communication are irrelevant to the engineering problem. The significant aspect is that the actual message is one selected from a set of possible messages." p. 385: "In telegraphy, for example, the messages to be transmitted consist of sequences of letters. These sequences, however, are not completely random. In general, they form sentences and have the statistical structure of, say, English. The letter E occurs more frequently than Q, the sequence TH more frequently than XP, etc. The existence of this structure allows one to make a saving in time (or channel capacity) by properly encoding the message sequences into signal sequences. This is already done to a limited extent in telegraphy by using the shortest channel symbol, a dot, for the most common English letter E". p. 385: "We can think of a discrete source as generating the message, symbol by symbol. It will choose successive symbols according to certain probabilities depending, in general, on preceding choices as well as the particular symbols in question. A physical system, or a mathematical model of a system which produces such a sequence of symbols governed by a set of probabilities is known as a stochastic process. We may consider a discrete source, therefore, to be represented by a stochastic process." The list that follows begins "1. Natural written languages such as English, German, Chinese."
- Note: "choose successive symbols according to certain probabilities depending ... on preceding choices" is next-symbol prediction stated in 1948. The "semantic aspects ... are irrelevant" sentence is often quoted; it is about engineering, not a claim that meaning does not matter, and the chapter should not overstate it.

### P4. Shannon (1948): entropy and the bit
- Source: `shannon1948mathematical`
- Location: pp. 380, 393-394.
- Quotes: p. 380: "If the base 2 is used the resulting units may be called binary digits, or more briefly bits, a word suggested by J. W. Tukey. A device with two stable positions, such as a relay or a flip-flop circuit, can store one bit of information." p. 393: "Quantities of the form H = -sum p_i log p_i (the constant K merely amounts to a choice of a unit of measure) play a central role in information theory as measures of information, choice and uncertainty. The form of H will be recognized as that of entropy as defined in certain formulations of statistical mechanics". p. 394: "H = 0 if and only if all the p_i but one are zero, this one having the value unity. Thus only when we are certain of the outcome does H vanish. Otherwise H is positive." and "For a given n, H is a maximum and equal to log n when all the p_i are equal (i.e., 1/n). This is also intuitively the most uncertain situation." p. 394, Fig. 7: entropy of two possibilities with probabilities p and 1 - p (the coin curve, 1 bit at p = 1/2).
- Also: `goodfellow2016deep` p. 72: the Shannon entropy "gives a lower bound on the number of bits (if the logarithm is base 2, otherwise the units are different) needed on average to encode symbols drawn from a distribution P. Distributions that are nearly deterministic (where the outcome is nearly certain) have low entropy; distributions that are closer to uniform have high entropy." `jurafsky2026speech` p. 18, eq. 3.32, and pp. 18-19: the eight-horse example (uniform: 3 bits; skewed 1/2, 1/4, 1/8, 1/16, 1/64 x 4: 2 bits) from Cover and Thomas.
- Note: the reader needs only: entropy = average surprise, in bits; maximal when all outcomes equal; zero when certain. Example 4 below makes it concrete with dice.

### P5. Shannon (1948): the redundancy of English is about 50%
- Source: `shannon1948mathematical`
- Location: pp. 398-399.
- Quotes: p. 398: "F_N is the entropy of the N-th order approximation to the source of the type discussed above." pp. 398-399: "The redundancy of ordinary English, not considering statistical structure over greater distances than about eight letters is roughly 50%. This means that when we write English half of what we write is determined by the structure of the language and half is chosen freely. The figure 50% was found by several independent methods which all gave results in this neighborhood. One is by calculation of the entropy of the approximations to English. A second method is to delete a certain fraction of the letters from a sample of English text and then let someone attempt to restore them." p. 399: "The redundancy of a language is related to the existence of crossword puzzles. If the redundancy is zero any sequence of letters is a reasonable text in the language and any two dimensional array of letters forms a crossword puzzle."
- Note: the letter-deletion method is already a human prediction experiment, three years before the 1951 paper.

### P6. Shannon (1951): from about 2.3 bits per letter to about 1 bit, by prediction
- Source: `shannon1951prediction`
- Location: p. 50 (abstract and introduction; read on the page image).
- Quotes: Abstract: "A new method of estimating the entropy and redundancy of a language is described. This method exploits the knowledge of the language statistics possessed by those who speak the language, and depends on experimental results in prediction of the next letter when the preceding text is known." p. 50: "If the language is translated into binary digits (0 or 1) in the most efficient way, the entropy H is the average number of binary digits required per letter of the original language." "It was estimated that when statistical effects extending over not more than eight letters are considered the entropy is roughly 2.3 bits per letter, the redundancy about 50 per cent." "This method is based on a study of the predictability of English; how well can the next letter of a text be predicted when the preceding N letters are known." "From this analysis it appears that, in ordinary literary English, the long range statistical effects (up to 100 letters) reduce the entropy to something of the order of one bit per letter, with a corresponding redundancy of roughly 75%. The redundancy may be still higher when structure extending over paragraphs, chapters, etc. is included. However, as the lengths involved are increased, the parameters in question become more erratic and uncertain, and they depend more critically on the type of text involved."
- Note: the title's phrase "prediction of the next letter" is the chapter's thesis in Shannon's own words.

### P7. Shannon (1951): N-gram entropies of English computed from frequency tables
- Source: `shannon1951prediction`
- Location: pp. 51-54.
- Quotes and numbers: p. 51: "F_N may be called the N-gram entropy; it measures the amount of information or entropy due to statistics extending over N adjacent letters of text." "The equation (1) can be interpreted as measuring the average uncertainty (conditional entropy) of the next letter j when the preceding N-1 letters are known." p. 51: F_0 "may be taken (by definition) to be log2 26, or 4.7 bits per letter"; F_1 = 4.14; F_2 = 7.70 - 4.14 = 3.56; p. 52: F_3 = 11.0 - 7.7 = 3.3, less reliable because the trigram table ignored trigrams bridging two words and "the sampling error in identifying probability with sample frequency is more serious". p. 52: "Since tables of N-gram frequencies were not available for N > 3, F_4, F_5, etc. could not be calculated in the same way." Words: "The most frequent English word "the" has a probability .071 ... The next most frequent word "of" has a probability of .034", and on log-log axes "the curve is approximately a straight line with slope -1" (Zipf, p_n = k/n, p. 52); p. 53: the word entropy "= 11.82 bits per word, or 11.82/4.5 = 2.62 bits per letter since the average word length in English is 4.5 letters." Summary table, p. 54 (page image): 26 letter: F_0 4.70, F_1 4.14, F_2 3.56, F_3 3.3, F_word 2.62; 27 letter: 4.76, 4.03, 3.32, 3.1, 2.14.
- Note: the falling sequence 4.7, 4.1, 3.6, 3.3 is the n-gram story in one row: each extra letter of context lowers the uncertainty about the next letter, and Shannon could not go past three letters because nobody had counted longer n-grams. See Example 6 for an arithmetic quibble (11.82/4.5).

### P8. Shannon (1951): the guessing game, first form
- Source: `shannon1951prediction`
- Location: pp. 54-55 (sequence (8) read on a 300 dpi page image).
- Procedure, p. 54: "Select a short passage unfamiliar to the person who is to do the predicting. He is then asked to guess the first letter in the passage. If the guess is correct he is so informed, and proceeds to guess the second letter. If not, he is told the correct first letter and proceeds to his next guess. This is continued through the text. ... Spaces were included as an additional letter, making a 27 letter alphabet. The first line is the original text; the second line contains a dash for each letter correctly guessed. In the case of incorrect guesses the correct letter is copied in the second line."
- The data, p. 54, as printed:
  ```
  (1) THE ROOM WAS NOT VERY LIGHT A SMALL OBLONG
  (2) ----ROO------NOT-V-----I------SM----OBL----
  (1) READING LAMP ON THE DESK SHED GLOW ON
  (2) REA----------O------D----SHED-GLO--O--
  (1) POLISHED WOOD BUT LESS ON THE SHABBY RED CARPET
  (2) P-L-S-----O---BU--L-S--O------SH-----RE--C------
  ```
  (This block is the ledger's transcription for the drafter; Book 1 cannot print code blocks [D-003], so the chapter must render it as a table or figure.)
- Result, p. 55: "Of a total of 129 letters, 89 or 69% were guessed correctly. The errors, as would be expected, occur most frequently at the beginning of words and syllables where the line of thought has more possibility of branching out."
- Check done in this session: each reduced line is one character longer than its text line (the space that ends the line), giving 43 + 38 + 48 = 129 symbols and exactly 89 dashes, and every printed letter in line (2) matches the letter above it. So the transcription is consistent with the printed totals.
- The reduced text, p. 55: "both lines contain the same information in the sense that it is possible, at least in principle, to recover the first line from the second. To accomplish this we need an identical twin of the individual who produced the sequence. The twin (who must be mathematically, not just biologically identical) will respond in the same way when faced with the same problem."
- Note: this is the outline's key figure. The "identical twin" is the clearest pre-computer statement of why a good predictor is a good compressor, which is why cross-entropy measures a language model (P24).

### P9. Shannon (1951): the guessing game, second form (count the guesses)
- Source: `shannon1951prediction`
- Location: pp. 55-56 (sequence (9) read on a 400 dpi page image).
- Procedure, p. 55: "As before, the subject knows the text up to the current point and is asked to guess the next letter. If he is wrong, he is told so and asked to guess again. This is continued until he finds the correct letter." p. 56: "the numbers in the second line indicate the guess at which the correct letter was obtained."
- First line of (9), p. 56: "THERE IS NO REVERSE ON A MOTORCYCLE A" with guess numbers, one per symbol including spaces, transcribed from the page image: 1 1 1 5 1 1 2 1 1 2 1 1 15 1 17 1 1 1 2 1 3 2 1 2 2 7 1 1 1 1 4 1 1 1 1 1 3 1 (38 numbers for 38 symbols with the line-ending space; so T H E are 1, 1, 1, R is 5, the R of REVERSE took 15 guesses and its V took 17). - Result, p. 56: "Out of 102 symbols the subject guessed right on the first guess 79 times, on the second guess 8 times, on the third guess 3 times, the fourth and fifth guesses 2 each and only eight times required more than five guesses. This algorithm differs in several ways from Kneser and Ney's original algorithm: interpolation is used instead of backoff, we use a separate discount for one- and two-counts instead of a single discount for all counts, and we estimate discounts on held-out data instead of using a formula based on training data counts." p. 59: "we estimated that the standard deviation of the performance of Katz smoothing relative to the baseline method for a single run is about 0.015 bits, which translates to about a 1% difference in perplexity." p. 60: "The factor with the largest influence is the use of a modified backoff distribution as in Kneser-Ney smoothing."
- Also: `jurafsky2026speech` p. 22: Chen and Goodman "showed the advantages of Modified Interpolated Kneser-Ney, which became the standard baseline for n-gram language modeling around the turn of the century, especially because they showed that caches and class-based models provided only minor additional improvement."
- Note: the winner is "modified Kneser-Ney" (Chen and Goodman's own variant, interpolated). Say Kneser and Ney invented the method and Chen and Goodman modified it and showed it won. The wider lesson for the reader is methodological and familiar: compare on several data sets and sizes, tune on held-out data, report variance. See D5 for which year to cite.

### P24. Perplexity and cross-entropy: definitions and intuition
- Sources: `jurafsky2026speech`, `chen1998empirical`, `brown1992class`, `katz1987estimation`, `jelinek2009dawn`.
- Quotes: Jurafsky and Martin, p. 9: "A better model is better at predicting upcoming words, and so it will be less surprised by (i.e., assign a higher probability to) each word when it occurs in the test set." "The perplexity (sometimes abbreviated as PP or PPL) of a language model on a test set is the inverse probability of the test set (one over the probability of the test set), normalized by the number of words (or tokens)." eq. 3.14: perplexity(W) = P(w_1 w_2 ... w_N)^(-1/N). p. 9: "the higher the probability of the word sequence, the lower the perplexity. Thus the lower the perplexity of a model on the data, the better the model." p. 10: WSJ, "trained unigram, bigram, and trigram models on 38 million words ... perplexity of the 1.5 million word test set": Unigram 962, Bigram 170, Trigram 109. p. 10: "A trigram model is less surprised than a unigram model because it has a better idea of what words might come next". p. 10: "the perplexity of two language models is only comparable if they use identical vocabularies." p. 10: "An (intrinsic) improvement in perplexity does not guarantee an (extrinsic) improvement in the performance of a language processing task like speech recognition or machine translation." pp. 10-11: "perplexity can also be thought of as the weighted average branching factor of a language", with the color example: uniform model A over {red, blue, green} on "red red red red blue" has perplexity 3; model B with P(red) = 0.8, P(green) = 0.1, P(blue) = 0.1 has perplexity 0.04096^(-1/5) = 1.89. pp. 20-21: "the cross-entropy H(p, m) is an upper bound on the entropy H(p)"; "Between two models m_1 and m_2, the more accurate model will be the one with the lower cross-entropy." eq. 3.42: H(W) = -(1/N) log2 P(w_1 w_2 ... w_N), and "The perplexity of a model P on a sequence of words W is now formally defined as 2 raised to the power of this cross-entropy".
- Chen and Goodman, p. 7: "given a language model that assigns probability p(T) to a text T, we can derive a compression algorithm that encodes the text T using -log2 p(T) bits." The cross-entropy "can be interpreted as the average number of bits needed to encode each of the W_T words in the test data". "The perplexity PP_p(T) of a model p is the reciprocal of the (geometric) average probability assigned by the model to each word in the test set T, and is related to cross-entropy by the equation PP_p(T) = 2^H_p(T)". "Typical perplexities yielded by n-gram models on English text range from about 50 to almost 1000 (corresponding to cross-entropies from about 6 to 10 bits/word), depending on the type of text."
- Also: Brown et al. 1992, p. 468: "The perplexity of a language model with respect to a sample of text, S, is the reciprocal of the geometric average of the probabilities of the predictions in S. ... Because the perplexity depends not only on the language model but also on the text with respect to which it is measured, it is important that the text be representative". Katz p. 401: "the lower the perplexity, the better the model". Jelinek 2009 p. 488: PP = 2^H(W), introduced at IBM to replace "branching factor" (P12).
- Note: three equivalent ways for the reader: (1) average surprise in bits (cross-entropy); (2) "as confused as if choosing uniformly among PP options" (weighted branching factor); (3) the cost of encoding the text with the model's code (Shannon's twin, P8). Shannon's guessing game measures the same quantity with a human as the model. Formulas allowed by [D-002]: the standard forms H = -(1/N) log2 P(w_1 ... w_N) and PP = 2^H, every symbol explained.

### P25. Cross-entropy, entropy, and the cost of a wrong model
- Source: `goodfellow2016deep`
- Location: pp. 72-73.
- Quotes: p. 73: "A quantity that is closely related to the KL divergence is the cross-entropy H(P, Q) = H(P) + D_KL(P||Q) ... H(P, Q) = -E_x~P log Q(x)." "Minimizing the cross-entropy with respect to Q is equivalent to minimizing the KL divergence, because Q does not participate in the omitted term." p. 72, on KL: "the extra amount of information (measured in bits if we use the base-2 logarithm, but in machine learning we usually use nats and the natural logarithm) needed to send a message containing symbols drawn from probability distribution P, when we use a code that was designed to minimize the length of messages drawn from probability distribution Q."
- Note: Example 4 makes this concrete: rolls of a loaded die scored by a model that thinks the die is fair cost more bits than the true entropy. The gap is the price of the wrong model. Mention nats only if the chapter needs to read GPT-era papers (they report losses in nats); otherwise stay in bits.

### P26. Cross-entropy is the standard training loss (the promise made in `destination`)
- Sources: `goodfellow2016deep`, `radford2018improving`.
- Quotes: Goodfellow et al., p. 174: "Most modern neural networks are trained using maximum likelihood. This means that the cost function is simply the negative log-likelihood, equivalently described as the cross-entropy between the training data and the model distribution." p. 130: "Many authors use the term "cross-entropy" to identify specifically the negative log-likelihood of a Bernoulli or softmax distribution, but that is a misnomer. Any loss consisting of a negative log-likelihood is a cross-entropy between the empirical distribution defined by the training set and the probability distribution defined by model." Also p. 130: "Maximum likelihood thus becomes minimization of the negative log-likelihood (NLL), or equivalently, minimization of the cross-entropy." Radford et al. 2018, p. 3: "Given an unsupervised corpus of tokens U = {u_1, ..., u_n}, we use a standard language modeling objective to maximize the following likelihood: L_1(U) = sum_i log P(u_i | u_i-k, ..., u_i-1; Θ) where k is the size of the context window".
- Note: this closes the loop with `destination` ("the chapter on prediction introduces the standard one"). The training loss of an LLM is the cross-entropy of P24 measured on training text: the n-gram's maximum-likelihood counts and the GPT objective are the same criterion, maximised in different families of models. Keep the formula out; the chapter can say "the average surprise, in nats or bits, of the true next token". `goodfellow2016deep` is from 2016 and `radford2018improving` from 2018; "standard" should carry a date stamp or be phrased as "the loss used by the GPT papers".

### P28. The curse of dimensionality, and why n-grams cannot generalize
- Sources: `bengio2003neural`, `goodfellow2016deep`, `jurafsky2026speech`.
- Quotes: Bengio et al., abstract (p. 1137): "A goal of statistical language modeling is to learn the joint probability function of sequences of words in a language. This is intrinsically difficult because of the curse of dimensionality: a word sequence on which the model will be tested is likely to be different from all the word sequences seen during training. Traditional but very successful approaches based on n-grams obtain generalization by concatenating very short overlapping sequences seen in the training set." p. 1137: "if one wants to model the joint distribution of 10 consecutive words in a natural language with a vocabulary V of size 100,000, there are potentially 100,000^10 - 1 = 10^50 - 1 free parameters." p. 1138: "a new sequence of words is generated by "gluing" very short and overlapping pieces of length 1, 2 ... or up to n words that have been seen frequently in the training data." "Typically researchers have used n = 3, i.e. trigrams". p. 1139: n-grams are "not taking into account the "similarity" between words. For example, having seen the sentence "The cat is walking in the bedroom" in the training corpus should help us generalize to make the sentence "A dog was running in a room" almost as likely". p. 1139, footnote 1: "n-grams with n up to 5 (i.e. 4 words of context) have been reported, though, but due to data scarcity, most predictions are made with a much shorter context."
- Goodfellow et al., p. 152: "Many machine learning problems become exceedingly difficult when the number of dimensions in the data is high. This phenomenon is known as the curse of dimensionality. Of particular concern is that the number of possible distinct configurations of a set of variables increases exponentially as the number of variables increases." p. 153 (Figure 5.9): "For d dimensions and v values to be distinguished along each axis, we seem to need O(v^d) regions and examples." p. 458: "Classical n-gram models are particularly vulnerable to the curse of dimensionality. There are |V|^n possible n-grams and |V| is often very large. Even with a massive training set and modest n, most n-grams will not occur in the training set. One way to view a classical n-gram model is that it is performing nearest neighbor lookup. ... The problem for a language model is even more severe than usual, because any two different words have the same distance from each other in one-hot vector space. It is thus difficult to leverage much information from any "neighbors", only training examples that repeat literally the same context are useful for local generalization." (The source has a dash before "only"; written here with a comma.)
- Jurafsky and Martin, p. 22: neural models solve "the two major problems with n-grams: (1) the number of parameters increases exponentially as the n-gram order increases, and (2) n-grams have no way to generalize from training examples to test set examples unless they use identical words."
- Note: this is where the chapter ends and `embeddings` begins. The two failures, stated once: the table grows as V^n, and "cat" teaches the table nothing about "dog". The reader knows the curse of dimensionality from k-nearest neighbours and density estimation; Goodfellow's p. 458 comparison of an n-gram to nearest-neighbour lookup is the exact bridge.

### P29. Before Shannon: Markov (1913)
- Sources: `jurafsky2026speech`, `chen1998empirical` (Markov 1913 itself not fetched).
- Quotes: Jurafsky and Martin, pp. 21-22: "The underlying mathematics of the n-gram was first proposed by Markov (1913), who used what are now called Markov chains (bigrams and trigrams) to predict whether an upcoming letter in Pushkin's Eugene Onegin would be a vowel or a consonant. ### D1. OCR errors in the Shannon scans (not a dispute, but a trap)

### D2. "Order" means different things
Shannon's "second-order approximation" (1948, p. 388) is the digram (bigram) model: the order counts the letters in the n-gram. In Markov-chain terminology an n-gram model is "a Markov model of order n - 1" (`chen1998empirical` p. 4), so Shannon's second-order is a first-order Markov chain. Shannon's "Second-Order Word Approximation" is a word bigram model. The chapter should use "bigram" and "trigram" and avoid "order" or define it once.

## Scope notes for drafting

- **What not to say.**
  - Not that Shannon invented the n-gram (Markov 1913, P29), or coined the word (not checked, P3).

  - Not to compare GPT-2's bits per character with Shannon's estimate as if on the same footing (P27).

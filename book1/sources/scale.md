### GPT-2 and its staged release (2019)

### SC1. GPT-2's training data: WebText
- Source: `radford2019language`
- Location: section 2.1, p. 3; p. 5.
- Quotes: p. 3: "we scraped all outbound links from Reddit, a social media platform, which received at least 3 karma. This can be thought of as a heuristic indicator for whether other users found the link interesting, educational, or just funny. The resulting dataset, WebText, contains the text subset of these 45 million links." "All results presented in this paper use a preliminary version of WebText which does not include links created after Dec 2017 and which after de-duplication and some heuristic based cleaning contains slightly over 8 million documents for a total of 40 GB of text. We removed all Wikipedia documents from WebText since it is a common data source for other datasets and could complicate analysis due to overlapping training data with test evaluation tasks." p. 5: "<UNK> which is extremely rare in WebText - occurring only 26 times in 40 billion bytes."
- Note: the paper gives the dataset in documents and bytes, **not in tokens**, and does not state how many tokens the models were trained on. The key figure must not invent a GPT-2 token count (see Figures).

### SC2. GPT-2's four model sizes, and "still underfits"
- Source: `radford2019language`
- Location: section 2.3 and Table 2, p. 4; section 3, p. 4; p. 8.
- Quotes: Table 2 (p. 4), "Architecture hyperparameters for the 4 model sizes": 117M (12 layers, d_model 768), 345M (24, 1024), 762M (36, 1280), 1542M (48, 1600). p. 4: "We trained and benchmarked four LMs with approximately log-uniformly spaced sizes. ... The smallest model is equivalent to the original GPT, and the second smallest equivalent to the largest model from BERT (Devlin et al., 2018). Our largest model, which we call GPT-2, has over an order of magnitude more parameters than GPT." "All models still underfit WebText and held-out perplexity has as of yet improved given more training time." p. 4 also: context "increase the context size from 512 to 1024 tokens and a larger batchsize of 512 is used." p. 8: "performance on both the training and test sets of WebText are similar and improve together as model size is increased. This suggests even GPT-2 is still underfitting on WebText in many ways."
- Note: "approximately log-uniformly spaced" means each size is a roughly constant multiple of the last (a bridge to log axes). OpenAI later named the sizes differently (SCD2).

### SC3. GPT-2's zero-shot results: a log-linear trend with size
- Source: `radford2019language`
- Location: abstract p. 1; p. 2; Table 3 p. 5; section 3.3 p. 5; sections 3.6-3.8 pp. 6-7; conclusion p. 10.
- Quotes: abstract (p. 1): "When conditioned on a document plus questions, the answers generated by the language model reach 55 F1 on the CoQA dataset - matching or exceeding the performance of 3 out of 4 baseline systems without using the 127,000+ training examples. The capacity of the language model is essential to the success of zero-shot task transfer and increasing it improves performance in a log-linear fashion across tasks. Our largest model, GPT-2, is a 1.5B parameter Transformer that achieves state of the art results on 7 out of 8 tested language modeling datasets in a zero-shot setting but still underfits WebText." Definition (p. 2): "We demonstrate language models can perform down-stream tasks in a zero-shot setting - without any parameter or architecture modification." Table 3 (p. 5) "Zero-shot results on many datasets. No training or fine-tuning was performed for any of these results.": LAMBADA perplexity 35.13 (117M), 15.60 (345M), 10.87 (762M), 8.63 (1542M) against a previous best of 99.8; still worse than prior work on the One Billion Word Benchmark (42.16 against 21.8; p. 5: "Our model is still significantly worse than prior work on the One Billion Word Benchmark"). Weak tasks: translation "On the WMT-14 English-French test set, GPT-2 gets 5 BLEU" (p. 6); French-English "achieving 11.5 BLEU ... but is still much worse than the 33.5 BLEU of the current best unsupervised machine translation approach" (p. 7); "GPT-2 answers 4.1% of questions correctly" on Natural Questions (p. 7). Conclusion (p. 10): "high-capacity models trained to maximize the likelihood of a sufficiently varied text corpus begin to learn how to perform a surprising amount of tasks without the need for explicit supervision."
- Note: "log-linear" here means performance rises roughly in a straight line when plotted against the logarithm of model size: each multiplication of size buys a similar step. The chapter should give both sides: striking gains on language-modeling benchmarks, and task scores (translation, question answering) far below supervised systems. The PTB perplexity (35.76 vs 46.54) is already in `prediction`; do not repeat it.

- Source: `openai2019better` (original snapshot)

### The general-methods argument

### SC11. Sutton, "The Bitter Lesson" (13 March 2019)
- Source: `sutton2019bitter`
- Quotes: "The biggest lesson that can be read from 70 years of AI research is that general methods that leverage computation are ultimately the most effective, and by a large margin. The ultimate reason for this is Moore's law, or rather its generalization of continued exponentially falling cost per unit of computation." "Seeking an improvement that makes a difference in the shorter term, researchers seek to leverage their human knowledge of the domain, but the only thing that matters in the long run is the leveraging of computation." "Search and learning are the two most important classes of techniques for utilizing massive amounts of computation in AI research." "The bitter lesson is based on the historical observations that 1) AI researchers have often tried to build knowledge into their agents, 2) this always helps in the short term, and is personally satisfying to the researcher, but 3) in the long run it plateaus and even inhibits further progress, and 4) breakthrough progress eventually arrives by an opposing approach based on scaling computation by search and learning. The eventual success is tinged with bitterness, and often incompletely digested, because it is success over a favored, human-centric approach." "One thing that should be learned from the bitter lesson is the great power of general purpose methods, of methods that continue to scale with increased computation even as the available computation becomes very great. The two methods that seem to scale arbitrarily in this way are search and learning." "We want AI agents that can discover like we can, not which contain what we have discovered."
- Examples Sutton gives: computer chess (Kasparov, 1997, "massive, deep search"), computer Go ("delayed by a further 20 years"), speech recognition (DARPA competition in the 1970s; HMMs "won out over the human-knowledge-based methods"; "This led to a major change in all of natural language processing, gradually over decades, where statistics and computation came to dominate the field"), computer vision (SIFT features "discarded"; deep networks "use only the notions of convolution and certain kinds of invariances").
- Note: the essay **does not mention language models, GPT-2, or scaling laws**; "language" occurs once ("natural language processing"). The chapter may present it as the general argument the period seemed to bear out, but not as a comment on LLMs. Its date (four weeks after GPT-2's announcement) is a coincidence the chapter should not dress up as a response. ### SC25. The three power laws, their exponents, and what the constants mean
- Source: `kaplan2020scaling`
- Location: section 1.2, pp. 4-5; notation p. 6; Table 5, p. 20.
- Quotes: p. 4: "The test loss of a Transformer trained to autoregressively model language can be predicted using a power-law when performance is limited by only either the number of non-embedding parameters N, the dataset size D, or the optimally allocated compute budget C_min". Eq. 1.1: L(N) = (N_c/N)^alpha_N; alpha_N ~ 0.076, N_c ~ 8.8 x 10^13 (non-embedding parameters), "For models with a limited number of parameters, trained to convergence on sufficiently large datasets". Eq. 1.2: L(D) = (D_c/D)^alpha_D; alpha_D ~ 0.095, D_c ~ 5.4 x 10^13 (tokens), "For large models trained with a limited dataset with early stopping". Eq. 1.3: L(C_min) = (C_c^min/C_min)^alpha_C^min; alpha_C^min ~ 0.050, C_c^min ~ 3.1 x 10^8 (PF-days). p. 5: "These relations hold across eight orders of magnitude in C_min, six orders of magnitude in N, and over two orders of magnitude in D." "doubling the number of parameters yields a loss that is smaller by a factor 2^-alpha_N = 0.95. The precise numerical values of N_c, C_c^min, and D_c depend on the vocabulary size and tokenization and hence do not have a fundamental meaning." Notation (p. 6): "L - the cross entropy loss in nats." "N - the number of model parameters, excluding all vocabulary and positional embeddings". Table 5 (p. 20) repeats alpha_N = 0.076, alpha_D = 0.095, alpha_C^min = 0.050, and gives alpha_C = 0.057 for the fixed-batch compute trend.
- Setup (p. 7): models from "768 to 1.5 billion non-embedding parameters", datasets "from 22 million to 23 billion tokens", trained on WebText2: "20.3M documents containing 96 GB of text and 1.62 x 10^10 words ... which yields 2.29 x 10^10 tokens".
- Note: loss is in **nats** (natural log), while `prediction` used bits (log base 2): 1 nat = 1.4427 bits. Only exponents are meaningful across setups; the constants are tied to the tokenizer. The largest model studied (1.5B non-embedding) is about GPT-2's size; GPT-3 was the extrapolation.

### SC35. Reconciling Kaplan and Chinchilla (1): counting parameters differently, at small scale
- Source: `pearce2024reconciling`
- Location: abstract p. 1; p. 2; p. 11.
- Quotes: abstract (p. 1): "These studies produced different estimates for how the number of parameters (N) and training tokens (D) should be set to achieve the lowest possible loss for a given compute budget (C). Kaplan: N_optimal proportional to C^0.73, Chinchilla: N_optimal proportional to C^0.50. This paper finds that much of this discrepancy can be attributed to Kaplan counting non-embedding rather than total parameters, combined with their analysis being performed at small scale. Simulating the Chinchilla study under these conditions produces biased scaling coefficients close to Kaplan's. Hence, this paper reaffirms Chinchilla's scaling coefficients, by explaining the primary cause of Kaplan's original overestimation." p. 2: "There have been suggestions that differences could be explained by different optimization schemes (Hoffmann et al., 2022) or datasets (Bi et al., 2024). "determine that responsibility for the discrepancy can be attributed, in decreasing order of significance, to; 1) Kaplan counting non-embedding rather than total compute. 2) Kaplan using a fixed-length warmup period that was too long for smaller models, making them appear less efficient. 3) Kaplan not fully tuning optimization hyperparameters."
- Note: the "embedding" part of a model is the token table of `destination` (and the position table). For small models it is a large share of all parameters, so leaving it out bends the curve at small scale. The reader has the concept from `destination`'s pipeline.

- Location: section 5.1, p. 7.
- Quotes: "It is also important to consider the evaluation metrics used to measure emergent abilities (BIG-Bench, 2022). For instance, using exact string match as the evaluation metric for long-sequence targets may disguise compounding incremental improvements as emergence. Similar logic may apply for multi-step or arithmetic reasoning problems, where models are only scored on whether they get the final answer to a multi-step problem correct, without any credit given to partially correct solutions. However, the jump in final answer accuracy does not explain why the quality of intermediate steps suddenly emerges to above random, and using evaluation metrics that do not give partial credit are at best an incomplete explanation, because emergent abilities are still observed on many classification tasks (e.g., the tasks in Figure 2D-H)." Also: "cross-entropy loss improves even for small model scales where the downstream metrics (exact match, BLEU, and accuracy) are close to random and do not improve".
- Note: the metric explanation predates Schaeffer et al.; Wei et al. considered it "at best an incomplete explanation". This matters for presenting SCD6 fairly.

### SC48. Schaeffer, Miranda, Koyejo (NeurIPS 2023): "a mirage" of the metric
- Source: `schaeffer2023emergent`
- Location: abstract p. 1; pp. 1-2; section 2, pp. 3-4; Figure 5 p. 7; section 6 p. 9.
- Quotes: abstract (p. 1): "Here, we present an alternative explanation for emergent abilities: for a particular task and model family, when analyzing fixed model outputs, emergent abilities appear due to the researcher's choice of metric rather than due to fundamental changes in models with scale. Specifically, nonlinear or discontinuous metrics produce seemingly emergent abilities, whereas linear or continuous metrics produce smooth, continuous, predictable changes in model performance." "we provide evidence that emergent abilities disappear with different metrics or with better statistics, and may not be a fundamental property of scaling AI models." Two properties (pp. 1-2): "Sharpness, transitioning seemingly instantaneously from not present to present" and "Unpredictability, transitioning at seemingly unforeseeable model scales". p. 2: "> 92% of emergent abilities on BIG-Bench tasks [33] (hand-annotated by [37]) appear under either of these two metrics": Multiple Choice Grade and Exact String Match. The model (p. 4): if per-token cross-entropy falls as a power law, the per-token probability of the correct token is exp(-L_CE(N)), and for a task scored correct only if all L tokens are right, "Accuracy(N) ~ p_N(single token correct)^num. of tokens"; "To be clear, we do not require this particular functional form to hold; rather, we use it for illustrative purposes." Footnote 1 (p. 4): "While the independence assumption is not true, the approximation yields results qualitatively matching the observed emergence claims." Their three factors (p. 4): "(1) the researcher choosing a metric that nonlinearly or discontinuously scales the per-token error rate, (2) having insufficient resolution to estimate model performance in the smaller parameter regime, with resolution set by 1/test dataset size, and (3) insufficiently sampling the larger parameter regime." Limitations (p. 9): "First, nothing in this paper should be interpreted as claiming that large language models cannot display emergent abilities; rather, our message is that some previously claimed emergent abilities appear to be mirages induced by researcher analyses. Second, our experiments and analyses are limited because some LLMs with claimed emergent abilities (e.g., PaLM 1, Gopher, Chinchilla) are private and not queryable at the time of our analysis."
- Note: the arithmetic in mechanism item 7 is their mechanism with toy numbers. Their own limitation sentence must be in the chapter if the paper is used, so the reader does not come away with "emergence is debunked".

### SC49. Ganguli et al. (2022): predictable loss, unpredictable abilities
- Source: `ganguli2022predictability`
- Location: abstract p. 1; pp. 3-5.
- Quotes: abstract (p. 1): "these generative models have a paradoxical combination of predictable loss on a broad training distribution (as embodied in their "scaling laws"), and unpredictable specific capabilities, inputs, and outputs. We believe that the high-level predictability and appearance of useful capabilities drives rapid development of such models, while the unpredictable qualities make it difficult to anticipate the consequences of model deployment." p. 4: "Abrupt, specific capability scaling: Though performance is predictable at a general level, performance on a specific task can sometimes emerge quite unpredictably and abruptly at scale. While counter-intuitive, this is possible because any specific task is a tiny slice of a model's output probability distribution, and so can change rapidly even as the full distribution remains smooth." p. 5: "abrupt scaling of a specific capability can co-exist with smooth general scaling for the same reason that daily weather is less predictable than seasonal averages: individual data points can vary much more than broad averages." "three digit addition is performed accurately less than 1% of the time on any model with less than 6B parameters, but this jumps to 8% accuracy on a 13B parameter model and 80% accuracy on a 175B parameter model". p. 3: "We believe that these scaling laws de-risk investments in building larger and generally more capable models despite the high resource costs".
- Note: authors are at Anthropic and include Kaplan and other GPT-3 authors; the weather analogy is theirs and is a good one for this reader (average error vs a single-task score). The 3-digit numbers match GPT-3 Table H.1 few-shot (0.90, 8.40, 80.4).

## Disputed or inconsistent points

### SCD1. Was withholding GPT-2 justified?
Present the decision, the criticism, and the outcome; do not judge.
- **OpenAI's case (February 2019):** misuse for fake news, impersonation, abuse, spam (SC5); explicitly "an experiment", "not sure that it is the right decision today"; wanted time for discussion and for norms (SC5, SC6).
- **Criticism (February 2019):** Lipton: the work was "straight down the middle of the mainstream NLP research", others could replicate it soon, and the media rollout looked orchestrated; he reports Anandkumar called the "too dangerous to release" framing clickbait (SC10). Lipton grants "both sides have an element of truth" and agrees the technology "poses some societal risks".
- **What happened:** releases in February, May, August, and November 2019 (SC7-SC9); OpenAI reported "no strong evidence of misuse so far" (SC8) and "minimal evidence of planned misuse" (SC9), while detection stayed hard (~95% at best, "not high enough", SC8). Others replicated GPT-2 (OpenAI: "more than five groups", SC7).
- Suggested wording: "OpenAI held back its largest model, citing possible misuse, and called the decision an experiment. Critics such as Zachary Lipton argued the work was mainstream and the caution mostly publicity. Over nine months OpenAI released larger versions in stages, reported no strong evidence of misuse, and published the full model in November 2019. Whether the caution was needed is still argued; what the episode showed clearly is that by 2019 a language model's text could be good enough for the question to be asked."

### SCD2. GPT-2's model sizes have two sets of names
- Paper (Table 2, p. 4): 117M, 345M, 762M, 1542M. OpenAI's later posts and report: 124M, 355M, 774M, 1558M (SC7, SC9); "1.5B" in both. No fetched source explains the difference. Suggested: use "1.5 billion" in prose and cite the paper's 1542M in the figure, with a footnote that OpenAI later quoted slightly larger counts. Do not explain the cause (not verified).

### SCD3. Sutton's lesson and its critics
- Sutton (SC11): general methods that use computation win in the long run; building in human knowledge plateaus.
- Brooks (SC12): the human knowledge moved rather than vanished (architectures like convolution, labelled data); cost, energy, and access matter; Moore's law is slowing.
- Common ground in the sources: Sutton himself names "convolution and certain kinds of invariances" as what deep networks keep; that is exactly Brooks's example of built-in knowledge.
- Suggested framing: present Sutton as the period's most quoted statement of the general-methods argument and Brooks as a contemporary objection; let the chapter's own evidence (scaling laws, and the Chinchilla correction about **how** to scale) show that "use more compute" still left real design choices.

### SCD4. Kaplan vs Chinchilla: how to spend more compute
- **Kaplan (2020):** model size should grow much faster than data, N proportional to C^0.73, D proportional to C^0.27 (SC27); GPT-3 followed this (SC17).
- **Hoffmann (2022):** equal scaling, a ~ b ~ 0.5 by three methods (SC31); Chinchilla beat Gopher at equal compute (SC33). Their explanation: Kaplan's fixed learning-rate schedule and small models (SC30).
- **Later explanations:** Pearce and Song (2024): mainly non-embedding parameter counting at small scale (SC35). Porian et al. (2024): last-layer compute, warmup length, and optimizer tuning; careful learning-rate decay "not essential", contradicting Hoffmann's own hypothesis (SC36). Bi et al. (2024, not fetched) proposed data quality (via SC35 and SC37).
- **Where the field landed (as of 2024, per the sources):** both reconciliation papers conclude in favour of Chinchilla-like scaling; Besiroglu et al. report replication by others (SC37). Practice then went **past** compute-optimal for small models to save inference cost (LLaMA, Llama 3; SC38, SC40).
- Suggested wording: "Two careful studies, two years apart, gave different advice. Later work traced most of the gap to how the first study counted parameters and set up its small runs, and sided with the second. In practice, labs then trained small models on far more data than either rule called for, because a smaller model is cheaper to run."

### SCD5. "About 20 tokens per parameter": a reading, not a rule
- Hoffmann et al. never state it (SC32); their Table 3 implies 20-22 per parameter at every size listed.
- Their Approach 3 fit, as published, implies about 70 per parameter (Besiroglu et al., SC37); Besiroglu et al.'s refit gives about 20 and labels "D/N = 20" a "rule of thumb".
- Llama 3's own fit on its data implies about 41 per parameter at 3.8e25 FLOPs (16.55T / 402B; SC40). Hoffmann et al. (SC34) and Bi et al. (via SC37) say the answer depends on data quality.
- Suggested wording: "Read off Hoffmann et al.'s table, the compute-optimal model sees about 20 tokens for every parameter, and that ratio became a rule of thumb. It is a property of their data and setup, not a constant of nature: Meta's own fit for Llama 3 came out near 40."

### SCD6. Are emergent abilities real?
- **For:** Wei et al. (SC46): many tasks show near-random performance up to a scale, then a jump; not predictable by extrapolation. Ganguli et al. (SC49): abrupt task-level scaling alongside smooth loss.
- **Against (partly):** Schaeffer et al. (SC48): most claimed cases use all-or-nothing metrics; with continuous metrics and enough test data, curves are smooth; but "nothing in this paper should be interpreted as claiming that large language models cannot display emergent abilities".
- **Shared ground:** Wei et al. themselves flagged exact-match metrics (SC47), but called that explanation "at best an incomplete explanation" because classification tasks also jump. Both agree cross-entropy improves smoothly.
- Suggested framing: separate two questions. (1) Do some task scores jump sharply with scale? Yes, in the published data (GPT-3 arithmetic, SC20). (2) Does that mean something new appears inside the model at a threshold, rather than a gradual improvement crossing a strict scoring line? That is disputed. Present the p^L arithmetic (mechanism item 7) as the mechanism behind one side, with Wei et al.'s reply.

### SCD7. Energy estimates: large revisions and different methods
- Strubell et al. (2019) estimated 626,155 lbs CO2e for a neural architecture search (SC50); Patterson et al. (2021), with access to how it was actually run, reduced that estimate 88-fold (SC51).
- All carbon figures here are formula estimates: GPU-hours x assumed power x PUE x grid intensity (LLaMA, Llama 2 use TDP; Patterson and PaLM use measured system power). Results depend on location by "~5X-10X" (Patterson abstract).
- Suggested: give energy in MWh or GPU-hours where possible, mark every carbon figure as an estimate with its year, and avoid comparisons to flights unless quoting the paper that made them.

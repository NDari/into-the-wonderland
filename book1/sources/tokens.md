## Sourced claims

### TK1. Gage (1994): byte pair encoding was a compression algorithm
- Source: `gage1994new`
- Location: sections "Theory", "Advantages of BPE", "Results", "Conclusion" (no page numbers; see table above).
- Quotes: Introduction: "This article describes a simple general-purpose data compression algorithm, called Byte Pair Encoding (BPE), which provides almost as much compression as the popular Lempel, Ziv, and Welch (LZW) method." "The main advantage of BPE is the small, fast expansion routine, ideal for applications with limited memory." Theory: "The simple approach I present is to replace common pairs of bytes by single bytes." "The algorithm compresses data by finding the most frequently occurring pairs of adjacent bytes in the data and replacing all instances of the pair with a byte that was not in the original data. The algorithm repeats this process until no further compression is possible, either because there are no more frequently occurring pairs or there are no more unused bytes to represent pairs. The algorithm writes out the table of pair substitutions before the packed data." Advantages: "The BPE algorithm adapts exponentially to patterns, since both bytes in a pair can represent previously defined pair codes." "For example, under BPE a run of 1024 identical bytes in a row is reduced to a single byte after only ten pair substitutions." The worked example: "Original input data string: ABABCABCD / Change pair AB to unused X: XXCXCD / Change pair XC to unused Y: XYYD". Also: "compression never increases the data size". Results (Table 1): on the 544,789-byte file WIN386.EXE, default BPE gave 276,955 bytes against 299,118 (12-bit LZW) and 292,588 (14-bit LZW); compression 55 s against 28 s, expansion 20 s against 25-27 s; expansion data 550 bytes. Conclusion: "The disadvantages of BPE are slow compression speed and a lower compression ratio than provided by some of the commonly used algorithms, such as LZW."
- Also: the author note says Gage was "a software engineer in Colorado Springs"; the journal is the C Users Journal (February 1994; volume and pages from `sennrich2016neural`'s reference list).
- Note: three things for the chapter. (1) BPE began as compression of bytes for software, with no connection to language or neural networks; it was borrowed 21 years later (TK4). (2) Gage's toy example ABABCABCD is a ready-made first illustration of "replace the most frequent pair, repeat", and it shows the nesting (XC contains X, which is AB). (3) Gage's goal was a small, fast decoder, not a vocabulary. Do not say Gage invented subword tokenization. The 1024-byte claim is checkable arithmetic (2^10 = 1024; see Mechanism item 5).

### TK4. Sennrich, Haddow, and Birch adapt BPE to words: the procedure
- Source: `sennrich2016neural`
- Location: pp. 1716-1718.
- Quotes: p. 1716: "We adapt byte pair encoding (BPE) (Gage, 1994), a compression algorithm, to the task of word segmentation. BPE allows for the representation of an open vocabulary through a fixed-size vocabulary of variable-length character sequences". p. 1717: "Byte Pair Encoding (BPE) (Gage, 1994) is a simple data compression technique that iteratively replaces the most frequent pair of bytes in a sequence with a single, unused byte. We adapt this algorithm for word segmentation. Instead of merging frequent pairs of bytes, we merge characters or character sequences." "Firstly, we initialize the symbol vocabulary with the character vocabulary, and represent each word as a sequence of characters, plus a special end-of-word symbol '·', which allows us to restore the original tokenization after translation. We iteratively count all symbol pairs and replace each occurrence of the most frequent pair ('A', 'B') with a new symbol 'AB'. Each merge operation produces a new symbol which represents a character n-gram. Frequent character n-grams (or whole words) are eventually merged into a single symbol, thus BPE requires no shortlist. The final symbol vocabulary size is equal to the size of the initial vocabulary, plus the number of merge operations, the latter is the only hyperparameter of the algorithm." (The source sets off the last clause with an en-dash; written here with a comma.) "For efficiency, we do not consider pairs that cross word boundaries. The algorithm can thus be run on the dictionary extracted from a text, with each word being weighted by its frequency." p. 1718: "At test time, we first split words into sequences of characters, then apply the learned operations to merge the characters into larger, known symbols. This is applicable to any word, and allows for open-vocabulary networks with fixed symbol vocabularies." p. 1718, on Huffman codes: "our symbol sequences are still interpretable as subword units, and that the network can generalize to translate and produce new words (unseen at training time) on the basis of these subword units."
- Also footnote 3, p. 1718: "The only symbols that will be unknown at test time are unknown characters, or symbols of which all occurrences in the training text have been merged into larger symbols, like 'safeguar', which has all occurrences in our training text merged into 'safeguard'."
- Note: the formula "final vocabulary = initial characters + number of merges" is the cleanest single sentence for the reader and is checkable on the toy (Mechanism item 1). The two halves (learn merges from counts; then replay the merges, in order, on any new word) should be presented as separate steps, matching the source. The end-of-word marker is what lets "est" at the end of "newest" differ from "est" inside "estate"; say so only as an illustration, not as the paper's example.

### TK8. WordPiece's origin: Japanese and Korean voice search (Schuster and Nakajima, 2012)
- Source: `schuster2012japanese`
- Location: pp. 5149-5151 (OCR).
- Quotes: Abstract, p. 5149: "We describe the techniques used to deal with an infinite vocabulary". p. 5149: "The concept of spaces between words exists not at all or only partially depending on the language (traditionally Japanese and Chinese do not have spaces between characters ...". p. 5150: "One choice is to use the Unicode characters themselves but in experiments we found that for Japanese there are too many pronunciations per character on average to be able to run an efficient and accurate enough search. Other segmenters we tried had problems segmenting arbitrary sequences of characters as often found in web text data (resulting in many out-of-vocabulary words (OOVs)) and also were often language-dependent and complex ... we wanted to use a technique that depends only on the data and preferably does not produce any OOVs. We developed a technique (WordPieceModel) to learn word units from large amounts of text automatically and incrementally by running a greedy algorithm as described below. This gives us a user-specified number of word units (we often use 200k) which are chosen in a greedy way without focusing on semantics to maximize likelihood on the language model training data". The algorithm (p. 5150): "1. Initialize the word unit inventory with the basic Unicode characters (Kanji, Hiragana, Katakana for Japanese, Hangul for Korean) and including all ASCII, ending up with about 22000 total for Japanese and 11000 for Korean. 2. Build a language model on the training data using the inventory from 1. 3. Generate a new word unit by combining two units out of the current word inventory to increment the word unit inventory by one. Choose the new word unit out of all possible ones that increases the likelihood on the training data the most when added to the model. 4. Goto 2 until a predefined limit of word units is reached or the likelihood increase falls below a certain threshold." Also p. 5150: "Using these greedy speed-ups we can build a 200k word piece inventory out of a frequency-weighted query list in a few hours on a single machine." And: "Since the word inventory is limited to 200k units that can model every possible character sequence and does not produce any OOVs".
- Space markers, p. 5150: "When segmenting the LM data with WordPieceModel attach a space marker (we use an underscore or a tilde) before and after each unit when you see a space, otherwise don't attach."
- Note: WordPiece is the same family as BPE (start from characters, repeatedly join two units), with a different choice rule: the pair that most increases language-model likelihood, not the most frequent pair (see TKD3). The application was speech recognition, and the language model was an n-gram model (p. 5151: "usually entropy-pruned 3- to 5-grams with Katz back-off"), which the reader knows. That makes a good bridge: subword units first appeared in a 2000s-style n-gram pipeline. The paper was not in machine translation and not in neural networks.

### TK9. WordPiece in Google's neural translation system (Wu et al., 2016)
- Source: `wu2016google`
- Location: pp. 1, 7-8, 16-17.
- Quotes: Abstract, p. 1: "To improve handling of rare words, we divide words into a limited set of common sub-word units ("wordpieces") for both input and output. This method provides a good balance between the flexibility of "character"-delimited models and the efficiency of "word"-delimited models". p. 7: "we adopt the wordpiece model (WPM) implementation initially developed to solve a Japanese/Korean segmentation problem for the Google speech recognition system [35]. This approach is completely data-driven and guaranteed to generate a deterministic segmentation for any possible sequence of characters. It is similar to the method used in [38] to deal with rare words in Neural Machine Translation." ([35] is Schuster and Nakajima 2012; [38] is Sennrich et al.) The example (p. 7): "Word: Jet makers feud over seat width with big orders at stake" / "wordpieces: \_J et \_makers \_fe ud \_over \_seat \_width \_with \_big \_orders \_at \_stake", with "'\_' is a special character added to mark the beginning of a word." p. 7: "Given a training corpus and a number of desired tokens D, the optimization problem is to select D wordpieces such that the resulting corpus is minimal in the number of wordpieces when segmented according to the chosen wordpiece model." "We also cut the number of basic characters to a manageable number depending on the data (roughly 500 for Western languages, more for Asian languages) and map the rest to a special unknown character". ### TK11. The unigram language model tokenizer (Kudo, 2018)
- Source: `kudo2018subword`
- Location: pp. 66, 68-69.
- Quotes: Abstract, p. 66: "While sentences are usually converted into unique subword sequences, subword segmentation is potentially ambiguous and multiple segmentations are possible even with the same vocabulary." "we propose a new subword segmentation algorithm based on a unigram language model." Table 1 (p. 66) shows five segmentations of "Hello World" with the same vocabulary, for example "Hell/o/ world", "H/ello/ world", "He/llo/ world". p. 68, on BPE: "BPE first splits the whole sentence into individual characters. The most frequent adjacent pairs of characters are then consecutively merged until reaching a desired vocabulary size." "Therefore, only with a small fixed size of vocabulary (usually 16k to 32k), the number of required symbols to encode a sentence will not significantly increase". "One downside is, however, that BPE is based on a greedy and deterministic symbol replacement, which can not provide multiple segmentations with probabilities." The unigram model (p. 68): "The unigram language model makes an assumption that each subword occurs independently, and consequently, the probability of a subword sequence x = (x1, ..., xM) is formulated as the product of the subword occurrence probabilities p(xi)". The most probable segmentation is found "with the Viterbi algorithm". The vocabulary is found by an iterative algorithm (p. 68): "1. Heuristically make a reasonably big seed vocabulary from the training corpus. 2. Repeat the following steps until |V| reaches a desired vocabulary size. (a) Fixing the set of vocabulary, optimize p(x) with the EM algorithm. (b) Compute the loss_i for each subword x_i, where loss_i represents how likely the likelihood L is reduced when the subword x_i is removed from the current vocabulary. (c) Sort the symbols by loss_i and keep top η % of subwords (η is 80, for example). Note that we always keep the subwords consisting of a single character to avoid out-of-vocabulary." p. 69: "BPE and the unigram language model share the same idea that they encode a text using fewer bits with a certain data compression principle (dictionary vs. entropy)."
- Footnotes, p. 68: "Strictly speaking, wordpiece model (Schuster and Nakajima, 2012) is different from BPE. We consider wordpiece as a variant of BPE, as it also uses an incremental vocabulary generation with a different loss function." "Wordpiece model uses a likelihood instead of frequency."
- Note: for the reader, the unigram method is the mirror image of BPE: BPE starts small and adds merges; the unigram method starts big and prunes. Its pieces have probabilities, and picking a segmentation is a Viterbi search, which the reader may know from hidden Markov models. Its original purpose was "subword regularization" (training on several segmentations as noise), which is detail the chapter can skip. The EM algorithm and Viterbi can be named without being explained, or omitted [D-002]. As of 2024, `land2024fishing` (p. 11631) says the unigram method "despite work suggesting it outperforms BPE ... is not in common use"; date-stamp if used.

### TK12. SentencePiece (Kudo and Richardson, 2018): no pre-splitting into words, whitespace as a symbol
- Source: `kudo2018sentencepiece`
- Location: pp. 66-68.
- Quotes: Abstract, p. 66: "While existing subword segmentation tools assume that the input is pre-tokenized into word sequences, SentencePiece can train subword models directly from raw sentences, which allows us to make a purely end-to-end and language independent system." p. 66: the Moses pre- and postprocessor "is built upon hand-crafted and language dependent rules whose effectiveness for NMT has not been proven. In addition, these tools are mainly designed for European languages where words are segmented with whitespaces. To train NMT systems for non-segmented languages such as Chinese, Korean and Japanese, we need to run word segmenters independently." "SentencePiece implements two subword segmentation algorithms, byte-pair-encoding (BPE) (Sennrich et al., 2016) and unigram language model (Kudo, 2018), with the extension of direct training from raw sentences." p. 67: "We call this design lossless tokenization, in which all the information to reproduce the normalized text is preserved in the encoder's output. The basic idea of lossless tokenization is to treat the input text just as a sequence of Unicode characters. Even whitespace is handled as a normal symbol. For the sake of clarity, SentencePiece first escapes the whitespace with a meta symbol \_ (U+2581), and tokenizes the input into an arbitrary subword sequence, for example: Raw text: Hello\_world. Tokenized: [Hello] [\_wor] [ld] [.]" The irreversibility it fixes (p. 67): with ordinary tokenization of "Hello world." into [Hello] [world] [.], "The information that no space exists between "world" and "." is not kept in the tokenized sequence." p. 68: "While subword-nmt specifies the number of merge operations, SentencePiece specifies the final size of vocabulary". "SentencePiece reserves vocabulary ids for special meta symbols, e.g., unknown symbol (<unk>), BOS (<s>), EOS (</s>) and padding (<pad>)." p. 68: "By default, SentencePiece normalizes the input text with the Unicode NFKC normalization."
- Note: the motivation the outline asks for is here in the authors' words: language independence (no word segmenter for Japanese or Chinese) and losslessness (whitespace is just another symbol, so decoding is exact). The symbol is U+2581 "LOWER ONE EIGHTH BLOCK", which looks like an underscore; the chapter should show it as "▁" and say it stands for a space. The special symbols <unk>, <s>, </s>, <pad> are the second family of special-token examples (beginning and end of sequence, padding). SentencePiece is a tool that implements BPE and unigram, not a third algorithm; say so.

### TK13. GPT-2's byte-level BPE: the paper
- Source: `radford2019language`
- Location: p. 4 (section 2.2, "Input Representation"; section 2.3).
- Quotes: "A general language model (LM) should be able to compute the probability of (and also generate) any string. Current large scale LMs include pre-processing steps such as lowercasing, tokenization, and out-of-vocabulary tokens which restrict the space of model-able strings. While processing Unicode strings as a sequence of UTF-8 bytes elegantly fulfills this requirement as exemplified in work such as Gillick et al. (2015), current byte-level LMs are not competitive with word-level LMs on large scale datasets such as the One Billion Word Benchmark (Al-Rfou et al., 2018)." "Byte Pair Encoding (BPE) (Sennrich et al., 2015) is a practical middle ground between character and word level language modeling which effectively interpolates between word level inputs for frequent symbol sequences and character level inputs for infrequent symbol sequences. Despite its name, reference BPE implementations often operate on Unicode code points and not byte sequences. These implementations would require including the full space of Unicode symbols in order to model all Unicode strings. This would result in a base vocabulary of over 130,000 before any multi-symbol tokens are added. This is prohibitively large compared to the 32,000 to 64,000 token vocabularies often used with BPE. In contrast, a byte-level version of BPE only requires a base vocabulary of size 256. However, directly applying BPE to the byte sequence results in sub-optimal merges due to BPE using a greedy frequency based heuristic for building the token vocabulary. We observed BPE including many versions of common words like dog since they occur in many variations such as dog. dog! dog? . This results in a sub-optimal allocation of limited vocabulary slots and model capacity. To avoid this, we prevent BPE from merging across character categories for any byte sequence. We add an exception for spaces which significantly improves the compression efficiency while adding only minimal fragmentation of words across multiple vocab tokens." "Since our approach can assign a probability to any Unicode string, this allows us to evaluate our LMs on any dataset regardless of pre-processing, tokenization, or vocab size." Section 2.3: "The vocabulary is expanded to 50,257. We also increase the context size from 512 to 1024 tokens".
- Note: the paper does **not** say how 50,257 is made up; that comes from the code release (TK14). "Character categories" means classes such as letters, digits, and punctuation; the code makes this concrete (TK14). The paper's phrase "the 32,000 to 64,000 token vocabularies often used with BPE" is a 2019 statement; date it. Byte-level BPE needs UTF-8 (TK23) for the reader to see why 256 suffices: every possible text is a sequence of bytes, and there are only 256 byte values.

### TK14. GPT-2's byte-level BPE: the released code and vocabulary
- Source: `openai2019gpt2code`
- Facts (counted with `python3 -I` on the downloaded files, 2026-11-11): `encoder.json` has 50,257 entries with IDs 0 to 50,256. `vocab.bpe` has a header line "#version: 0.2" and 50,000 merge rules. The entry "<|endoftext|>" has ID 50,256 (the last). IDs 0 to 255 are the 256 single-byte tokens. So 50,257 = 256 bytes + 50,000 merges + 1 special token. The first merges in the file are "Ġ t", "Ġ a", "h e", "i n", "r e", "o n", "Ġt he", where "Ġ" is how the code displays a space byte.
- The pre-splitting rule (`src/encoder.py`): before any merging, text is cut with the pattern `'s|'t|'re|'ve|'m|'ll|'d| ?\p{L}+| ?\p{N}+| ?[^\s\p{L}\p{N}]+|\s+(?!\S)|\s+`, that is: common English contractions; an optional space followed by a run of letters; an optional space followed by a run of digits; an optional space followed by a run of other symbols; and runs of whitespace. Merges never cross these pieces. This is the paper's "prevent BPE from merging across character categories" with the "exception for spaces". A comment in the code reads: "Should haved added re.IGNORECASE so BPE merges can happen for capitalized versions of contractions" (sic).
- The byte-to-character table (`bytes_to_unicode`), docstring: "This means you need a large # of unicode characters in your vocab if you want to avoid UNKs. When you're at something like a 10B token dataset you end up needing around 5K for decent coverage. This is a signficant percentage of your normal, say, 32K bpe vocab." (sic.)
- Preliminary tokenizations (this agent; a standard-library re-implementation of `encoder.py` that replaces `\p{L}` with `[^\W\d_]` and `\p{N}` with `\d`, which is exact for the inputs below): "The cat sat on the mat." gives 7 tokens: The | ␣cat | ␣sat | ␣on | ␣the | ␣mat | . with IDs 464, 3797, 3332, 319, 262, 2603, 13. ### TK23. Characters are not bytes: UTF-8

- Location: p. 4 (section 3).

## Disputed or inconsistent points

### TKD1. Sennrich et al.'s toy example is internally inconsistent
- Algorithm 1 (p. 1718) runs on {low: 5, lower: 2, newest: 6, widest: 3} with the end-of-word symbol "</w>". Figure 1 (p. 1718) is captioned as learned "from dictionary {'low', 'lowest', 'newer', 'wider'}", with no frequencies and the symbol "·". - Suggested handling: build the chapter's merge table from Algorithm 1's corpus (it has counts, which the reader needs in order to see "most frequent"), say plainly that the chapter follows the toy corpus printed in Sennrich et al.'s code, and show "lowest", which never occurs in the corpus, being segmented as low | est</w> (preliminary). Do not reproduce Figure 1 alongside it.

### TKD2. 2015 or 2016 for BPE in translation
See TK7. Both are defensible: arXiv August 2015, ACL August 2016. Later papers cite "2015" (GPT-2, LLaMA); the outline says 2015-2016.

### TKD3. What WordPiece optimises, and whether it is "a kind of BPE"
- Schuster and Nakajima (2012, p. 5150): add the pair that "increases the likelihood on the training data the most".
- Wu et al. (2016, p. 7): "select D wordpieces such that the resulting corpus is minimal in the number of wordpieces"; the greedy algorithm "is similar to [38] [Sennrich et al.] and is described in more detail in [35] [Schuster and Nakajima]".
- Kudo (2018, p. 68, footnotes 1 and 2): "Strictly speaking, wordpiece model ... is different from BPE. We consider wordpiece as a variant of BPE ... Wordpiece model uses a likelihood instead of frequency."
- Also: the boundary markers differ: Schuster and Nakajima mark spaces on both sides; Wu et al. mark only the beginning of a word ("Compared to the original implementation used in [35], we use a special symbol only at the beginning of the words and not at both ends", p. 7); BERT marks continuations with "##" (TK10).
- Note: WordPiece's training procedure was never published in full detail (both papers summarise it). Safe wording: "WordPiece, developed at Google for Japanese and Korean speech recognition in 2012, also builds its vocabulary by repeatedly joining two pieces, but chooses the join that most improves a language model's fit to the training text rather than the most frequent pair." Do not claim WordPiece came before BPE in a priority sense: Gage's BPE (1994) predates it, and Sennrich et al. developed their method independently of it (they do not cite it).

### TKD4. "Typical" vocabulary sizes disagree, and they are dated
Sennrich et al. 2016 (p. 1715): word vocabularies "typically limited to 30 000-50 000 words". Wu et al. 2016 (p. 7): "between 8k and 32k wordpieces". Kudo 2018 (p. 68): "usually 16k to 32k". Radford et al. 2019 (p. 4): "the 32,000 to 64,000 token vocabularies often used with BPE". BERT 2019: 30,000. GPT-2 2019: 50,257. Llama 3 2024: 128,000. OpenAI `o200k_base` (as of 2026-11): about 200,000. These are not in conflict, they are snapshots; the chapter should present them as a trend with dates and not state a single "typical" size.

### TKD5. Which vocabulary file GPT-3 used
`brown2020language` (p. 8) says GPT-3 reuses GPT-2's tokenization. OpenAI's `tiktoken` maps the original GPT-3 models ("davinci" and the others) to `r50k_base`, which is byte-for-byte GPT-2's vocabulary (checked, TK18). `singh2024tokenization` (p. 2 and Figures 2-3) says `p50k_base` is "the BPE tokenizer for GPT-3" and "was used to train GPT-3"; tiktoken maps `p50k_base` to the later "text-davinci-002/003" and Codex models. Follow the GPT-3 paper and tiktoken; if Singh and Strouse's Figure 2 (which 3-digit strings are single tokens) is used, say it shows `p50k_base`. This agent's count for `r50k_base` (777 of the 1,000 strings "000" to "999" are single tokens) can be used instead, computed by the drafter's script.

### TKD6. Llama 3's vocabulary size
`grattafiori2024llama` gives 128,000 (Table 3, p. 7) and "128K tokens" (p. 7). Meta's code adds 256 reserved special tokens after the ordinary tokens (TK17). If the ordinary vocabulary is 128,000, the model's embedding table has 128,256 rows; the ordinary count could not be checked because the tokenizer file is gated. Keep "128,000" as the paper's figure (as `destination.md` does) and, if special tokens are discussed, say "plus 256 IDs reserved for special tokens, according to Meta's released code".

### TKD7. Is tokenization the cause of the spelling and arithmetic failures?
Present as a contributing cause supported by evidence, not as the whole explanation.
- For: GPT-3's authors point to BPE tokens covering "significant fractions of a word" (TK20); Nogueira et al. tie T5's failure to inconsistent number tokenization (TK21); Singh and Strouse show accuracy changes with chunk direction alone (TK21).
- Against or qualifying: Kaushal and Mahowald show character information is "robustly" encoded in token embeddings (TK20); Nogueira et al. find character-level digits also fail beyond 15 digits and that length extrapolation fails regardless (p. 2, p. 4); Singh and Strouse find the gap shrinks with scale (TK21).
- Suggested wording: "Tokenization makes these tasks harder in a way that can be measured; it is not the only reason models find them hard."

### TKD8. Small inconsistencies in sources
- `brown2020language` p. 24 says "0.7 words per token" while defining nothing further; OpenAI's help page says "three-quarters of a word". Same idea, different rounding.
- `sennrich2016neural` abstract says "up to 1.1 and 1.3 BLEU"; the arXiv abstract (record fetched) says "by 1.1 and 1.3 BLEU". Immaterial.
- `petrov2023language` says "differences up to 15 times" (abstract) but its own Table 1 has a GPT-2 premium of 18.76 for Shan; the 15 is the ChatGPT/GPT-4 figure (15.05). Quote the table.
- `gpt2` code docstring and comment contain typos ("signficant", "haved"); quote with "(sic)" or paraphrase.

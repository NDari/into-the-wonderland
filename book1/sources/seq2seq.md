## Sourced claims

### Q1. Mikolov et al. (2010): a recurrent network as a language model, compared with n-grams
- Source: `mikolov2010recurrent`
- Location: pp. 1045-1048.
- Quotes: Abstract (p. 1045): "A new recurrent neural network based language model (RNN LM) with applications to speech recognition is presented. Results indicate that it is possible to obtain around 50% reduction of perplexity by using mixture of several RNN LMs, compared to a state of the art backoff language model. Speech recognition experiments show around 18% reduction of word error rate on the Wall Street Journal task when comparing models trained on the same amount of data, and around 5% on the much harder NIST RT05 task, even when the backoff model is trained on much more data than the RNN LM. We provide ample empirical evidence to suggest that connectionist language models are superior to standard n-gram techniques, except their high computational (training) complexity."
- Why recurrence (p. 1045): "A major deficiency of Bengio's approach is that a feedforward network has to use fixed length context that needs to be specified ad hoc before training. Usually this means that neural networks see only five to ten preceding words when predicting the next one." Then: "Recurrent neural networks do not use limited size of context." 
- The model (p. 1046): "we have used an architecture that is usually called a simple recurrent neural network or Elman network [7]". Input is the current word (1-of-N coding) plus the previous hidden state; sigmoid hidden layer; softmax output; vocabulary "in practice 30 000 - 200 000" (source prints an en-dash), hidden layer "usually 30 - 500 hidden units". Basic training "is also referred to as truncated backpropagation through time with τ = 1. It is not optimal" (p. 1046). "For RNN LM, only size of hidden (context) layer needs to be selected" (p. 1046).
- Numbers (p. 1047, Table 2, WSJ DEV, 6.4M training words, read on the page image): modified Kneser-Ney 5-gram (KN5) perplexity 221, word error rate 13.5%. Single RNN 250/5: perplexity 173 alone, 155 interpolated with KN5. Mixture of 3 dynamic RNNs: 128 alone, 121 with KN5, WER 11.1%. Text, p. 1047: "perplexity reductions are one of the largest ever reported, almost 50% when comparing KN 5gram and mixture of 3 dynamic RNN LMs - actually, by mixing static and dynamic RNN LMs with larger learning rate used when processing testing data (α = 0.3), the best perplexity result was 112." (source prints an en-dash before "actually"). Table 1 (p. 1047): the gain grows with data: KN5 336 vs KN5+RNN 271 at 200K words; 287 vs 225 at 1M; 221 vs 156 at 6.4M.
- Cost (p. 1047): "As it is very time consuming to train RNN LM on large data, we have used only up to 6.4M words for training RNN models (300K sentences) - it takes several weeks to train the most complex models." Also: about 6 hours to train on the Brown corpus (800K words, 100 hidden units) "while Bengio reports 113 days for basic implementation and 26 hours with importance sampling". NIST RT05 (p. 1048): RNNs used 5.4M words of in-domain data "RNN training was too time consuming with more data" (source prints an en-dash), against backoff models trained on more than 1.3G words.
- Conclusion (p. 1048): "Obtained results are breaking myth that language modeling is just about counting n-grams". And a limit: "it does not seem that simple recurrent neural networks can capture truly long context information, as cache models still provide complementary information even to dynamic models trained with BPTT. Explanation is discussed in [6]" ([6] is `bengio1994learning`).
- Note: "around 50%" needs care. 221 to 112 is 49% (the best mix, including models that keep learning on the test data); 221 to 121 is 45%; a single RNN is 22% (alone) or 30% (interpolated). "Dynamic" means the model keeps training while it reads the test set (p. 1046). The chapter can safely say "a single recurrent model cut perplexity by about a fifth to a third; a mixture of several, some adapting as they read, cut it nearly in half", and must say the n-gram baseline was trained on the same 6.4M words in Table 2. Perplexity itself is defined in `prediction`.

### Q2. Phrase-based statistical machine translation (the 2000s baseline)
- Sources: `koehn2003statistical`; `cho2014learning`.
- Quotes: Koehn et al., abstract (p. 48): "Our empirical results, which hold for all examined language pairs, suggest that the highest levels of performance can be obtained through relatively simple means: heuristic learning of phrase translations from word-based alignments and lexical weighting of phrase translations. Surprisingly, learning phrases longer than three words and learning phrases from high-accuracy word-level alignment models does not have a strong impact on performance." (source hyphenates "word-level" across a line). The model (p. 49): "During decoding, the foreign input sentence f is segmented into a sequence of I phrases ... Each foreign phrase in f is translated into an English phrase. The English phrases may be reordered." (symbols lost in extraction; paraphrase). Phrase table built by counting (p. 50, section 3.1): "We collect all aligned phrase pairs that are consistent with the word alignment ... we estimate the phrase translation probability distribution by relative frequency" (the count ratio formula did not extract), "No smoothing is performed." Word alignments come from "the Giza++ [Och and Ney, 2000] toolkit for the IBM models [Brown et al., 1993]" (p. 50). Odd phrases are kept: "translations for phrases such as "house the" may be learned" (p. 50). Table 1 (p. 51, page image): "Size of the phrase translation table in terms of distinct phrase pairs (maximum phrase length 4)": 84k pairs from 10k training sentence pairs, rising to 3,152k from 320k. Data (p. 51): Europarl, "over 20 million words in each of the eleven official languages"; test "1755 sentences of length 5-15". Decoder (p. 49): beam search; "translating 1755 sentence of length 5-15 words takes about 10 minutes on a 2 GHz Linux system".
- How the parts are combined, in a 2014 description: Cho et al. (pp. 1726-1727): "most SMT systems model log p(f | e) as a log-linear model with additional features and corresponding weights" (Eq. 9: a weighted sum of feature functions), "The weights are often optimized to maximize the BLEU score on a development set." "In the phrase-based SMT framework introduced in (Koehn et al., 2003) and (Marcu and Wong, 2002), the translation model log p(e | f) is factorized into the translation probabilities of matching phrases in the source and target sentences."
- Note: two honest bridges for the reader. (1) The phrase table is estimated by counting and dividing, exactly like an n-gram table (`prediction`), and it is huge and sparse (3 million pairs from 320k sentences). (2) The log-linear combination is a weighted sum of features passed through an exponential, the same form as logistic regression. Phrase-based systems also use an n-gram language model for the target side (Koehn et al. use a trigram, p. 49).

### Q4. BLEU, the score every paper in this chapter reports
- Source: `papineni2002bleu`
- Location: pp. 311, 312, 315.
- Quotes: p. 311: "The closer a machine translation is to a professional human translation, the better it is. This is the central idea behind our proposal." "The main idea is to use a weighted average of variable length phrase matches against the reference translations." p. 312: "The cornerstone of our metric is the familiar precision measure." The "clipping" example (p. 312): candidate "the the the the the the the" against references "The cat is on the mat." and "There is a cat on the mat." has "Modified Unigram Precision = 2/7". p. 315: "We take the geometric mean of the test corpus' modified precision scores and then multiply the result by an exponential brevity penalty factor." "In our baseline, we use N = 4 and uniform weights wn = 1/N." "The BLEU metric ranges from 0 to 1. Few translations will attain a score of 1 unless they are identical to a reference translation. For this reason, even a human translator will not necessarily score 1." Also p. 315: a human translator "scored 0.3468 against four references and scored 0.2571 against two references".
- Note: one-line definition for the chapter: "BLEU counts how many of the system's word sequences of length one to four also appear in a human reference translation, combines these into one number, and penalizes output that is too short; papers report it times 100." Later papers report BLEU on a 0-100 scale (for example 33.30); Koehn et al. 2003 use 0-1 (".27", Fig. 1, p. 51). The BLEU paper is the reader's only needed source; BLEU's limits are noted by `cho2014properties` p. 109 ("it is not the perfect metric") and `wu2016google` p. 14 ("BLEU score does not fully capture the quality of a translation").

### Q5. Before 2014, neural networks were parts inside phrase-based systems
- Sources: `bahdanau2015neural`, `koehn2017neural`, `cho2014learning`.
- Quotes: Bahdanau et al., p. 9: "the role of neural networks has been largely limited to simply providing a single feature to an existing statistical machine translation system or to re-rank a list of candidate translations provided by an existing system." Koehn, p. 5: "The modern resurrection of neural methods in machine translation started with the integration of neural language models into traditional statistical machine translation systems. The pioneering work by Schwenk (2007) showed large improvements in public evaluation campaigns. However, these ideas were only slowly adopted, mainly due to computational concerns. The use of GPUs for training also posed a challenge for many research groups that simply lacked such hardware or the experience to exploit it." - Note: "rescoring an n-best list" = the old system proposes, say, 1000 candidate translations and the network re-ranks them. This is how Mikolov-style language models entered translation. Koehn (p. 6) also singles out Devlin et al. (2014) as "influential since it showed large quality improvements on top of a very competitive statistical machine translation system" (not fetched).

### Q8. The gated recurrent unit (GRU)
- Sources: `cho2014learning`, `chung2014empirical`, `bahdanau2015neural`.
- Quotes: Cho et al., p. 1726, section "Hidden Unit that Adaptively Remembers and Forgets": "we also propose a new type of hidden unit ... that has been motivated by the LSTM unit but is much simpler to compute and implement." Reset gate: "when the reset gate is close to 0, the hidden state is forced to ignore the previous hidden state and reset with the current input only. This effectively allows the hidden state to drop any information that is found to be irrelevant later in the future". Update gate: "controls how much information from the previous hidden state will carry over to the current hidden state. This acts similarly to the memory cell in the LSTM network and helps the RNN to remember long-term information." Footnote 1 (p. 1726): the LSTM unit "has a memory cell and four gating units that adaptively control the information flow inside the unit, compared to only two gating units in the proposed hidden unit." Also p. 1726: "We were not able to get meaningful result with an oft-used tanh unit without any gating." The update (Eq. 7, p. 1726): new value = z x old value + (1 - z) x candidate value (per unit).
- Name: the words "GRU" and "gated recurrent" do not occur in `cho2014learning`, `cho2014properties`, or `bahdanau2015neural` (checked by search; they say "gated hidden unit"). `chung2014empirical` p. 2: "another type of recurrent unit, to which we refer as a gated recurrent unit (GRU), was proposed by Cho et al. [2014]." Comparison, `chung2014empirical` abstract (p. 1): "we found GRU to be comparable to LSTM"; conclusion (p. 7): "The evaluation clearly demonstrated the superiority of the gated units; both the LSTM unit and GRU, over the traditional tanh unit. ... However, we could not make concrete conclusion on which of the two gating units was better." (tasks: polyphonic music and raw speech, not translation).
- Note: credit the unit to Cho et al. 2014 and the name to Chung et al. (December 2014). Cho et al.'s "four gating units" for the LSTM counts the input transformation; the usual count of LSTM gates is three (input, forget, output), as in `structure`. Write "two gates instead of the LSTM's three, and no separate memory cell" and attribute the count to the chapter's own comparison. ### Q9. Sutskever, Vinyals, and Le (2014): sequence to sequence with deep LSTMs
- Source: `sutskever2014sequence`
- Location: pp. 1-6, 8.
- Quotes: Abstract (p. 1): "Our method uses a multilayered Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) to map the input sequence to a vector of a fixed dimensionality, and then another deep LSTM to decode the target sequence from the vector. Our main result is that on an English to French translation task from the WMT-14 dataset, the translations produced by the LSTM achieve a BLEU score of 34.8 on the entire test set, where the LSTM's BLEU score was penalized on out-of-vocabulary words. Additionally, the LSTM did not have difficulty on long sentences. For comparison, a phrase-based SMT system achieves a BLEU score of 33.3 on the same dataset. When we used the LSTM to rerank the 1000 hypotheses produced by the aforementioned SMT system, its BLEU score increases to 36.5, which is close to the previous state of the art." Motivation (p. 1): "DNNs can only be applied to problems whose inputs and targets can be sensibly encoded with vectors of fixed dimensionality." p. 2: "The second LSTM is essentially a recurrent neural network language model [28, 23, 30] except that it is conditioned on the input sequence" ([23] is Mikolov et al. 2010). End symbol (p. 3): "we require that each sentence ends with a special end-of-sentence symbol "<EOS>", which enables the model to define a distribution over sequences of all possible lengths." - Numbers. Table 1 (p. 6, test BLEU, ntst14): Bahdanau et al. ### Q13. Bahdanau, Cho, and Bengio: the bottleneck argument and the mechanism
- Source: `bahdanau2015neural`
- Location: pp. 1-4, 12-14.
- Bottleneck (abstract, p. 1): "The models proposed recently for neural machine translation often belong to a family of encoder-decoders and encode a source sentence into a fixed-length vector from which a decoder generates a translation. In this paper, we conjecture that the use of a fixed-length vector is a bottleneck in improving the performance of this basic encoder-decoder architecture, and propose to extend this by allowing a model to automatically (soft-)search for parts of a source sentence that are relevant to predicting a target word, without having to form these parts as a hard segment explicitly." p. 1: "A potential issue with this encoder-decoder approach is that a neural network needs to be able to compress all the necessary information of a source sentence into a fixed-length vector. This may make it difficult for the neural network to cope with long sentences, especially those that are longer than the sentences in the training corpus. Cho et al. (2014b) showed that indeed the performance of a basic encoder-decoder deteriorates rapidly as the length of an input sentence increases."
- The fix (p. 2): "it does not attempt to encode a whole input sentence into a single fixed-length vector. Instead, it encodes the input sentence into a sequence of vectors and chooses a subset of these vectors adaptively while decoding the translation. This frees a neural translation model from having to squash all the information of a source sentence, regardless of its length, into a fixed-length vector."
- Mechanism (p. 3): "The context vector ci is, then, computed as a weighted sum of these annotations hi" (Eq. 5); the weights by a softmax over scores (Eq. 6); the score eij = a(si-1, hj) "is an alignment model which scores how well the inputs around position j and the output at position i match. The score is based on the RNN hidden state si-1 (just before emitting yi, Eq. (4)) and the j-th annotation hj of the input sentence." "We parametrize the alignment model a as a feedforward neural network which is jointly trained with all the other components of the proposed system." p. 4: "Note that unlike in traditional machine translation, the alignment is not considered to be a latent variable. Instead, the alignment model directly computes a soft alignment, which allows the gradient of the cost function to be backpropagated through." "We can understand the approach of taking a weighted sum of all the annotations as computing an expected annotation, where the expectation is over possible alignments." "Intuitively, this implements a mechanism of attention in the decoder. The decoder decides parts of the source sentence to pay attention to. By letting the decoder have an attention mechanism, we relieve the encoder from the burden of having to encode all information in the source sentence into a fixed-length vector."
- Encoder (p. 4): a bidirectional RNN, so "the annotation hj contains the summaries of both the preceding words and the following words. Due to the tendency of RNNs to better represent recent inputs, the annotation hj will be focused on the words around xj."
- The scoring network (appendix A.1.2, p. 12): "a(si-1, hj) = va^T tanh(Wa si-1 + Ua hj)", "a single-layer multilayer perceptron"; "the model needs to be evaluated Tx x Ty times for each sentence pair of lengths Tx and Ty". Appendix A.2.2 (p. 14): "Note that the model becomes RNN Encoder-Decoder (Cho et al., 2014a), if we fix ci to" the forward encoder's last state (the arrow over h is lost in extraction; read as a right arrow). In plain words: attention with all the weight on one fixed summary is the old model.
- Note: the paper's own vocabulary is "align" and "(soft-)search"; the word "attention" occurs three times, all in one passage on p. 4 (corrected by the independent check). The chapter can call the scores "how well this source word matches what I am about to write", the weights "how much to look at each word", and the context "a weighted average of the source word vectors". The softmax turns scores into positive weights that sum to one (the reader knows it from multinomial logistic regression, if `toolkit` or `prediction` has not already introduced it). For the toy example the drafter may use a plain dot product as the score (Luong et al.'s "dot" variant, Q17) and must say that Bahdanau et al. used a small learned network instead.

### Q21. Why recurrence is slow to train
- Sources: `vaswani2017attention`, `sutskever2014sequence`, `wu2016google`, `mikolov2010recurrent`.
- Quotes: Vaswani et al., p. 2: "Recurrent models typically factor computation along the symbol positions of the input and output sequences. Generating output is word by word for any model, including later transformers; the training-time difference is the point. Do not say "transformers generate in parallel".

"In all but a few cases [27], however, such attention mechanisms are used in conjunction with a recurrent network." ### Q23. Graves (2013): a soft window that learns where to look
- Source: `graves2013generating`
- Location: pp. 24-26, 28.
- Quotes: p. 24: "The main challenge in conditioning the predictions on the text is that the two sequences are of very different lengths (the pen trace being on average twenty five times as long as the text), and the alignment between them is unknown until the data is generated." "This work proposes an alternative model, where a 'soft window' is convolved with the text string and fed in as an extra input to the prediction network." p. 26: "it dynamically determines an alignment between the text and the pen locations. Put simply, it learns to decide which character to write next." The window weights are a mixture of Gaussians over character positions (Eq. 46-47, p. 26), and the window location "κt = κt-1 + exp(κ̂t)" moves only forward: "this means that network learns how far to slide each window at each step, rather than an absolute location. Using offsets was essential to getting the network to align the text with the pen trace." (p. 26). Fig. 13 (p. 28): "Window weights during a handwriting synthesis sequence ... The bright line is the alignment chosen by the network between the characters and the writing."
- Note: a learned, differentiable weighting over input positions, a year before Bahdanau et al., for handwriting. It can only move forward (monotonic). See D2.

## Disputed or inconsistent points

### D1. Who first built a neural encoder-decoder for translation
Present the strands; do not crown one.
- **Earlier attempts (1990s):** Koehn, p. 5: "the models proposed by Forcada and Ñeco (1997) and Castaño et al. (1997) are striking similar to the current dominant neural machine translation approaches. However, none of these models were trained on data sizes large enough to produce reasonable results for anything but toy examples. The computational complexity involved by far exceeded the computational resources of that era, and hence the idea was abandoned for almost two decades." Bahdanau et al. cite Forcada and Ñeco (1997) among neural MT papers (p. 2). Wu et al. (p. 2): "The concept of end-to-end learning for machine translation has been attempted in the past (e.g., [8]) with limited success" ([8] Chrisman 1991). None of these was fetched.
- **Kalchbrenner and Blunsom (2013):** Sutskever et al. (p. 2) call them "the first to map the entire input sentence to vector", and (p. 8) "the first to map the input sentence into a vector and then back to a sentence". Cho et al. 2014b (p. 103) credit neural MT to "(Kalchbrenner and Blunsom, 2013; Sutskever et al., 2014)". Their encoder was convolutional, and they rescored rather than generated (Q6).
- **Cho et al. and Sutskever et al. (2014):** Goodfellow et al. (pp. 390, 392): "first proposed by Cho et al. (2014a) and shortly after by Sutskever et al. (2014), who independently developed that architecture". arXiv dates: Cho et al. 3 June 2014; Sutskever et al. 10 September 2014. Cho et al.'s network scored phrase pairs inside Moses; Sutskever et al.'s translated on its own.
- **Suggested wording:** "The idea of an encoder network and a decoder network had been tried in the 1990s on toy problems. In 2013 Kalchbrenner and Blunsom built one with a convolutional encoder; in 2014 Cho and colleagues, and independently Sutskever, Vinyals, and Le, built recurrent ones, and Sutskever's group was the first to make the network translate on its own better than a phrase-based baseline."

### D2. Credit for attention
- **Bahdanau, Cho, Bengio (2014-2015):** the mechanism as used in translation (Q13). Their own terms are "align" and "soft-search"; "attention" appears twice, as an intuition (p. 4).
- **Graves (2013):** a differentiable soft window over the input for handwriting (Q23). Sutskever et al. (p. 2): "Graves [10] introduced a novel differentiable attention mechanism that allows neural networks to focus on different parts of their input, and an elegant variant of this idea was successfully applied to machine translation by Bahdanau et al. [2]." Bahdanau et al. (p. 8) acknowledge it and draw the line: "A similar approach of aligning an output symbol with an input symbol was proposed recently by Graves (2013) in the context of handwriting synthesis. ... his alignment was restricted to predict the location such that the location increases monotonically. The main difference from our approach is that, in (Graves, 2013), the modes of the weights of the annotations only move in one direction. In the context of machine translation, this is a severe limitation, as (long-distance) reordering is often needed to generate a grammatically correct translation (for instance, English-to-German)."
- **Older "attention" in neural networks:** `schmidhuber2014deep` p. 34 refers to "previous work since 1990 on NNs that learn selective attention through RL of (a) motor actions such as saccade control (Sec. 6.1) and (b) internal actions controlling spotlights of attention within RNNs". This is attention in a different sense (learned by reinforcement, mostly for vision); it is not the soft weighted average of this chapter. Mention only if the chapter discusses the word's history.
- **Timing:** Bahdanau et al.'s arXiv v1 (1 September 2014) predates Sutskever et al.'s v1 (10 September 2014) by nine days, and Sutskever et al.'s NeurIPS version already compares with it (Table 1, 28.45). So attention did not arrive "after" seq2seq in time; the two were simultaneous, from neighbouring groups.
- **Not used:** informal accounts of who proposed the word "attention" (see Not fetched).
- Suggested wording: "Bahdanau, Cho, and Bengio introduced attention to translation in 2014. Graves had used a similar learned, soft way of choosing where to look in 2013, for handwriting, but his could only move forward through the input; translation needs to jump back and forth."

### D3. Did fixed-vector models fail on long sentences?
- **Yes:** `cho2014properties` (Q12): degradation "rapidly" with length; `bahdanau2015neural` (Q14): RNNencdec "dramatically drops"; `koehn2017neural` p. 6: they "fell apart with increasing sentence length".
- **Not much:** `sutskever2014sequence` (Q11): "no degradation on sentences with less than 35 words", "only a minor degradation on the longest sentences", credited to reversing the source.
- **Facts that bear on it:** Cho et al. and Bahdanau et al. used gated RNNs with 1000 hidden units, a 30,000-word vocabulary, trained 4-5 days on sentences up to 30 or 50 words (no layer or GPU count is given in Cho et al. 2014b; corrected by the independent check); Sutskever et al. used 4-layer LSTMs, 160k/80k vocabularies, ensembles of five 380M-parameter models, about ten days on 8 GPUs, reversed input. Cho et al. 2014b themselves wonder whether the decoder is the problem (p. 110).
- Suggested wording: "Whether one vector was enough depended on how big the model was and how it was trained. Small single-layer models degraded badly on long sentences; Sutskever's much larger, reversed-input LSTMs held up. Attention removed the question: the decoder no longer depends on one vector at all."

## Mechanism claims to check numerically at drafting [D-009]

1. **Source arithmetic.** Mikolov et al. Table 2: (221 - 173) / 221 = 21.7% (single RNN alone); (221 - 155) / 221 = 29.9% (single RNN + KN5); (221 - 121) / 221 = 45.2% (3 dynamic RNNs + KN5); (221 - 112) / 221 = 49.3% (best mix); WER (13.5 - 11.1) / 13.5 = 17.8%. Sutskever et al.: 34.81 - 33.30 = 1.51 BLEU; 36.5 - 33.30 = 3.2; 37.0 - 36.5 = 0.5; reversal +4.7 BLEU (25.9 to 30.6). Cho et al.: +0.57 and +1.34 BLEU. Wu et al.: 96 GPUs x 6 days = 576 GPU-days.
2. **The bottleneck in sizes.** Sutskever et al.'s decoder starts from one fixed vector whatever the sentence length (their v, the last hidden state; 1000 cells per layer). Bahdanau et al. keep one annotation per source word of 2 x 1000 = 2000 numbers (forward and backward states concatenated), so a 10-word sentence keeps 20,000 numbers, 30 words 60,000, 50 words 100,000, while the fixed vector stays at 1000 (one layer) for all. Print a small table; label the 1000 as "one layer's state", since Sutskever et al. have four layers and the paper says only "the last hidden state".
3. **Where the parameters are (reconstruction, label it so).** For Sutskever et al.'s model, assuming each LSTM layer has four weight blocks reading a 1000-number input and a 1000-number previous state, and ignoring biases: 4 x 1000 x (1000 + 1000) = 8M per layer; 4 layers = 32M per LSTM; 64M for encoder plus decoder, which matches the paper's "64M are pure recurrent connections (32M ...)". Add input embeddings 160,000 x 1000 = 160M, decoder embeddings 80,000 x 1000 = 80M, output softmax 80,000 x 1000 = 80M: total 384M, close to the printed 380M. About 83% of the parameters are word tables. The paper does not give this breakdown; the softmax split "1000 x 20000" per GPU (p. 5) is 80,000 / 4, consistent with it. This is a good bridge to `tokens` (vocabulary size drives cost).
4. **A toy attention step (the key worked example).** Four source words, each with a small vector (2 or 3 numbers), and one decoder state. Scores = dot products (Luong et al.'s "dot", Q17; say that Bahdanau et al. used a small network). Weights = softmax of the scores: check they are positive and sum to 1. Context = weighted average of the four source vectors. Show that changing the decoder state moves the weights to a different word. Suggested sentence: English "the European economic area" (or a 4-word piece of Fig. 3a) and French "la zone économique européenne", with vectors chosen so the weight for "zone" peaks on "area" and the weights for "économique" and "européenne" step back one word at a time (the published behaviour, Q15). The vectors are constructed, not learned: say so in the text. Optional: a temperature-free illustration that larger score gaps give sharper weights.
5. **Reversal keeps the average distance and shrinks the minimum (checked).** Lay out source + end symbol + target as one sequence. With n source words in normal order, every source word is n + 1 steps from its translation (if word i translates to word i). Reversed, the distances are 2, 4, ..., 2n: the average stays n + 1, the minimum drops to 2. For n = 3: normal 4, 4, 4; reversed 2, 4, 6. For n = 20: average 21 both ways, minimum 21 vs 2. This reproduces Sutskever et al.'s sentence on p. 4 exactly.
6. **What "60% fewer errors" means (checked).** For each row of Wu et al.'s Table 10, (GNMT - PBMT) / (Human - PBMT): English-Spanish 0.543 / 0.619 = 87.7%; English-French 64.4%; English-Chinese 58.7%; Spanish-English 63.0%; French-English 83.0%; Chinese-English 60.4%. The printed values are these, truncated (87, 64, 58, 63, 83, 60). Mean of the printed six: 69.2%.
7. **Sequential steps.** A one-layer RNN over an n-word sentence needs n steps one after the other; a layer that processes all positions at once needs 1 (Vaswani et al. Table 1: O(n) vs O(1)). With L stacked RNN layers placed one per GPU and pipelined (Sutskever et al., Wu et al.), the last layer finishes after n + L - 1 steps rather than n x L. For n = 30, L = 4: 33 pipelined steps vs 120 unpipelined vs 4 for four fully parallel layers. Training-time count only; generation is word by word in every model (Q21 note).
8. **Attention cost.** Bahdanau et al.'s scores per sentence pair = Tx x Ty: 15 x 15 = 225, 30 x 30 = 900, 50 x 50 = 2,500. Quadratic growth, to be picked up in `attention`.
9. **Optional BLEU toy (only if the chapter shows BLEU at work).** Papineni et al.'s Example 2: candidate of seven "the" against the two references: plain unigram precision 7/7, clipped ("modified") 2/7 = 0.286. The brevity penalty e^(1 - r/c) for c = 12, r = 15: e^(-0.25) = 0.78.

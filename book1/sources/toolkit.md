## Sourced claims

### K1. Hyperparameters and the validation set: the reader's own tools, by the textbook's definition
- Source: `goodfellow2016deep`
- Location: ch. 5, pp. 118-119; ch. 11, pp. 424-425.
- Quotes: "Most machine learning algorithms have hyperparameters, settings that we can use to control the algorithm's behavior. The values of hyperparameters are not adapted by the learning algorithm itself" (p. 118). "The λ value used to control the strength of weight decay is another example of a hyperparameter" (p. 118, sentence continues past the fetched excerpt; the opening words were located). "The subset of data used to guide the selection of hyperparameters is called the validation set. Typically, one uses about 80 percent of the training data for training and 20 percent for validation." (p. 119). "The learning rate is perhaps the most important hyperparameter. If you have time to tune only one hyperparameter, tune the learning rate." (p. 424). "When the learning rate is too large, gradient descent can inadvertently increase rather than decrease the training error. In the idealized quadratic case, this occurs if the learning rate is at least twice as large as its optimal value (LeCun et al., 1998a)." (p. 424). Figure 11.1 caption: "Typical relationship between the learning rate and the training error. Notice the sharp rise in error when the learning is above an optimal value." (p. 425).
- Note: the bridge. Validation sets and hyperparameter search are unchanged from the reader's era. "Weight decay" is the network name for L2 regularization (ridge); say so at first use. The "twice the optimal value" rule is the same divergence the `backprop` chapter showed with learning rate 0.25.

### K2. Early stopping is regularization, and the most used form
- Source: `goodfellow2016deep`; `srivastava2014dropout`
- Location: Goodfellow ch. 7, pp. 243, 247; Srivastava p. 1929.
- Quotes: "Every time the error on the validation set improves, we store a copy of the model parameters. When the training algorithm terminates, we return these parameters, rather than the latest parameters." "This strategy is known as early stopping. It is probably the most commonly used form of regularization in deep learning." "In this view, the number of training steps is just another hyperparameter." (p. 243). Figure 7.3 caption: "the training objective decreases consistently over time, but the validation set average loss eventually begins to increase again, forming an asymmetric U-shaped curve." (p. 243). "Indeed, we can show how, in the case of a simple linear model with a quadratic error function and simple gradient descent, early stopping is equivalent to L2 regularization." (p. 247; the source prints em-dashes around "in the case of ... gradient descent"). Srivastava et al. list the standard remedies for overfitting: "stopping the training as soon as performance on a validation set starts to get worse, introducing weight penalties of various kinds such as L1 and L2 regularization and soft weight sharing" (p. 1929).
- Note: the cleanest bridge in the chapter. Early stopping and L2 are the reader's tools and are still the baseline.

### K4. Minibatches: a noisy but cheap estimate of the gradient
- Sources: `goodfellow2016deep`; `bottou2010large`; `rumelhart1986learning`
- Location: Goodfellow ch. 8, pp. 275-276, 290; Bottou p. 2 (author's version); Rumelhart et al. p. 535.
- Quotes: "Recall that the standard error of the mean (equation 5.46) estimated from n samples is given by σ/√n ... Compare two hypothetical estimates of the gradient, one based on 100 examples and another based on 10,000 examples. The latter requires 100 times more computation than the former but reduces the standard error of the mean only by a factor of 10." (p. 275). Optimizers using more than one but fewer than all examples "were traditionally called minibatch or minibatch stochastic methods, and it is now common to call them simply stochastic methods." (p. 276). Batch-size factors (p. 276): "Larger batches provide a more accurate estimate of the gradient, but with less than linear returns"; memory "scales with the batch size"; "Especially when using GPUs, it is common for power of 2 batch sizes to offer better runtime. Typical power of 2 batch sizes range from 32 to 256, with 16 sometimes being attempted for large models."; "Small batches can offer a regularizing effect (Wilson and Martinez, 2003), perhaps due to the noise they add to the learning process. Generalization error is often best for a batch size of 1." SGD's noise: "the SGD gradient estimator introduces a source of noise (the random sampling of m training examples) that does not vanish even when we arrive at a minimum." (p. 290). Bottou: "The stochastic gradient descent (SGD) algorithm is a drastic simplification. Instead of computing the gradient of En(fw) exactly, each iteration estimates this gradient on the basis of a single randomly picked example" (p. 2). Rumelhart et al., 1986: "One way of using ∂E/∂w is to change the weights after every input-output case. This has the advantage that no separate memory is required for the derivatives. An alternative scheme, which we used in the research reported here, is to accumulate ∂E/∂w over all the input-output cases before changing the weights." (p. 535; OCR'd, symbols restored).
- Note: both extremes (one case at a time, and the whole set) were already in the 1986 letter; the minibatch is the compromise. Vocabulary trap: "SGD" now usually means minibatch SGD (Goodfellow p. 276). Also: "batch" in "batch gradient descent" means the whole training set, but "batch size" means the minibatch size. Goodfellow p. 275: "This terminology can be somewhat confusing because the word "batch" is also often used to describe the minibatch used by minibatch stochastic gradient descent. Typically the term "batch gradient descent" implies the use of the full training set, while the use of the term "batch" to describe a group of examples does not. For example, it is common to use the term "batch size" to describe the size of a minibatch."

### K5. Where SGD comes from: Robbins and Monro, 1951
- Sources: `robbins1951stochastic`; `bottou2018optimization`; `bottou2010large`
- Location: Robbins and Monro p. 400; Bottou et al. pp. 4, 16; Bottou p. 3.
- Quotes: Robbins and Monro, Summary (p. 400; read on the page image): "Let M(x) denote the expected value at level x of the response to a certain experiment. M(x) is assumed to be a monotone function of x but is unknown to the experimenter, and it is desired to find the solution x = θ of the equation M(x) = α, where α is a given constant. We give a method for making successive experiments at levels x1, x2, ... in such a way that xn will tend to θ in probability." Bottou, Curtis, Nocedal: "the stochastic gradient method (SG) proposed by Robbins and Monro [130]" (p. 4); "The paper by Robbins and Monro [130] represents a landmark in the history of numerical optimization methods. ... The SG method was first proposed in [130], not as a gradient method, but as a Markov chain." (p. 16, Inset 3.1). Bottou 2010: "Convergence results usually require decreasing gains satisfying the conditions Σt γt² < ∞ and Σt γt = ∞." (p. 3). The same two conditions appear in Goodfellow as "A sufficient condition to guarantee convergence of SGD" (p. 291, eqs. 8.12-8.13).
- Note: a statistician's bridge: stochastic approximation is a 1951 statistics paper. The decreasing-step conditions explain why schedules decay the learning rate (K12). The text layer of the Robbins and Monro copy is a poor OCR; the summary was read on the page image (p. 400), including the symbols θ and α.

### K7. Momentum: a velocity, not a position (1964 and 1986)
- Sources: `rumelhart1986learning`; `rumelhart1986internal`; `sutskever2013importance`; `goodfellow2016deep`
- Location: Rumelhart et al. 1986a p. 535; 1986b p. 330; Sutskever et al. pp. 1139-1140; Goodfellow pp. 292-294.
- Quotes: Nature letter: the method "can be significantly improved, without sacrificing the simplicity and locality, by using an acceleration method in which the current gradient is used to modify the velocity of the point in weight space instead of its position", with "α ... an exponential decay factor between 0 and 1 that determines the relative contribution of the current gradient and earlier gradients to the weight change." (p. 535). PDP chapter: momentum "effectively filters out high-frequency variations of the error-surface in the weight space. This is useful in spaces containing long ravines that are characterized by sharp curvature across the ravine and a gently sloping floor. The sharp curvature tends to cause divergent oscillations across the ravine. To prevent these it is necessary to take very small steps, but this causes very slow progress along the ravine. The momentum filters out the high curvature and thus allows the effective weight steps to be bigger. In most of our simulations α was about 0.9." (p. 330; OCR spacing repaired). Sutskever et al.: "The momentum method (Polyak, 1964), which we refer to as classical momentum (CM), is a technique for accelerating gradient descent that accumulates a velocity vector in directions of persistent reduction in the objective across iterations." (p. 1140). Abstract: networks "that were considered to be almost impossible to train using stochastic gradient descent with momentum" can be trained when SGD "uses a well-designed random initialization and a particular type of slowly increasing schedule for the momentum parameter"; "both the initialization and the momentum are crucial" (p. 1139). Goodfellow: "The method of momentum (Polyak, 1964) is designed to accelerate learning, especially in the face of high curvature, small but consistent gradients, or noisy gradients." (p. 292). "It is thus helpful to think of the momentum hyperparameter in terms of 1/(1-α). For example, α = 0.9 corresponds to multiplying the maximum speed by 10 relative to the gradient descent algorithm. Common values of α used in practice include 0.5, 0.9, and 0.99." (p. 294). Figure 8.5 caption: momentum addresses "poor conditioning of the Hessian matrix and variance in the stochastic gradient" (p. 293).
- Note: the PDP "ravine" passage is the chapter's narrow-valley example in the 1986 authors' own words. See D2 for who gets credit. Sutskever et al. also give a convergence statement attributed to Polyak; see D7 before using it.

### K8. RMSProp: introduced in lecture slides, not a paper
- Source: `hinton2012lecture6`
- Location: slides 15, 26, 29.
- Quotes: slide 15: "rmsprop: Divide the learning rate for a weight by a running average of the magnitudes of recent gradients for that weight." Slide 29: "rmsprop: Keep a moving average of the squared gradient for each weight MeanSquare(w, t) = 0.9 MeanSquare(w, t-1) + 0.1 (∂E/∂w(t))²"; "Dividing the gradient by sqrt(MeanSquare(w, t)) makes the learning work much better (Tijmen Tieleman, unpublished)." Slide 28: rprop "would increment the weight nine times and decrement it once by about the same amount" for a weight that "gets a gradient of +0.1 on nine mini-batches and a gradient of -0.9 on the tenth" (the slide's motivation for averaging).
- Note: the per-weight step size is the idea Adam inherits. Cite as Tieleman and Hinton (2012) only if the chapter explains that this is how others cite unpublished lecture slides.

### K9. Adam (Kingma and Ba, 2014; ICLR 2015)
- Source: `kingma2015adam` (v9); `goodfellow2016deep`
- Location: v9 pp. 1-3, 5; v1 p. 2; Goodfellow p. 305.
- Quotes: "We introduce Adam, an algorithm for first-order gradient-based optimization of stochastic objective functions, based on adaptive estimates of lower-order moments." (p. 1). "The method computes individual adaptive learning rates for different parameters from estimates of first and second moments of the gradients; the name Adam is derived from adaptive moment estimation. Our method is designed to combine the advantages of two recently popular methods: AdaGrad (Duchi et al., 2011), which works well with sparse gradients, and RMSProp (Tieleman & Hinton, 2012), which works well in on-line and non-stationary settings" (p. 1). Algorithm 1 caption: "Good default settings for the tested machine learning problems are α = 0.001, β1 = 0.9, β2 = 0.999 and ε = 10^-8." (p. 2; checked on the page image). The moving averages "are initialized as (vectors of) 0's, leading to moment estimates that are biased towards zero, especially during the initial timesteps ... The good news is that this initialization bias can be easily counteracted, resulting in bias-corrected estimates" (p. 2). "The effective magnitude of the steps taken in parameter space at each timestep are approximately bounded by the stepsize setting α ... This can be understood as establishing a trust region around the current parameter value" (p. 3). Footnote on p. 1: "Equal contribution. Author ordering determined by coin flip over a Google Hangout." Goodfellow: "In the context of the earlier algorithms, it is perhaps best seen as a variant on the combination of RMSProp and momentum with a few important distinctions." (p. 305).
- Note: the "as printed" defaults depend on the version; see D6. Use v9 (the ICLR 2015 version) values. The magnitude bound (step about α regardless of gradient size) is Example 7's point.

### K11. Do adaptive methods generalize worse? (Wilson et al. 2017; Loshchilov and Hutter 2019)
- Sources: `wilson2017marginal`; `loshchilov2019decoupled`
- Location: Wilson p. 1; Loshchilov and Hutter p. 1.
- Quotes: Wilson et al.: "We observe that the solutions found by adaptive methods generalize worse (often significantly worse) than SGD, even when these solutions have better training performance. These results suggest that practitioners should reconsider the use of adaptive methods to train neural networks." (p. 1). Also: "Adam [8] in particular has become the default algorithm used across many deep learning frameworks." (p. 1; as of 2017). Loshchilov and Hutter: "L2 regularization and weight decay regularization are equivalent for standard stochastic gradient descent (when rescaled by the learning rate), but as we demonstrate this is not the case for adaptive gradient algorithms, such as Adam." ### K15. Initialization: from "small random" to scaled by layer size
- Sources: `goodfellow2016deep`; `glorot2010understanding`; `he2015delving`
- Location: Goodfellow pp. 297, 299; Glorot and Bengio pp. 249, 251, 253, 255; He et al. 2015 pp. 3-4.
- Quotes: Goodfellow: the initial parameters "need to "break symmetry" between different units" (p. 297). Glorot and Bengio, abstract: "Whereas before 2006 it appears that deep multi-layer neural networks were not successfully trained, since then several algorithms have been shown to successfully train them"; "Our objective here is to understand better why standard gradient descent from random initialization is doing so poorly with deep neural networks"; "We find that the logistic sigmoid activation is unsuited for deep networks with random initialization because of its mean value, which can drive especially the top hidden layer into saturation."; "Based on these considerations, we propose a new initialization scheme that brings substantially faster convergence." (p. 249). The old rule: "We initialized the biases to be 0 and the weights Wij at each layer with the following commonly used heuristic: Wij ∼ U[-1/√n, 1/√n] ... where U[-a, a] is the uniform distribution in the interval (-a, a) and n is the size of the previous layer" (p. 251). On p. 253 (read on the page image): to keep forward activations and backward gradients alike across layers, "∀i, ni Var[W^i] = 1" (eq. 10) and "∀i, ni+1 Var[W^i] = 1" (eq. 11); "As a compromise between these two constraints, we might want to have ∀i, Var[W^i] = 2/(ni + ni+1)" (eq. 12); "The standard initialization that we have used (eq. 1) gives rise to variance with the following property: nVar[W] = 1/3 (15) ... This will cause the variance of the back-propagated gradient to be dependent on the layer (and decreasing)."; "We call it the normalized initialization: W ∼ U[-√6/√(nj + nj+1), √6/√(nj + nj+1)] (16)". Conclusions: "The more classical neural networks with sigmoid or hyperbolic tangent units and standard initialization fare rather poorly, converging more slowly and apparently towards ultimately poorer local minima." (p. 255). Goodfellow: the Glorot heuristic "is designed to compromise between the goal of initializing all layers to have the same activation variance and the goal of initializing all layers to have the same gradient variance. The formula is derived using the assumption that the network consists only of a chain of matrix multiplications, with no nonlinearities." (p. 299). He et al. 2015: "Glorot and Bengio [7] proposed to adopt a properly scaled uniform distribution for initialization. This is called "Xavier" initialization in [14]. Its derivation is based on the assumption that the activations are linear. This assumption is invalid for ReLU and PReLU." "our initialization method allows for extremely deep models (e.g., 30 conv/fc layers) to converge, while the "Xavier" method [7] cannot." (p. 3). "This leads to a zero-mean Gaussian distribution whose standard deviation (std) is √(2/nl). This is our way of initialization." (p. 4, eq. 10).
- Note: in words: choose the spread of the random starting weights so that a signal neither shrinks nor grows as it passes through each layer, which means scaling it down as the number of inputs to a unit grows. Formula optional [D-002]; Example 9 shows the shrinking variance with the old rule. Names the reader will meet: "Xavier" or "Glorot" initialization, "He" or "Kaiming" initialization.

### K18. Dropout, journal version (Srivastava et al., JMLR 2014)
- Source: `srivastava2014dropout`
- Location: pp. 1929-1932, 1936-1937, 1950, 1952.
- Quotes: "The key idea is to randomly drop units (along with their connections) from the neural network during training. This prevents units from co-adapting too much. During training, dropout samples from an exponential number of different "thinned" networks. At test time, it is easy to approximate the effect of averaging the predictions of all these thinned networks by simply using a single unthinned network that has smaller weights." (p. 1929). "In the simplest case, each unit is retained with a fixed probability p independent of other units, where p can be chosen using a validation set or can simply be set at 0.5, which seems to be close to optimal for a wide range of networks and tasks. For the input units, however, the optimal probability of retention is usually closer to 1 than to 0.5." (p. 1930). "A neural net with n units, can be seen as a collection of 2^n possible thinned neural networks." (p. 1931; superscript lost in extraction). "If a unit is retained with probability p during training, the outgoing weights of that unit are multiplied by p at test time ... This ensures that for any hidden unit the expected output (under the distribution used to drop units at training time) is the same as the actual output at test time." (p. 1931). Motivation from evolution: "A motivation for dropout comes from a theory of the role of sex in evolution (Livnat et al., 2010)." (p. 1932). MNIST (Table 2, p. 1936): standard net (Simard et al. 2003) 1.60%; "SVM Gaussian kernel" 1.40%; dropout nets 1.35% (logistic), 1.25% (ReLU), down to 0.95% (2 layers of 8192 units, max-norm); 0.79% with DBM pretraining. Text (p. 1937): "Note that this network has more than 65 million parameters and is being trained on a data set of size 60,000. Training a network of this size to give good generalization error is very hard with standard regularization methods and early stopping. Dropout, on the other hand, prevents overfitting, even in this case. It does not even need early stopping." "All dropout nets use p = 0.5 for hidden units and p = 0.8 for input units." Linear regression (p. 1950): with inputs dropped, the expected objective "reduces to minimize ||y - pXw||² + p(1 - p)||Γw||²" with Γ = (diag(XᵀX))^½, so "dropout with linear regression is equivalent, in expectation, to ridge regression with a particular form for Γ." Cost (p. 1952): "One of the drawbacks of dropout is that it increases training time. A dropout network typically takes 2-3 times longer to train than a standard neural network of the same architecture." (the source prints an en-dash in "2-3").
- Note: the ridge-regression equivalence is the best bridge for this reader (Example 4b checks it numerically). p in this paper is the probability of keeping a unit; see K19 and D6.

### K24. Residual networks (He, Zhang, Ren, Sun, 2015)
- Source: `he2015deep`
- Location: pp. 1-4, 6-8.
- Quotes: "Deeper neural networks are more difficult to train. We present a residual learning framework to ease the training of networks that are substantially deeper than those used previously." Numerical differentiation (finite differences) has "the disadvantages of performing O(n) evaluations of f" for a gradient in n dimensions (p. 4). Reverse mode (p. 12): "In the first phase, the original function code is run forward, populating intermediate variables vi and recording the dependencies in the computational graph through a bookkeeping procedure. In the second phase, derivatives are calculated by propagating adjoints v̄i in reverse, from the outputs to the inputs." "In the extreme case of f : R^n → R, only one application of the reverse mode is sufficient to compute the full gradient ... compared with the n passes of the forward mode needed for populating the same. Because machine learning practice principally involves the gradient of a scalar-valued objective with respect to a large number of parameters, this establishes the reverse mode, as opposed to the forward mode, as the mainstay technique in the form of the backpropagation algorithm." Worked example used throughout (pp. 8, 10, 13): y = ln(x1) + x1x2 - sin(x2) at (x1, x2) = (2, 5). History (p. 14): "Prior to Werbos, the work by Linnainmaa (1970, 1976) is often cited as the first published description of the reverse mode. Speelpenning (1980) subsequently introduced reverse mode AD as we know it, in the sense that he gave the first implementation that was actually automatic". "the backpropagation algorithm is only a special case of AD" (p. 17). Goodfellow: "The back-propagation algorithm described here is only one approach to automatic differentiation. It is a special case of a broader class of techniques called reverse mode accumulation." (pp. 217-218).
- Note: the chapter keeps the `backprop` promise ("the reverse mode of automatic differentiation ... returns in the training toolkit"). The history is already in `backprop` (Linnainmaa via `griewank2012invented`); here only one back-reference is needed. The Baydin example is a good worked example (Example 12), computed at drafting.

## Disputed or inconsistent points

### D1. Why batch normalization works
Present both; do not pick a winner.
- **Original account:** `ioffe2015batch` (p. 448): the problem is "internal covariate shift", defined on p. 449 as "the change in the distribution of network activations due to the change in network parameters during training", and batch norm addresses it "by normalizing layer inputs". They also say it "regularizes the model" (p. 449).
- **Challenge:** `santurkar2018how` (pp. 1-3): the link to internal covariate shift is "tenuous, at best"; batch norm "might not even be reducing internal covariate shift"; injecting deliberate distribution shift after batch norm barely hurts; the effect they find is a "significantly smoother" optimization landscape, shared by other normalizations.
- **Textbook framing (2016):** `goodfellow2016deep` (p. 313) calls it "adaptive reparametrization" and explains it by the fact that all layers are updated at once while each gradient assumes the others stay fixed.
- Suggested wording: "Its inventors explained it as a cure for a shifting distribution of inputs to each layer. Later experiments cast doubt on that explanation and pointed to a smoother error surface instead. That it works is not in dispute; why is still argued." Date-stamp "still argued" (as of the 2018 paper; nothing later was fetched).

### D2. Who originated momentum
Not a quarrel between the parties, but a question the chapter must not answer silently.
- **Optimization:** `sutskever2013importance` (p. 1140) and `goodfellow2016deep` (p. 292) attribute the method to Polyak (1964). Polyak was not fetched (see "Not fetched").
- **Neural networks:** `rumelhart1986learning` (p. 535) and `rumelhart1986internal` (p. 330) use it, call it "an acceleration method" and "a momentum term", and do not cite Polyak (the PDP chapter's text was searched for "Polyak": no hit; the Nature letter's references were not searched).
- Suggested wording: "The idea came from numerical optimization (Boris Polyak, 1964, as later authors credit it) and was used for backpropagation in the 1986 papers." Do not imply the 1986 authors took it from Polyak; nothing fetched says so.

### D3. Adam: convergence and generalization
- **Convergence:** `kingma2015adam` gives a convergence theorem (Theorem 4.1; online convex setting). `reddi2018convergence` (pp. 1, 4, 5, 16) shows a convex counterexample and says the original proof "erroneously assumes" a matrix is positive semi-definite. Kingma and Ba's v9 (30 Jan 2017) predates Reddi et al.; whether a later correction exists was not checked.
- **Generalization:** `wilson2017marginal` (p. 1): adaptive methods "generalize worse (often significantly worse) than SGD" on the models they tested. `loshchilov2019decoupled` (p. 1): much of the gap came from how weight decay was implemented; with decoupling, Adam can "compete with SGD with momentum".
- **Practice (date-stamped):** the 2023 and 2024 LLaMA recipes use AdamW (`touvron2023llama`, `grattafiori2024llama`).
- Suggested wording: "Adam's original convergence proof turned out to be flawed, and some experiments found it generalized worse than plain SGD with momentum. A modified version, AdamW, is what the large language models of 2023-2024 were trained with."

### D4. Who gets credit for residual connections
- **He et al. (2015):** call highway networks "concurrent with our work" and distinguish their parameter-free identity shortcuts that are "never closed" from gated shortcuts (`he2015deep` p. 2). They also cite earlier "shortcut connections" (p. 2).
- **Schmidhuber (2022):** ResNet "is a version (with open gates) of our earlier Highway Net (May 2015)", and highway nets trace to LSTM (`schmidhuber2022annotated` PDF pp. 12, 20).
- **Facts:** highway networks were first posted in May 2015 (arXiv 1505.00387) and presented at an ICML 2015 workshop; ResNet was posted 10 Dec 2015 (`he2015deep` p. 1). Highway nets trained up to 900 layers in preliminary experiments (`srivastava2015highway` PDF p. 1). ResNet won ILSVRC 2015 (`he2015deep` p. 1).
- Suggested wording: "Highway networks, from Jürgen Schmidhuber's group earlier in 2015, had already trained very deep networks with gated shortcuts. ResNet's shortcuts have no gate at all; its authors call highway networks concurrent work, and Schmidhuber calls ResNet a highway network with its gates held open." This mirrors D1 and D2 in `structure`; the chapter should not relitigate the LSTM lineage beyond one clause.

### D5. Where warmup came from
A chronology rather than a dispute; do not name an inventor.
- `he2015deep` (posted Dec 2015, p. 7): a constant warmup of 0.01 for about 400 iterations on a 110-layer CIFAR network.
- `goyal2017accurate` (June 2017, p. 3): credits the warmup idea to He et al. ("a properly designed warmup [16]"), shows that constant warmup fails for very large minibatches, and proposes gradual (linear) warmup.
- `vaswani2017attention` (June 2017, p. 7): linear warmup over 4,000 steps, then inverse-square-root decay, with Adam.
- Earlier uses may exist; none were searched for. Suggested wording: "By 2017 a short warmup had become standard, both for very large minibatches and for the transformer."

### D6. Two inconsistencies in how numbers are printed
- **Dropout's p:** `srivastava2014dropout` (p. 1930) uses p for the probability of keeping a unit (0.5 hidden, 0.8 input). AlexNet (per `book1/sources/deep-turn.md` T16, not re-fetched) describes zeroing "with probability 0.5", the drop convention. `pytorch2024dropout` uses p for the probability of zeroing; `vaswani2017attention` (p. 8) writes Pdrop = 0.1. The same "p = 0.8" means opposite things in the two conventions.
- **Adam's defaults:** v1 of `kingma2015adam` (22 Dec 2014, p. 2) prints "α = 0.0002, β1 = 0.1, β2 = 0.001", with the decay rates parametrized the other way round (mt ← β1 · gt + (1 - β1) · mt-1). v9 (the ICLR 2015 version) prints α = 0.001, β1 = 0.9, β2 = 0.999, ε = 10^-8. v1's β1 = 0.1 is the same averaging as v9's β1 = 0.9; only α differs. Cite v9.

### D7. A momentum constant in Sutskever et al. (2013), unresolved
`sutskever2013importance` (p. 1140) says Polyak showed classical momentum needs "√R times fewer iterations than steepest descent ... where R is the condition number of the curvature at the minimum and µ is set to (√R - 1)/(√R + 1)" (read on the page image of p. 1140). The heavy-ball tuning usually given in optimization texts is, from model memory and **not verified here**, the square of that value, with a matching learning rate. A quick numeric check in this session on f = ½(x² + 100y²) (R = 100) from (10, 1): plain gradient descent at its best learning rate (2/101) needs 461 steps to bring f below 10^-6; momentum 0.818 needs 86-93 steps over learning rates 0.01-0.033; momentum 0.669 with learning rate 0.0331 needs 69. Both are large speed-ups; the √R = 10 figure is asymptotic. Recommendation: do not print the formula or the √R claim. Use only "momentum can cut the number of steps severalfold in a narrow valley", with the chapter's own computed numbers.

### D8. Small source details
- `touvron2023llama` (PDF p. 3) says they "vary the learning rate and batch size with the size of the model", but Table 2 lists a 4M batch for all four sizes, and the Figure 1 caption says "All models are trained with a batch size of 4M tokens." Use the table.
- Touvron et al. Figure 1 caption reads "65 models" (missing "B") as printed.
- `ioffe2015batch` reports 4.82% (test server) and 4.9% (validation) for the same ensemble; keep the two apart.
- `ba2016layer` p. 2 typo "normaliztion"; quote as printed or paraphrase.

## Mechanism claims to check numerically at drafting [D-009]

Suggested script: `code/checks/toolkit.py` (standard library only, matching the existing checks), imported by `figures/toolkit.py` [D-014]. All values below were computed once in this session by a scratch script (standard library, `random.Random` with fixed seeds); seeded values will differ if the script's sampling order differs, so the drafted text must use the repo script's output, not these.

1. **Tensor shapes.** A minibatch of 4 examples with 3 features is a 4 x 3 array; times a 3 x 2 weight matrix gives 4 x 2 (12 inputs, 6 weights). A batch of 32 sequences of 128 tokens, each a 512-number vector: shape (32, 128, 512) = 2,097,152 numbers; through a 512 x 2,048 weight matrix (1,048,576 weights): (32, 128, 2,048) = 8,388,608 numbers. ResNet's minibatch of 256 color images at 224 x 224 (K24, K30): (256, 3, 224, 224) = 38,535,168 numbers. Point: the weight count does not depend on the batch size; the activations do.
2. **Minibatch gradient noise.** Toy regression y = 2x + noise (x uniform on [0, 2], noise sd 1, 1,000 points), loss = mean squared error, gradient at w = 0. Full gradient -5.25. Over 2,000 random minibatches: size 1, sd 5.18; size 10, sd 1.68; size 100, sd 0.51; per-example sd 5.28, and 5.28/√10 = 1.67, 5.28/√100 = 0.53. Means all close to -5.25 (unbiased). Matches Goodfellow's σ/√n (K4): 100 times the examples, a tenth of the noise.
3. **Momentum in a narrow valley.** f(x, y) = ½(x² + 100y²) from (10, 1), stop when f < 10^-6 (start f = 100). Plain gradient descent: learning rate 0.01, 882 steps; 0.019, 463 steps; 0.021 diverges (y goes 1, -1.1, 1.21, -1.331, ...: oscillation across the ravine, the PDP chapter's "divergent oscillations", K7). With momentum 0.9 and learning rate 0.01: 162 steps; momentum 0.5: 431 steps. Terminal-velocity factor 1/(1 - 0.9) = 10 and 1/(1 - 0.99) = 100 (Goodfellow p. 294). See D7 before using any optimal-constant claim.
4. **Dropout's expected value.** (a) A unit outputs 2.0. Keep probability 0.5 (Srivastava's convention): during training the output is 0 or 2.0, mean 1.0; at test time the weight is multiplied by 0.5, giving 1.0. Framework convention (drop probability 0.5, scale 1/(1 - p) during training): training output 0 or 4.0, mean 2.0; test output 2.0 unchanged. Both match train and test in expectation. Thinned networks: 2^10 = 1,024 for 10 units; 2^100 is about 1.27 × 10^30. (b) Ridge equivalence (K18): for X = [[1, 2], [3, -1], [0.5, 4]], y = [3, 1, 2], w = [0.7, 0.4], keep probability 0.8, the average loss over all 64 masks weighted by probability is 4.9044, and Srivastava's closed form ||y - pXw||² + p(1 - p)||Γw||² is also 4.9044.
5. **Normalizing a small batch.** Table of 4 examples x 3 units: [2, 1, 0], [4, 3, 1], [6, 2, 2], [8, 6, 1]. Batch norm, unit 0 (down the column): mean 5, variance 5 (divide by 4, as in Ioffe's Algorithm 1), normalized -1.342, -0.447, 0.447, 1.342. With γ = 2, β = 1: -1.683, 0.106, 1.894, 3.683. With γ = √5 and β = 5 the original 2, 4, 6, 8 come back (the "identity transform", K20). Layer norm, example 0 (along the row): mean 1, variance 0.667, normalized 1.225, 0, -1.225. Example 3: mean 5, variance 8.667, normalized 1.019, 0.340, -1.359. (ε = 10^-5 added to the variance.) A batch of one: batch norm has nothing to average over; layer norm works (K23).
6. **A residual block adds a correction.** Running value [1.0, 2.0]; three blocks add corrections [0.1, -0.05], [-0.02, 0.08], [0.03, 0.01]: [1.1, 1.95], then [1.08, 2.03], then [1.11, 2.04]. Each block is "the value so far, plus a small change". Gradient illustration (scalar, labelled illustrative): 20 layers whose own slopes are drawn in [-0.1, 0.1] (seed fixed): a plain chain multiplies the slopes, |product| about 1.4 × 10^-28; a residual chain multiplies (1 + slope), about 0.92. Plain chain with slope 0.5 per layer for 20 layers: 9.5 × 10^-7 (same as `structure` Example 5). Ties to He et al. 2016b's "does not vanish even when the weights are arbitrarily small" (K25).
7. **Adam by hand.** Defaults α = 0.001, β1 = 0.9, β2 = 0.999, ε = 10^-8, one weight starting at 0, gradients 0.5, 0.4, -0.1. Step 1: m = 0.05, v = 0.00025; bias-corrected m̂ = 0.5, v̂ = 0.25; step = 0.001; weight -0.001. Step 2: m = 0.085, v = 0.00040975, m̂ = 0.4474, v̂ = 0.2050, step 0.000988; weight -0.001988. Step 3 (gradient flips sign): m = 0.0665, m̂ = 0.2454, v̂ = 0.1399, step 0.000656; weight -0.002644 (it keeps moving the same way: momentum). Scale invariance: a first gradient of 0.001 or of 1,000 gives the same first step, 0.001 (K9's "approximately bounded by α"); plain SGD with learning rate 0.001 would step 0.0005 for 0.5 and 0.000001 for 0.001. Without bias correction the first step would be 0.0032, three times too large in this case.
8. **Learning-rate schedules.** Transformer formula (K13), d_model = 512, warmup 4,000: step 100, 0.000017; step 1,000, 0.000175; peak at step 4,000, 0.000699; step 10,000, 0.000442; step 100,000, 0.000140. Seeded; numbers will vary slightly.
10. **Gradient clipping.** Gradient (3, 4) has norm 5; clipped to norm 1 it becomes (0.6, 0.8): same direction, shorter step.

### What not to say

- Not that "p = 0.5" or "p = 0.8" is a dropout rate without saying whether p means keep or drop (D6).
- Not Adam defaults "α = 0.0002, β1 = 0.1" (v1, D6); use the ICLR 2015 values.
- Not that Adam is proven to converge, nor that it "doesn't work"; see D3.
- Not that batch norm works "because it reduces internal covariate shift" as settled fact (D1).
- Not that batch norm "beat humans" without attributing the human estimate to Russakovsky et al. via Ioffe and Szegedy (K20).
- Not that ResNet "invented skip connections" (He et al. cite earlier shortcut connections, and highway networks came first in 2015; D4). Not that a single 152-layer network got 3.57% (ensemble; K24).
- Not that the degradation problem is overfitting (He et al. say the opposite; K24).
- Not that warmup was invented by Goyal et al. or by the Transformer (D5).
- Not that momentum was invented by Rumelhart, Hinton, and Williams, nor that they took it from Polyak (D2).
- Not that Nair and Hinton invented the rectifier, or that their paper was about classifiers (K16).
- Not that Glorot initialization was designed for ReLU (derived for linear units; He et al. 2015 adapt it; K15).
- Not that deep networks could not be trained at all before 2010; Glorot and Bengio describe post-2006 successes (K15).
- Not that SGD means one example at a time in modern usage (K4). Not "batch" for both the full set and the minibatch without saying which.
- Not that layer norm averages across the batch (K23).
- Not that PyTorch dates from 2019 (initial release January 2017; the paper is 2019), nor that TensorFlow dates from 2016 (open-sourced November 2015) (K29).
- Not that a "tensor" here has anything to do with the physics or differential-geometry meaning (K3).
- No present-tense claim about which framework or optimizer is most used without a date stamp and a source (see "Not fetched").
- Do not print Sutskever et al.'s momentum constant or the √R claim (D7).

## Worked examples used in the chapter [D-009]

All computed by `code/checks/toolkit.py` (standard library, fixed seeds). The values differ slightly from the research notes above, which were computed by a scratch script; the chapter uses the repo script's output.

- Example 1: shapes. A 4 x 3 batch through 3 x 2 weights gives 4 x 2. A (32, 128, 512) tensor holds 2,097,152 numbers; through 512 x 2,048 weights (1,048,576) it becomes (32, 128, 2,048), holding 8,388,608 numbers.
- Example 2: minibatch gradient estimates.
  - The full gradient is -5.25, and single-example gradients have a standard deviation of 5.28.
  - Batches of 1, 10, and 100 give means of -5.13, -5.23, and -5.26, and standard deviations of 5.16, 1.64, and 0.48. The square-root rule predicts 5.28, 1.67, and 0.53.
- Example 3: the ravine f = (x^2 + 100 y^2)/2 from (10, 1). Learning rate 0.01 takes 882 steps, 0.019 takes 463, and 0.021 diverges (y goes 1, -1.1, 1.21, -1.331). Momentum 0.9 with rate 0.01 takes 162 steps.
- Example 4: Adam's steps are 0.001000, 0.000988, and 0.000656. Its first step is 0.001 for a first gradient of 0.001 or 1,000.
- Example 5: the Transformer schedule gives 0.000017, 0.000175, 0.000699, 0.000442, and 0.000140 at steps 100, 1,000, 4,000, 10,000, and 100,000. The cosine schedule falls through 3.00e-4, 2.60e-4, 1.65e-4, 6.95e-5, and 3.00e-5. Linear scaling gives 3.2.
- Example 6: clipping (3, 4) gives (0.6, 0.8).
- Example 7: a toy run with 20 examples, 15 features, minibatches of 5, and momentum 0.9.
  - Rate 0.00005: after 300 epochs the training loss is 0.457 and the validation loss 3.7.
  - Rate 0.001: the lowest validation loss is 2.68, at epoch 82 (training 0.04). By epoch 300 validation is 2.82 and training 0.03.
  - Rate 0.08: the loss diverges by epoch 15.
  - With all weights at zero, the validation loss is 6.96.
- Example 8: Baydin's function gives y = 11.652, dy/dx1 = 5.500, and dy/dx2 = 1.716.

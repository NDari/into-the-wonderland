## Sourced claims

### AN1. The 2017 transformer: two stacks of identical layers, two (or three) sub-layers each
- Source: `vaswani2017attention`
- Location: pp. 2-3, Fig. 1 (p. 3).
- Quotes: p. 2: "Most competitive neural sequence transduction models have an encoder-decoder structure [5, 2, 35]. Here, the encoder maps an input sequence of symbol representations (x1, ..., xn) to a sequence of continuous representations z = (z1, ..., zn). Given z, the decoder then generates an output sequence (y1, ..., ym) of symbols one element at a time. At each step the model is auto-regressive [10], consuming the previously generated symbols as additional input when generating the next." p. 3: "Encoder: The encoder is composed of a stack of N = 6 identical layers. Each layer has two sub-layers. The first is a multi-head self-attention mechanism, and the second is a simple, position-wise fully connected feed-forward network." (the source hyphenates "position-wise" across a line). "Decoder: The decoder is also composed of a stack of N = 6 identical layers. In addition to the two sub-layers in each encoder layer, the decoder inserts a third sub-layer, which performs multi-head attention over the output of the encoder stack."
- Fig. 1 (page image): left stack "Input Embedding" + "Positional Encoding", then "Multi-Head Attention", "Add & Norm", "Feed Forward", "Add & Norm", marked "Nx"; right stack "Output Embedding" fed by "Outputs (shifted right)", then "Masked Multi-Head Attention", "Add & Norm", "Multi-Head Attention" (taking the encoder output), "Add & Norm", "Feed Forward", "Add & Norm", "Nx"; above it "Linear", "Softmax", "Output Probabilities". Caption: "Figure 1: The Transformer - model architecture."
- Note: the chapter's starting point. "Layer" (Vaswani's word), "block" (`radford2018improving` eq. 2, `raffel2020exploring` p. 4, `elhage2021mathematical`) mean the same unit; pick one and say so. The third sub-layer is what later papers call "encoder-decoder attention" (Vaswani p. 5) or cross-attention; define it once.

### AN2. Residual connection and layer norm around every sub-layer; one width throughout
- Source: `vaswani2017attention`
- Location: p. 3.
- Quotes: "We employ a residual connection [11] around each of the two sub-layers, followed by layer normalization [1]. That is, the output of each sub-layer is LayerNorm(x + Sublayer(x)), where Sublayer(x) is the function implemented by the sub-layer itself. To facilitate these residual connections, all sub-layers in the model, as well as the embedding layers, produce outputs of dimension dmodel = 512."
- Note: refs [11] and [1] are He et al. (`he2015deep`) and Ba et al. (`ba2016layer`), both explained in `depth`. The second sentence is the reason every vector in the model has the same length: an addition needs two vectors of equal size. This is the "post-norm" arrangement (AN11, AND3). `depth` already quotes "LayerNorm(x + Sublayer(x))"; do not re-explain residuals or layer norm, only place them.

### AN3. The feed-forward network: the same small two-layer network applied to each position
- Source: `vaswani2017attention`
- Location: p. 5, section 3.3.
- Quotes: "In addition to attention sub-layers, each of the layers in our encoder and decoder contains a fully connected feed-forward network, which is applied to each position separately and identically. This consists of two linear transformations with a ReLU activation in between. FFN(x) = max(0, xW1 + b1)W2 + b2 (2). While the linear transformations are the same across different positions, they use different parameters from layer to layer. Another way of describing this is as two convolutions with kernel size 1. The dimensionality of input and output is dmodel = 512, and the inner-layer has dimensionality dff = 2048."
- Note: the bridge is direct: this is the 1990s multilayer perceptron (`backprop`) with one hidden layer, applied to every token's vector separately with shared weights. The inner layer is four times wider (2048 = 4 x 512); `brown2020language` p. 8 states the same ratio for GPT-3 ("we always have the feedforward layer four times the size of the bottleneck layer, dff = 4 * dmodel"), and `devlin2019bert` p. 4173 footnote 3 for BERT ("4H"). "Two convolutions with kernel size 1" links to `structure`, optional.

### AN4. The output layer, and one table used twice (weight tying)
- Sources: `vaswani2017attention`; `press2017using`; `radford2018improving`; `openai2019gpt2code`; `raffel2020exploring`.
- Quotes: Vaswani p. 5, section 3.4: "Similarly to other sequence transduction models, we use learned embeddings to convert the input tokens and output tokens to vectors of dimension dmodel. We also use the usual learned linear transformation and softmax function to convert the decoder output to predicted next-token probabilities. In our model, we share the same weight matrix between the two embedding layers and the pre-softmax linear transformation, similar to [30]. In the embedding layers, we multiply those weights by √dmodel." ([30] is Press and Wolf.)
- Press and Wolf, abstract (p. 157): "We study the topmost weight matrix of neural network language models. We show that this matrix constitutes a valid word embedding. When training language models, we recommend tying the input embedding and this output embedding. ... Finally, we show that weight tying can reduce the size of neural translation models to less than half of their original size without harming their performance." p. 157: "We call U the input embedding, and V the output embedding. In both matrices, we expect rows that correspond to similar words to be similar: for the input embedding, we would like the network to react similarly to synonyms, while in the output embedding, we would like the scores of words that are interchangeable to be similar". p. 158: "Independently and concurrently with our work (Inan et al., 2016) presented an explanation for weight tying in NNLMs". p. 161 (Table 5 discussion): "weight tying significantly reduces perplexity on both the validation set and the test set, but not on the training set. This indicates less overfitting, as expected due to the reduction in the number of parameters."
- GPT-1, eq. (2), p. 3: "P(u) = softmax(hn WeT)", with "We is the token embedding matrix" (the transpose of the embedding table scores every token). GPT-2 code (`src/model.py`, function `model`): `logits = tf.matmul(h_flat, wte, transpose_b=True)`, where `wte` is the token embedding table; the 124M checkpoint index has no separate output matrix (AN17).
- T5, p. 5: "The output of the final decoder block is fed into a dense layer with a softmax output, whose weights are shared with the input embedding matrix."
- Note: in plain words, the score for token t is the dot product of the final vector with token t's own row of the embedding table. The bridge from `destination` ("one score per class, a weighted sum") holds; tying says the class weights are the token's embedding. Not every model ties: Llama 3's reference code has a separate `self.output` linear layer next to `self.tok_embeddings` (`meta2024llama3code`, `llama/model.py`, class `Transformer`). Say "GPT-2 ties; Llama 3 does not", not "transformers tie". For GPT-2 small, an untied output layer would add 38,597,376 parameters (31% more; mechanism item 3).

### AN5. Causal masking: a position may not look ahead
- Sources: `vaswani2017attention`; `raffel2020exploring`; `openai2019gpt2code`; `meta2024llama3code`; `grattafiori2024llama`.
- Quotes: Vaswani p. 3: "We also modify the self-attention sub-layer in the decoder stack to prevent positions from attending to subsequent positions. This masking, combined with fact that the output embeddings are offset by one position, ensures that the predictions for position i can depend only on the known outputs at positions less than i." (the source omits "the" before "fact"). p. 5: "self-attention layers in the decoder allow each position in the decoder to attend to all positions in the decoder up to and including that position. We need to prevent leftward information flow in the decoder to preserve the auto-regressive property. We implement this inside of scaled dot-product attention by masking out (setting to -∞) all values in the input of the softmax which correspond to illegal connections."
- T5, Fig. 3 caption (p. 15): "A dark cell at row i and column j indicates that the self-attention mechanism is allowed to attend to input element j at output timestep i. ... Left: A fully-visible mask allows the self-attention mechanism to attend to the full input at every output timestep. Middle: A causal mask prevents the ith output element from depending on any input elements from "the future". Right: Causal masking with a prefix allows the self-attention mechanism to use fully-visible masking on a portion of the input sequence." p. 16: "the causal mask (Figure 3, middle) sets any wi,j to zero if j > i."
- Code: GPT-2 `src/model.py`, `attention_mask` docstring "1's in the lower triangle, counting from the lower right corner", and `mask_attn_weights` replaces masked scores by a large negative number (`w*b - tf.cast(1e10, w.dtype)*(1-b)`) before the softmax. Llama 3 `llama/model.py`: `mask = torch.full((seqlen, seqlen), float("-inf"), ...)`, `mask = torch.triu(mask, diagonal=1)`.
- Llama 3 refinement, p. 6: "We use an attention mask that prevents self-attention between different documents within the same sequence. We find that this change had limited impact during in standard pre-training, but find it to be important in continued pre-training on very long sequences." (source's wording "during in").
- Note: the mechanism, by intuition: scores for later positions are set to minus infinity before the softmax, and e to the minus infinity is zero, so those positions get weight exactly zero (mechanism item 7). The 2000s bridge: it is the rule against leakage, the same reason one never validates a time-series model on data from before the training period's end; here every position is a separate prediction and must not see its own answer. Positional "shifted right" (Vaswani Fig. 1) means the input at position i is the token at i - 1 in the target.

### AN6. Training predicts every position at once
- Sources: `liu2018generating`; `radford2018improving`; `openai2018finetunecode`; `openai2019gpt2code`; `vaswani2017attention`.
- Quotes: Liu et al. p. 5: "train a model to predict the next word given the previous ones ... Since the model is forced to predict the next token in the input, m, as well as y, error signals are propagated from both input and output time-steps during training." GPT-1 p. 3, eq. (1): "L1(U) = Σi log P(ui | ui-k, ..., ui-1; Θ) where k is the size of the context window". GPT-1 code (`train.py`, `model`): `lm_h = tf.reshape(h[:, :-1], [-1, n_embd])`, `lm_logits = tf.matmul(lm_h, we, transpose_b=True)`, loss against `labels=tf.reshape(X[:, 1:, 0], [-1])`: the vector at every position but the last is scored against the token that follows it. GPT-2 code comment above the output layer: "# Language model loss.  Do tokens <n predict token n?", and the logits are computed for every position (`logits = tf.reshape(logits, [batch, sequence, hparams.n_vocab])`).
- Note: with the causal mask, one pass over a window of n + 1 tokens produces n next-token predictions, each seeing only its own past, and all n losses are averaged in one gradient step. This is why training is parallel across positions while generation is one token at a time (`seq2seq` Q21 note; `attention` owns the full parallelism argument; `training` owns the loss and batching). Count in mechanism item 8.

### AN7. The decoder-only form: drop the encoder, train as a language model
- Sources: `liu2018generating`; `radford2018improving`; `kaplan2020scaling`; `raffel2020exploring`.
- Quotes: Liu et al. abstract (p. 1): "For the abstractive model, we introduce a decoder-only architecture that can scalably attend to very long sequences, much longer than typical encoder-decoder architectures used in sequence transduction." Section 4.2.3, "Transformer Decoder (T-D)" (p. 5): "We introduce a simple but effective modification to T-ED for long sequences that drops the encoder module (almost reducing model parameters by half for a given hyper-parameter set), combines the input and output sequences into a single "sentence" and is trained as a standard language model. That is, we convert a sequence-transduction example (m1, ..., mn) 7→ (y1, ..., yη) into the sentence (w1, ..., wn+η+1) = (m1, ..., mn, δ, y1, ..., yη), where δ is a special separator token" (the "7→" is an extraction of a maps-to arrow). p. 6: "The Transformer Decoder (T-D) was identical to the decoder part of T-ED." Conclusion (p. 11): "We introduce a new, decoder-only sequence transduction model for the abstractive stage". arXiv v1: 30 Jan 2018; ICLR 2018.
- GPT-1, p. 3: "In our experiments, we use a multi-layer Transformer decoder [34] for the language model, which is a variant of the transformer [62]. This model applies a multi-headed self-attention operation over the input context tokens followed by position-wise feedforward layers to produce an output distribution over target tokens" ([34] is Liu et al., "Generating wikipedia by summarizing long sequences. ICLR, 2018"; [62] Vaswani et al.). p. 5: "We trained a 12-layer decoder-only transformer with masked self-attention heads".
- Kaplan et al. p. 6: "We primarily train decoder-only [LSP+18, RNSS18] Transformer [VSP+17] models" (LSP+18 = Liu et al.; RNSS18 = Radford et al. 2018).
- T5 p. 4: "It has recently also become common to use models consisting of a single Transformer layer stack, with varying forms of self-attention used to produce architectures appropriate for language modeling (Radford et al., 2018; Al-Rfou et al., 2019) or classification and span prediction tasks (Devlin et al., 2018; Yang et al., 2019)."
- Note: a decoder-only model is the 2017 decoder stack with its third sub-layer (attention to the encoder) removed, since there is no encoder: two sub-layers per block, causal mask, output softmax. GPT-1 cites Liu et al. for it. See AND2 on credit.

### AN8. GPT-1 (2018): the decoder-only recipe at 12 layers
- Sources: `radford2018improving`; `openai2018finetunecode`.
- Location: GPT-1 pp. 3-5, Fig. 1 (p. 4, page image).
- Quotes: p. 5, "Model specifications": "Our model largely follows the original transformer work [62]. We trained a 12-layer decoder-only transformer with masked self-attention heads (768 dimensional states and 12 attention heads). For the position-wise feed-forward networks, we used 3072 dimensional inner states. ... We train for 100 epochs on minibatches of 64 randomly sampled, contiguous sequences of 512 tokens. ... We used a bytepair encoding (BPE) vocabulary with 40,000 merges [53] ... For the activation function, we used the Gaussian Error Linear Unit (GELU) [18]. We used learned position embeddings instead of the sinusoidal version proposed in the original work." p. 3, eq. (2): "h0 = U We + Wp; hl = transformer_block(hl-1) ∀i ∈ [1, n]; P(u) = softmax(hn WeT)". Data (p. 4): "We use the BooksCorpus dataset [71] for training the language model. It contains over 7,000 unique unpublished books from a variety of genres including Adventure, Fantasy, and Romance. Crucially, it contains long stretches of contiguous text, which allows the generative model to learn to condition on long-range information."
- Fig. 1 (left, page image): "Text & Position Embed" at the bottom; then "Masked Multi Self Attention", a "+" node, "Layer Norm", "Feed Forward", "+", "Layer Norm", bracketed "12x"; on top "Text Prediction" and "Task Classifier". Caption: "Figure 1: (left) Transformer architecture and training objectives used in this work."
- Released code: `train.py` (`block`): `a = attn(x, ...)`, `n = norm(x+a, 'ln_1')`, `m = mlp(n, ...)`, `h = norm(n+m, 'ln_2')`: the 2017 post-norm order. `params_shapes.json`: 146 tensors; first two shapes [512, 768] (positions) and [40478, 768] (token table, 40,478 = entries in `encoder_bpe_40000.json`); total 116,534,784 numbers (counted this session).
- Note: the GPT-1 paper prints no parameter count (searched). The 116.5M total comes from the released shapes and is this ledger's count. GPT-1 is post-norm (Fig. 1 and code); GPT-2 moved the norm (AN11). "Text & Position Embed" is the destination chapter's "Vectors" box.

### AN9. GPT-2 (2019): sizes, context, vocabulary, and the changed block
- Sources: `radford2019language`; `openai2019gpt2code`.
- Location: PDF p. 4 (section 2.3 and Table 2; section 3).
- Quotes: section 2.3 "Model" (p. 4): "We use a Transformer (Vaswani et al., 2017) based architecture for our LMs. The model largely follows the details of the OpenAI GPT model (Radford et al., 2018) with a few modifications. Layer normalization (Ba et al., 2016) was moved to the input of each sub-block, similar to a pre-activation residual network (He et al., 2016) and an additional layer normalization was added after the final self-attention block. A modified initialization which accounts for the accumulation on the residual path with model depth is used. We scale the weights of residual layers at initialization by a factor of 1/√N where N is the number of residual layers. The vocabulary is expanded to 50,257. We also increase the context size from 512 to 1024 tokens and a larger batchsize of 512 is used." Table 2 (p. 4): "Architecture hyperparameters for the 4 model sizes": Parameters 117M, 345M, 762M, 1542M; Layers 12, 24, 36, 48; dmodel 768, 1024, 1280, 1600. Section 3 (p. 4): "The smallest model is equivalent to the original GPT, and the second smallest equivalent to the largest model from BERT (Devlin et al., 2018). Our largest model, which we call GPT-2, has over an order of magnitude more parameters than GPT."
- Code (`src/model.py`, `block`): `a, present = attn(norm(x, 'ln_1'), ...)`, `x = x + a`, `m = mlp(norm(x, 'ln_2'), 'mlp', nx*4, ...)`, `x = x + m`: normalize, compute, add (pre-norm). `model`: `h = tf.gather(wte, X) + tf.gather(wpe, positions_for(X, past_length))` (token row plus position row), the 12 blocks, then `h = norm(h, 'ln_f')`, then the tied output (AN4). `mlp` uses `gelu`. `wpe` has shape `[hparams.n_ctx, hparams.n_embd]`.
- `hparams.json` for 124M: {"n_vocab": 50257, "n_ctx": 1024, "n_embd": 768, "n_head": 12, "n_layer": 12}. For 355M, 774M, 1558M (fetched): n_embd 1024, 1280, 1600; n_head 16, 20, 25; n_layer 24, 36, 48; n_vocab and n_ctx unchanged.
- Note: "moved to the input of each sub-block" is the pre-norm arrangement (AN11); "He et al., 2016" is the identity-mappings paper (`he2016identity`, used in `depth`), the "pre-activation" ResNet. The position table literally has 1,024 rows: GPT-2 cannot be given a 1,025th position, which is a concrete meaning of "context window" (mechanism item 10). See AND1 for 117M vs 124M.

### AN10. GPT-3 (2020): the same block, larger, with a longer window
- Source: `brown2020language`
- Location: p. 8, section 2.1 and Table 2.1.
- Quotes: "We use the same model and architecture as GPT-2 [RWC+19], including the modified initialization, pre-normalization, and reversible tokenization described therein, with the exception that we use alternating dense and locally banded sparse attention patterns in the layers of the transformer, similar to the Sparse Transformer [CGRS19]." ### AN11. Pre-norm and post-norm: where the normalization sits
- Sources: `vaswani2017attention` (AN2); `radford2019language` (AN9); `brown2020language` (AN10); `touvron2023llama`; `xiong2020layer`; `liu2020understanding`; `raffel2020exploring`.
- Quotes: LLaMA, PDF p. 3: "Pre-normalization [GPT3]. To improve the training stability, we normalize the input of each transformer sub-layer, instead of normalizing the output. We use the RMSNorm normalizing function, introduced by Zhang and Sennrich (2019)." T5 p. 4: "Layer normalization (Ba et al., 2016) is applied to the input of each subcomponent. We use a simplified version of layer normalization where the activations are only rescaled and no additive bias is applied. After layer normalization, a residual skip connection (He et al., 2016) adds each subcomponent's input to its output."
- Xiong et al., abstract (PDF p. 1): "we prove with mean field theory that at initialization, for the original-designed Post-LN Transformer, which places the layer normalization between the residual blocks, the expected gradients of the parameters near the output layer are large. Therefore, using a large learning rate on those gradients makes the training unstable. The warm-up stage is practically helpful for avoiding this problem. On the other hand, our theory also shows that if the layer normalization is put inside the residual blocks (recently proposed as Pre-LN Transformer), the gradients are well-behaved at initialization. This motivates us to remove the warm-up stage for the training of Pre-LN Transformers. We show in our experiments that Pre-LN Transformers without the warm-up stage can reach comparable results with baselines while requiring significantly less training time and hyper-parameter tuning on a wide range of applications." Introduction (PDF p. 1): "The originally designed Transformer places the layer normalization between the residual blocks, which is usually referred to as the Transformer with Post-Layer Normalization (Post-LN) (Wang et al., 2019)." PDF p. 2: "we study another variant, the Transformer with Pre-Layer Normalization (Pre-LN) (Baevski & Auli, 2018; Child et al., 2019; Wang et al., 2019)."
- Liu et al. 2020, p. 5749: "Pre-LN training is more robust than Post-LN, while Post-LN is more likely to reach a better performance than Pre-LN." Footnote on the same page: "In a parameter grid search (as in Figure 10), Pre-LN converges in all 15 settings, and Post-LN diverges in 7 out of 15 settings; when Post-LN converges, it outperforms Pre-LN in 7 out of 8 settings." p. 5747, Fig. 1 caption: "Lacking enough robustness and stability, the 18-Layer Post-LN Transformer training (i.e.the original architecture) diverges and is omitted in the left graph."
- Note: the residual-stream picture makes the difference easy to draw. Post-norm: add the correction, then normalize the running value itself. Pre-norm: leave the running value alone; each sub-layer reads a normalized copy, and its output is added back unnormalized; a final normalization comes before the output layer (GPT-2's extra layer norm). The chapter needs the placement and the one-line reason the field gives (stability); the trade-off is AND3; the detailed story belongs to `since-2017`.

### AN13. The residual stream (an interpretability framing)
- Sources: `elhage2021mathematical`; `kaplan2020scaling`.
- Quotes (Elhage et al., section "High-Level Architecture"): "There are several variants of transformer language models. We focus on autoregressive, decoder-only transformer language models, such as GPT-3. (The original transformer paper had a special encoder-decoder structure to support translation, but many modern language models don't include this.) A transformer starts with a token embedding, followed by a series of "residual blocks", and finally a token unembedding. Each residual block consists of an attention layer, followed by an MLP layer. Both the attention and MLP layers each "read" their input from the residual stream (by performing a linear projection), and then "write" their result to the residual stream by adding a linear projection back in."
- Section "Virtual Weights and the Residual Stream as a Communication Channel": "One of the main features of the high level architecture of a transformer is that each layer adds its results into what we call the "residual stream."" Sidenote: "Constructing models with a residual stream traces back to early work by the Schmidhuber group, such as highway networks and LSTMs, which have found significant modern success in the more recent residual network architecture." Main text: "In transformers, the residual stream vectors are often called the "embedding." We prefer the residual stream terminology, both because it emphasizes the residual nature (which we believe to be important) and also because we believe the residual stream often dedicates subspaces to tokens other than the present token, breaking the intuitions the embedding terminology suggests. The residual stream is simply the sum of the output of all the previous layers and the original embedding. We generally think of the residual stream as a communication channel, since it doesn't do any processing itself and all layers communicate through it."
- Section "Subspaces and Residual Stream Bandwidth": "The residual stream is a high-dimensional vector space. In small models, it may be hundreds of dimensions; in large models it can go into the tens of thousands. This means that layers can send different information to different layers by storing it in different subspaces. ... Once added, information persists in a subspace unless another layer actively deletes it. From this perspective, dimensions of the residual stream become something like "memory" or "bandwidth"."
- Section "Attention Heads as Information Movement": "The fundamental action of attention heads is moving information. They read information from the residual stream of one token, and write it to the residual stream of another token." Section "Summary of Results": "All components of a transformer (the token embedding, attention heads, MLP layers, and unembedding) communicate with each other by reading and writing to different subspaces of the residual stream."
- Their caveats: "Model Simplifications": "we focus on "attention-only" transformers, which don't have MLP layers. ... we've also simply had much less success in understanding MLP layers so far". Layer norm is ignored in their analysis ("We also ignore layer normalization").
- Kaplan et al. p. 6 (Jan 2020): hyperparameters include "dmodel (dimension of the residual stream)".
- Note: this is how the chapter should frame the whole model: a stream of one vector per position, running from the embedding table to the output layer; every attention and feed-forward sub-layer reads from it and adds into it; attention is the only part that moves information between positions; the feed-forward part works on each position alone. "The sum of the output of all the previous layers and the original embedding" is exactly `depth`'s running value (`he2016identity` p. 3, via `toolkit` ledger K25), and `figures/depth.py` `residual_figure()` can be reused. Label it as a way of describing the computation that interpretability researchers find useful (as of 2021), not as a claim about what the model "knows". It holds exactly for pre-norm models; in post-norm models (the 2017 design, GPT-1) the running value is itself normalized after each addition. See AND4 on the term's origin.

### AN14. What the feed-forward layers may do (an interpretation)
- Source: `geva2021transformer`
- Location: pp. 5484-5487, 5494.
- Quotes: abstract (p. 5484): "Feed-forward layers constitute two-thirds of a transformer model's parameters, yet their role in the network remains under-explored. We show that feed-forward layers in transformer-based language models operate as key-value memories, where each key correlates with textual patterns in the training examples, and each value induces a distribution over the output vocabulary. Our experiments show that the learned patterns are human-interpretable, and that lower layers tend to capture shallow patterns, while upper layers learn more semantic ones. ... Finally, we demonstrate that the output of a feed-forward layer is a composition of its memories, which is subsequently refined throughout the model's layers via residual connections to produce the final output distribution." p. 5484: self-attention layers "account for only a third of a typical transformer's parameters (4d2 per layer, where d is the model's hidden dimension). Most of the parameter budget is spent on position-wise feed-forward layers (8d2 per layer)". Setup (p. 5486): "We conduct our experiment over the language model of Baevski and Auli (2019), a 16-layer transformer language model trained on WikiText-103 (Merity et al., 2017). This model defines d = 1024 and dm = 4096, and has a total of dm · 16 = 65,536 potential keys to analyze. We randomly sample 10 keys per layer (160 in total)." p. 5487: "the lower layers (layers 1-9) are dominated by shallow patterns, often with prefixes that share the last word". Appendix B (p. 5494): "(247M parameters)".
- Note: interpretation, not established mechanism. The bridge is honest and attractive: the first matrix of the feed-forward network acts like a bank of pattern detectors (each row a "key" matched against the vector by a dot product, through the nonlinearity), the second like a table of what to add when a detector fires. Attribute it ("Geva and colleagues argued in 2021"), give the scope (one 16-layer model trained on Wikipedia text, 160 sampled units, human annotation), and pair it with Elhage et al.'s admission that MLPs were "much harder to get traction on" (AN13). "Two-thirds" is two-thirds of the block parameters only (8d^2 of 12d^2), not of the whole model (AND8).

### AN15. Where the parameters are, and the standard estimate
- Source: `kaplan2020scaling`
- Location: pp. 6-7, eq. (2.1) and Table 1.
- Quotes: p. 6: "We parameterize the Transformer architecture using hyperparameters nlayer (number of layers), dmodel (dimension of the residual stream), dff (dimension of the intermediate feed-forward layer), dattn (dimension of the attention output), and nheads (number of attention heads per layer). We include nctx tokens in the input context, with nctx = 1024 except where otherwise noted. We use N to denote the model size, which we define as the number of non-embedding parameters N ≈ 2dmodel nlayer (2dattn + dff) = 12nlayer d2model with the standard dattn = dff/4 = dmodel (2.1) where we have excluded biases and other sub-leading terms. Table 1 (p. 7), "Parameter counts and compute (forward pass) estimates for a Transformer model. ### AN17. Context window: what it is, dated sizes, and why it costs
- Sources: `radford2018improving` (512, p. 5); `radford2019language` (1024, p. 4); `openai2019gpt2code` (`wpe` shape); `brown2020language` (2048, p. 8); `grattafiori2024llama` (8K then 128K, pp. 3, 10, as in `book1/sources/destination.md` C6); `vaswani2017attention` (Table 1, p. 6); `devlin2019bert` (p. 4183); `kaplan2020scaling` (p. 7); `ainslie2023gqa`; `grattafiori2024llama` p. 6.
- Quotes: Vaswani Table 1 (p. 6): "Complexity per Layer": self-attention "O(n2 · d)", recurrent "O(n · d2)"; caption: "n is the sequence length, d is the representation dimension". pp. 6-7: "self-attention layers are faster than recurrent layers when the sequence length n is smaller than the representation dimensionality d, which is most often the case with sentence representations used by state-of-the-art models in machine translations". p. 7: "To improve computational performance for tasks involving very long sequences, self-attention could be restricted to considering only a neighborhood of size r in the input sequence centered around the respective output position." BERT p. 4183: "Longer sequences are disproportionately expensive because attention is quadratic to the sequence length." Kaplan p. 7: "For contexts and models with dmodel > nctx/12, the context-dependent computational cost per token is a relatively small fraction of the total compute." GQA p. 4895: "Autoregressive decoder inference is a severe bottleneck for Transformer models due to the memory bandwidth overhead from loading decoder weights and all attention keys and values at every decoding step". Llama 3 p. 6: GQA "with 8 key-value heads to improve inference speed and to reduce the size of key-value caches during decoding."
- Dated sizes: GPT-1 512 tokens (2018); GPT-2 1,024 (2019); GPT-3 2,048 (2020); Llama 3.1 up to 128K = 131,072 (2024). `destination` already prints the last three with citations; this chapter adds GPT-1 and the mechanism.
- Note: two costs to separate. (1) Compute: each attention head in each layer compares every position with every earlier one, so the number of scores grows with the square of the length: 1,048,576 per head per layer at 1,024 tokens, about 17.2 billion at 131,072 (128 times longer, 16,384 times more scores; mechanism item 9). Kaplan's point that this term is small next to the 2N per-token cost for short contexts keeps the claim honest: quadratic growth matters at long contexts, not at GPT-2's. (2) Memory during generation: the stored keys and values grow with the length (GQA and Llama 3 quotes); the mechanism of the cache belongs to `generation`, with one forward sentence here. Also a structural limit: a learned position table (GPT-1, GPT-2) has a fixed number of rows; models with rotary positions (LLaMA, Llama 3) have no such table, so their limit is what they were trained on (AN18).

### AND2. Who introduced the decoder-only transformer?

- Kaplan et al. (2020) cite both: "decoder-only [LSP+18, RNSS18]" (p. 6). T5 (2020) cites Radford et al. 2018 and Al-Rfou et al. 2019 (p. 4). Al-Rfou et al. was not fetched.
- And the decoder itself, with causal masking, is in Vaswani et al. (2017, p. 3); a decoder-only model removes the encoder and the cross-attention sub-layer.
- Suggested wording: "Dropping the encoder and training the decoder alone as a language model was proposed by Peter Liu and colleagues at Google in early 2018, for summarizing Wikipedia sources; OpenAI's GPT, a few months later, used the same design and cited them." No "first" beyond what Liu et al. say of themselves ("we introduce").

### AND3. Pre-norm or post-norm?
- For pre-norm: Xiong et al. (2020): post-LN has large gradients near the output at initialization and needs a learning-rate warm-up; pre-LN is "well-behaved at initialization" and trains without warm-up to "comparable results" with less tuning (abstract). LLaMA: "To improve the training stability" (PDF p. 3). GPT-2, GPT-3, T5, LLaMA, and Llama 3 all use it (AN9-AN11, AN18).
- For post-norm: Liu et al. (2020): "Post-LN is more likely to reach a better performance than Pre-LN"; in their grid "when Post-LN converges, it outperforms Pre-LN in 7 out of 8 settings", but it diverged in 7 of 15 (p. 5749); they propose a modified initialization (Admin) to get both. Their experiments are machine translation (WMT'14, IWSLT'14), not large language models.
- Present both. Suggested wording: "Moving the normalization made deep stacks easier to train; one 2020 study found that the original arrangement, when it trained at all, often ended slightly better. Large language models have used the easier-to-train version." The last sentence is supported for the models cited here (GPT-2, GPT-3, LLaMA, Llama 3), as of 2024; do not generalize to "all models".
- Inconsistency in a secondary source: Xiong et al. list Radford et al. 2019 (GPT-2) among models "based on the Post-LN Transformer architecture" (PDF p. 1), but GPT-2's own text (p. 4) and code (`src/model.py`) put the norm at the input of each sub-block. Do not cite Xiong et al. for GPT-2's arrangement.

### AND4. The term "residual stream": who and when
- Elhage et al. (Dec 2021) introduce it as "what we call the "residual stream"" and contrast it with the common name "embedding". But Kaplan et al. (Jan 2020) already define dmodel as "dimension of the residual stream" (p. 6). The two papers share authors: Jared Kaplan, Sam McCandlish, Tom Henighan, and Tom Brown are on both (checked on the Elhage page and on the Kaplan title page, whose first six authors are Kaplan, McCandlish, Henighan, Brown, Chess, Child).
- Elhage et al. trace the idea of a residual stream to "highway networks and LSTMs" from "the Schmidhuber group" and to residual networks (sidenote); `depth` already presents the highway-network credit dispute.
- Suggested wording: "Interpretability researchers call this running vector the residual stream" (cite Elhage et al.), without "coined". Note for terminology [one running thread]: the book needs one name for the per-position vector between layers. "Embedding" is already used for the table row (`embeddings`, `destination`); Elhage et al. explain why reusing it is misleading. Recommend "residual stream" for the running vector, "embedding" only for the table row, and mention "hidden state" once as a synonym readers will meet.

### AND5. "Encoder" and "decoder" mean two things
- In Vaswani et al. they are two stacks joined by cross-attention (AN1). In later usage "encoder" often means "bidirectional (fully visible) stack" and "decoder" means "causal stack", whether or not there is another stack: BERT footnote 4 ("the bidirectional Transformer is often referred to as a "Transformer encoder" while the left-context-only version is referred to as a "Transformer decoder"", pp. 4173-4174). A "decoder-only" model has no cross-attention at all.
- Suggested handling: define the three families by their masks (AN12), then say that "decoder-only" names the causal mask, not a decoder of anything.

### AND6. Small inconsistencies in primary sources
- `brown2020language` Table 2.1: GPT-3 13B prints dmodel 5140 with 40 heads of dhead 128, whose product is 5120. Do not print 5140 without comment; better, omit the 13B row.
- `vaswani2017attention`: the base model is listed at 65M parameters (Table 3, p. 9), but a reconstruction from the paper's own description (6 + 6 layers, dmodel 512, dff 2048, shared embedding of "about 37000" tokens, tied output, no positional parameters) gives about 63.1M (mechanism item 11). The paper does not give the exact vocabulary or how it counted. Do not use the 2017 base model for the counting example; GPT-2 small is exact.
- `radford2019language` p. 4: "an additional layer normalization was added after the final self-attention block". In the code the final layer norm comes after the final block (after its feed-forward sub-layer): `h = norm(h, 'ln_f')` after the loop. Describe it as "one more normalization before the output layer".
- `radford2018improving` eq. (2) writes "∀i ∈ [1, n]" while the layer index is l; trivial.
- `touvron2023llama` p. 3 prints "Here are the main difference" and "where we were found the inspiration"; quote with care or paraphrase.

### AND7. How much do architecture details matter?
- Llama 3 (p. 6): gains "primarily driven by improvements in data quality and diversity as well as by increased training scale", not architecture. Kaplan et al., abstract (p. 1): "Other architectural details such as network width or depth have minimal effects within a wide range." GPT-3 p. 8, citing Kaplan et al.: "Previous work [KMH+20] suggests that validation loss is not strongly sensitive to these parameters within a reasonably broad range." Shazeer 2020: improvements from GLU variants with "no explanation". Liu et al. 2020 vs Xiong et al. 2020 on norm placement (AND3).
- Not a dispute so much as a caution: the chapter should present the block as a stable design with small, empirically chosen variations, and not suggest that each component's form is derived from theory.

### AND8. "Two-thirds of the parameters are in the feed-forward layers"
- Geva et al. (abstract): "Feed-forward layers constitute two-thirds of a transformer model's parameters". True of the blocks (8d^2 of 12d^2: 66.6% of GPT-2 small's block parameters), not of the whole model when the embedding table is counted (GPT-2 small: feed-forward 45.5% of all parameters, token table 31.0%; mechanism item 1). In Llama 3 8B the feed-forward share of each block is 80.8% (SwiGLU at 14,336 wide and only 8 key/value heads; mechanism item 6). Give the share for the model in the table, computed.

### AND9. Is the Geva et al. picture established?
- It is one study of one 16-layer model trained on WikiText-103, with 160 sampled feed-forward units annotated by experts (AN14). Elhage et al. (2021) say MLP layers were much harder to understand than attention. Present as "one influential interpretation, as of 2021", not as the function of feed-forward layers.

### AND10. GPT-1's size, as seen by BERT
- BERT p. 4173: "BERTBASE was chosen to have the same model size as OpenAI GPT" with "Total Parameters=110M"; GPT-1's released weights are 116.5M (AN8). "Same size" here means same layers and width (12, 768, 12 heads), with a smaller vocabulary (30,000 WordPiece tokens). Not a real conflict; do not imply the counts are equal.

### AND11. Llama 3 vocabulary: 128,000 or more?
- `grattafiori2024llama` Table 3 (p. 7) and text: 128,000 / "128K tokens"; the destination chapter prints 128,000. The released configuration files (not fetched; gated) are commonly reported as having 128,256 rows, which would include reserved special tokens. Unverified here. The Llama 3 8B count in mechanism item 6 is 8.028B with 128,000 and 8.030B with 128,256: either way "8B". If the chapter computes the 8B count, say it uses the paper's 128,000.

With GPT-2's batch of 512 sequences (`radford2019language` p. 4), 512 x 1,024 = 524,288 predictions per training step (state as arithmetic on the printed batch size and context, not as a reported number).

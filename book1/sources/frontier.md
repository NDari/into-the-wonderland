## Sourced claims

### Part A. Reasoning models and test-time computation

### FR1. OpenAI introduces o1 (September 2024): reinforcement learning to use a chain of thought
- Source: `openai2024learning` (opening section, dated September 12, 2024).
- Quotes: "We are introducing OpenAI o1, a new large language model trained with reinforcement learning to perform complex reasoning. o1 thinks before it answers - it can produce a long internal chain of thought before responding to the user." "Our large-scale reinforcement learning algorithm teaches the model how to think productively using its chain of thought in a highly data-efficient training process." Section "Chain of Thought": "Through reinforcement learning, o1 learns to hone its chain of thought and refine the strategies it uses. It learns to recognize and correct its mistakes. It learns to break down tricky steps into simpler ones. It learns to try a different approach when the current one isn't working."
- Release: "we are releasing an early version of this model, OpenAI o1-preview, for immediate use in ChatGPT and to trusted API users".
- Note: the post gives no details of the RL algorithm or reward. "Thinks" is OpenAI's word; the chapter should say "generates intermediate text before its answer" and attribute "thinks" to the vendor.

### FR2. Two compute dials: train-time RL and test-time thinking (o1)
- Source: `openai2024learning` (opening section).
- Quote: "We have found that the performance of o1 consistently improves with more reinforcement learning (train-time compute) and with more time spent thinking (test-time compute). The constraints on scaling this approach differ substantially from those of LLM pretraining, and we are continuing to investigate them." Figure title: "o1 performance smoothly improves with both train-time and test-time compute".
- Note: the figure's axes are log scale with no printed values; nothing may be read off it [D-009]. The claim is OpenAI's, about its own model, as of 2024-09.

### FR3. o1 on the 2024 AIME: one sample, majority vote, re-ranking
- Source: `openai2024learning` ("Evals").
- Quote: "On the 2024 AIME exams, GPT-4o only solved on average 12% (1.8/15) of problems. o1 averaged 74% (11.1/15) with a single sample per problem, 83% (12.5/15) with consensus among 64 samples, and 93% (13.9/15) when re-ranking 1000 samples with a learned scoring function. A score of 13.9 places it among the top 500 students nationally and above the cutoff for the USA Mathematical Olympiad." Also: "Unless otherwise specified, we evaluated o1 on the maximal test-time compute setting."
- Note: three ways of spending more computation at answer time on the same model: one long answer, majority vote over 64, best of 1,000 chosen by a scorer. Arithmetic in M2.

### FR4. o1's GPQA result and OpenAI's own caveat; where o1 was not preferred
- Source: `openai2024learning` ("Evals", "Human preference evaluation").
- Quotes: "We found that o1 surpassed the performance of those human experts, becoming the first model to do so on this benchmark. These results do not imply that o1 is more capable than a PhD in all respects - only that the model is more proficient in solving some problems that a PhD would be expected to solve." "o1-preview is preferred to gpt-4o by a large margin in reasoning-heavy categories like data analysis, coding, and math. However, o1-preview is not preferred on some natural language tasks, suggesting that it is not well-suited for all use cases."
- Note: "No hype": use the caveat with the claim, or neither.

### FR5. o1 hides its raw chain of thought, for monitoring and other reasons
- Source: `openai2024learning` ("Hiding the Chains of Thought").
- Quotes: "We believe that a hidden chain of thought presents a unique opportunity for monitoring models. Assuming it is faithful and legible, the hidden chain of thought allows us to "read the mind" of the model and understand its thought process." "However, for this to work the model must have freedom to express its thoughts in unaltered form, so we cannot train any policy compliance or user preferences onto the chain of thought." "Therefore, after weighing multiple factors including user experience, competitive advantage, and the option to pursue the chain of thought monitoring, we have decided not to show the raw chains of thought to users." "For the o1 model series we show a model-generated summary of the chain of thought."
- Note: "Assuming it is faithful" is the hinge; faithfulness itself belongs to `prompting`. The listed reasons include "competitive advantage".

### FR6. DeepSeek-R1-Zero: reinforcement learning from a base model with only a correctness reward
- Source: `deepseekai2025r1v2`, pp. 1-4.

### FR7. GRPO: score each answer against the others in its group
- Source: `deepseekai2025r1v2`, p. 3 (Eqs. 1-3).
- Text: "For each question q, GRPO samples a group of outputs {o1, o2, ..., oG} from the old policy ... and then optimizes the policy model". The advantage (Eq. 3): A_i = (r_i - mean({r1, r2, ..., rG})) / std({r1, r2, ..., rG}). Settings for R1-Zero (p. 3): "we sample 16 outputs with a maximum length of 32,768 tokens before the 8.2k step and 65,536 tokens afterward", "a total of 10,400 steps, corresponding to 1.6 training epochs. Each training step consists of 32 unique questions, resulting in a training batch size of 512."
- Note: the advantage is a z-score of the reward within the group: answers better than their siblings are reinforced, worse ones discouraged. This is the honest bridge for the reader (standardizing a variable). Whether "std" is the population or sample standard deviation is not stated; M5 states its assumption. The clipped-ratio and KL terms of Eq. 1 are out of scope (D-002); say only that the update is kept small and the model is kept near a reference model.

### FR10. Rule-based rewards were chosen because learned reward models get "hacked"
- Source: `deepseekai2025r1v2`, pp. 4, 8, 11, 35.
- Quotes: p. 4: "we abstain from applying neural reward models - whether outcome-based or process-based - to reasoning tasks. This decision is predicated on our observation that neural reward models are susceptible to reward hacking during large-scale reinforcement learning." p. 11: "The success of pure RL depends on reliable reward signals. ... such dependable RMs are difficult to construct for certain tasks, such as writing. Consequently, for complex tasks that cannot be effectively evaluated by a reliable reward model, scaling up pure RL methods remains an open challenge." p. 35 (B.5) defines reward hacking: "the phenomenon wherein a model exploits flaws or biases in the reward function, thereby achieving high reward scores without truly aligning with the underlying human intent."
- Note: the bridge is optimizing a proxy metric (Goodhart's law; overfitting to a validation metric). This is the method's main limit: it works where answers can be checked.

### FR25. METR's time horizon: the measure
- Source: `kwa2025measuring` (v4), pp. 1-2; `metr2026horizons` (FAQ).
- Quotes: Abstract (p. 1): "we propose a new metric: 50%-task-completion time horizon, the time humans typically take to complete tasks that AI models can complete with 50% success rate. We first timed humans with relevant domain expertise on a combination of RE-Bench, HCAST, and 66 novel shorter tasks." p. 2: "totaling 170 tasks", "We evaluate the performance of 12 frontier models from 2019 to 2025", "Using methodology inspired by human psychometric studies, we then estimate the duration of tasks that models can complete with 50% success rate". METR page, "Methodological Details": "For each agent, we fit a logistic curve to predict the probability it successfully completes tasks as a function of human task duration. To get the 50%-time horizon (or 80%-time horizon), we find the task duration where the fitted curve intersects with 50% (or 80%) success probability." FAQ: "It's a measure of the difficulty of a task, rather than the time an AI spends to complete the task."
- Paper, p. 5: "we fit time horizons by performing the following logistic regression: p_success(agent, task) = sigma((log h_agent - log t_task) . beta_agent)" and "Compare to Item Response Theory (IRT) [24]. Like in IRT, we use logistic regression to find the task difficulty at which the agent has a 50% chance of success, but unlike IRT, we use difficulty ratings directly based on human baseline time rather than ratings learned from agent performance." p. 3: "we fit a logistic model to find the time horizon at which each AI agent has a 50% chance of success".
- Note: the reader's logistic regression, with log task length as the input; when a task's human time equals the agent's horizon h, the predicted success is sigma(0) = 0.5. This formula is allowed in standard form (D-002) and is the best bridge in the chapter.

### FR33. CLIP (2021): images and captions in one embedding space
- Source: `radford2021learning`, pp. 1, 2, 4, 5, 6, 7.

### FR40. Polysemantic neurons and superposition (2022-2023)
- Sources: `bricken2023towards` (introduction); `elhage2022toy` (opening section).
- Quotes: Bricken et al.: "many neurons are polysemantic: they respond to mixtures of seemingly unrelated inputs. In a small language model we discuss in this paper, a single neuron responds to a mixture of academic citations, English dialogue, HTTP requests, and Korean text." "One potential cause of polysemanticity is superposition, a hypothesized phenomenon where a neural network represents more independent "features" of the data than it has neurons by assigning each feature its own linear combination of neurons." - Note: the source itself makes the PCA bridge: dense features give a PCA-like answer; sparse features let more directions share the space at the cost of "interference". "Hypothesized" is the source's word.

## Disputed or inconsistent points

### FRD1. Do reasoning models "collapse", or did the test design cause it? (2025)
Shojaee et al. (FR16) report a collapse in accuracy beyond a puzzle size, reduced reasoning effort near that point, and no gain from being given the algorithm. Lawsen (FR17) argues that much of the collapse comes from output-length limits, all-or-nothing scoring of long move lists, and unsolvable River Crossing instances, and reports that models write a correct recursive program for 15 disks; his trials were few ("Due to budget constraints") and the comment is not peer reviewed. Shojaee et al.'s v3 (NeurIPS 2025) answers: failures start well within context limits (around 40 of 255 moves for 8 disks), temperature-zero runs collapse too, and River Crossing collapses already at N = 3. They dropped River Crossing N >= 6 and Tower of Hanoi N > 12, which concedes part of the critique. The chapter should present it as an open argument about what puzzle tests measure, as of 2025-11, and say that both sides agree the models can write the algorithm but often fail to execute long move sequences by hand.

### FRD2. How fast are agent time horizons growing, and at what reliability?
METR's paper gives 207 days (95% CI 166-240) for 2019-2025 and 172 days for 2023-2025 non-SWAA tasks (FR26); TH1.1 gives 196 days stitched, 131 days post-2023, 89 days post-2024 (FR28); the YAML of September 2026 gives 187.8 days stitched and 128.7 [104.4, 158.0] from 2023 (FR29). The International AI Safety Report 2026 cites Kwa et al. as "30 minutes around 80% of the time" with doubling "every seven months" (FR50 source section), while METR's 50% horizons for the same period are hours: the two describe different reliability thresholds. The 80% horizon is "4-6x shorter" in the paper (FR26), but for the newest model in the YAML (Claude Opus 4.6) it is about 10x shorter (M1), and the newest point estimates have confidence intervals spanning a factor of ten; METR calls estimates above 16 hours unreliable. The chapter should give one doubling time with its period and threshold, say the recent trend may be faster, and not extrapolate past what METR itself says.

### FRD3. Benchmarks rising vs measured usefulness
METR's own RCT found experienced developers 19% slower with early-2025 AI tools, against their forecast of 24% faster (FR30), at a time when the same organization measured horizons near one to two hours (FR26). METR lists what the RCT does not show. Present it as evidence that benchmark progress and workplace effect can diverge, dated early 2025, not as a verdict on 2026 tools.

### FRD4. Benchmark scores change meaning: SWE-bench and OSWorld
SWE-bench Verified went from the "standard metric" to "no longer suitable" in OpenAI's own words (FR21); the score history mixes the original set (1.96%) with the Verified subset (33.2% and later). For OSWorld, Anthropic's two posts of the same day give the next-best system as 7.8% and 7.7% (FR23); in 2026, vendors report "OSWorld 2.1 (offline set, partial)" (Fable 5.2 announcement), a different test from the 2024 benchmark whose human baseline was 72.36% (FR22). Never put 2024 OSWorld and 2026 "OSWorld 2.1" numbers on one axis.

### FRD5. Are sparse autoencoders the way into models?
Anthropic's work (FR41) presents SAE features as interpretable and causally effective (steering); Anthropic's own limits sections (FR42) say coverage is incomplete and the objective is a proxy; Google DeepMind's team (FR43) found SAEs underperformed simple linear probes on a practical task and deprioritized them, while saying SAEs are not useless. Present both as of 2025, without a winner.

### FRD6. How large are the risks?
The CAIS statement (2023) and Narayanan and Kapoor (2025) take different positions (FR49); the International AI Safety Report records that "experts disagree" on loss of control (2025, p. 102) and on whether scaling continues (2026) (FR47, FR50). The chapter should report the spread, not adjudicate. Vendor frameworks (FR48) are statements of intent by the companies, not independent assessments.

### FRD7. Does scaling continue?
See FR50: the 2026 synthesis lists plateau, continuation, and acceleration as all plausible to 2030. The book's `scale` chapter ends with scaling laws (as of 2022-2024); this chapter should not say scaling has stopped or that it will continue.

### FRD8. What did DeepSeek-R1 cost?
$294K (R1's RL stage, at an assumed $2 per H800 GPU hour) sits on a base model whose final run was priced at $5.576M at the same assumed rate, which "excludes the costs associated with prior research and ablation experiments" (FR13). Neither is the cost of the hardware, the staff, or the failed experiments. Wide public claims that "R1 cost $6 million" or "$294K" are not supported by the papers as worded. Also: the cost table appears in v2 (January 2026); whether v1 (January 2025) printed it was not checked.

### FRD9. "PhD-level" and similar labels
OpenAI's "exceeds human PhD-level accuracy" on GPQA comes with OpenAI's own sentence that this does not mean o1 is "more capable than a PhD in all respects" (FR4). The International AI Safety Report 2026 uses "jagged" for the same pattern (FR50). Use both or neither.

### FRD10. DeepSeek-V4's arXiv identifier and date do not match
The abstract page of arXiv 2606.19348 shows "[v1] Sun, 26 Apr 2026", but a 2606 identifier normally means June 2026. Not resolved; the drafter should cite the model card for sizes or re-check the arXiv record at drafting.

### FRD11. Energy: tiny per prompt, large in aggregate
Google's median text prompt is 0.24 Wh (FR46), and the IEA says simple AI text queries replacing all web searches would use less than 4 TWh a year (FR45); yet data-centre demand is projected to roughly double to 2030 (FR45). Both can be true: per-task efficiency improves fast while use grows faster, and long reasoning and agent runs are not median prompts. The IEA 2025 and 2026 projections for 2030 (945 and 950 TWh) differ in base year and wording; cite each with its date.

### FRD12. Anthropomorphic words
"Thinks" (OpenAI), "thinking" (Anthropic), "Large Reasoning Models" and "The Illusion of Thinking" (Apple), "aha moment" (DeepSeek), "scheming" (Apollo). The chapter should define these as the vendors' or authors' words for generating intermediate text, or for behaviors in tests, and use them in quotation marks when attributing.

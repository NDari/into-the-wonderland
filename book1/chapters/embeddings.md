[The previous chapter](#prediction) ended with two failures of counting. The table of contexts grows as the vocabulary raised to the length of the context, and the model has no idea that words resemble each other: what it learns about "cat" teaches it nothing about "dog". This chapter is about the fix, and it is the most important new idea in the book: **represent each word as a point in a space, so that words used in similar ways are close together**. The position of a word, a list of a few dozen to a few hundred numbers, is learned from text.

The idea has two roots. One is a claim from linguistics in the 1950s, that words with similar meanings occur in similar company. The other is a standard tool of statistics: the matrix factorization behind PCA, applied to word counts. Neural networks joined them in the 2000s by learning the positions as a side effect of predicting words, and by 2013 that could be done on billions of words in a day.

## Every word equally far from every other

How does a counting model, or the networks of the earlier chapters, see a word? As a **one-hot vector**: a list as long as the vocabulary, with a 1 in the word's own position and 0 everywhere else. Elman's network in [the chapter on networks with structure](#structure) used codes of this kind. In such vectors, every pair of words is the same Euclidean distance apart, $\sqrt{2}$ [@goodfellow2016deep, p. 459].

<!-- D-009: code/checks/embeddings.py, Example 1 -->

The distance between "cat" and "dog" is 1.414, and so is the distance between "cat" and "car". Measured by the angle between them, any two one-hot vectors are perpendicular. With the vocabulary of about 400,000 words used later in this chapter, each word's vector is a single 1 among roughly 400,000 zeros. Nothing in the representation says that two words are alike.

The alternative is a short list of numbers for each word, in which every number carries some information and similar words get similar lists. Such a list is a **dense vector**, and when it represents a word it is called a **word embedding** [@goodfellow2016deep, p. 459]. [The destination chapter](#destination) introduced these as the rows of an LLM's embedding table. This chapter explains where the idea came from and how such vectors are learned.

## Similarity as closeness

Before asking how to get good vectors, fix how to compare them. The standard measure is the **cosine** of the angle between two vectors: 1 if they point the same way, 0 if they are perpendicular, -1 if opposite. A current textbook (as of its 2026-08 draft) calls it the most popular such measure [@jurafsky2026embeddings, p. 22]. In its standard form,

$$\cos(a, b) = \frac{a \cdot b}{\|a\| \, \|b\|}$$

where $a \cdot b$ is the dot product of the vectors $a$ and $b$ (multiply matching entries and add), and $\|a\|$ and $\|b\|$ are their lengths. Dividing by the lengths means that only the direction counts.

<!-- D-009: code/checks/embeddings.py, Example 2 -->

With three made-up vectors of three numbers each, chosen so that "cat" and "dog" are similar and "car" is not, the cosine of "cat" and "dog" is 0.990 and of "cat" and "car" 0.303. Doubling the length of the "cat" vector leaves its cosine with "dog" at 0.990. In document retrieval the cosine compares documents by their word counts, where ignoring length stops long documents from looking similar to everything.

## The distributional idea

Where would similar vectors for similar words come from? The linguist Zellig Harris proposed an answer in 1954. He defined the distribution of an element, such as a word, as "the sum of all its environments" [@harris1954distributional, p. 146], and wrote that if A and B differ more in meaning than A and C, their distributions will often differ more too: "difference of meaning correlates with difference of distribution" [@harris1954distributional, p. 156].

His example was "oculist" and "eye-doctor", which turn up in nearly the same environments, while "oculist" occurs in many sentences where "lawyer" does not, such as "I've had my eyes examined by the same oculist for twenty years" [@harris1954distributional, p. 156]. He made it graded: when two words share some environments and not others, as "oculist" and "lawyer" do, he called their meanings different, with the size of the difference in meaning roughly tracking the size of the difference in their environments [@harris1954distributional, p. 157]. Harris was careful: he wrote "often" and "correlates", and he noted that the person who suggested the example held that differences of meaning cannot be shown to match differences of distribution [@harris1954distributional, p. 156].

<!-- D-009: code/checks/embeddings.py, Example 7 -->

To see the idea in numbers, invent a small table: how often each of three words appears in five environments, such as "examined my eyes", "drafted the will", or "went to court". Give "oculist" and "eye-doctor" similar counts and "lawyer" different ones. The cosine of the two eye-related words' count vectors is 0.989; of "oculist" and "lawyer", 0.137. (The counts are invented; the illustration is of Harris's statement, not his data.) Similar distributions give similar vectors.

The most quoted line in this area comes from the linguist J. R. Firth in 1957: "You shall know a word by the company it keeps!" [@firth1957synopsis, p. 11]. Its context is narrower than its fame. Firth was writing about **collocation**, the words a word habitually appears next to, and his example was "ass" in "You silly ass!" and "Don't be such an ass!" [@firth1957synopsis, p. 11]. He added on the same page that collocation is not the kind of context that carries a word's whole conceptual meaning [@firth1957synopsis, p. 11]. A 2022 close reading argues that Firth and Harris held two separate theories of meaning that differ in many respects [@brunila2022company, p. 1]. Modern textbooks pair them under the name **distributional hypothesis**: "Words that occur in similar contexts tend to have similar meanings" [@jurafsky2026embeddings, p. 1]. The name is a later label. How far the builders of word vectors drew on the two linguists is taken up in [the credit appendix](#credit-embeddings).

## Counting meaning: latent semantic analysis

One early route to word vectors came from counting, and from an older statistical tool. In 1990, Scott Deerwester and colleagues described a method for document retrieval that breaks a large table of terms by documents down into about 100 orthogonal factors, which together give an approximation of the original table [@deerwester1990indexing, p. 391]. Their paper's title calls it **latent semantic analysis** (LSA); within the paper the method is latent semantic indexing (LSI), and the two names are used for the same thing. The decomposition is the singular-value decomposition, the same matrix factorization that underlies PCA, applied to a table of how often each word appears in each document.

The problem they set out to solve was vocabulary mismatch. People describe the same thing with different words: in an earlier study the authors cite, two people describing the same well-known object picked the same main keyword less than 20% of the time [@deerwester1990indexing, p. 392]. A search for "human" misses a document that says "user". Keeping only the main factors of the count table pulls together words that appear in the same kinds of documents, even if never in the same one.

<!-- D-009: code/checks/embeddings.py, Example 3 -->

The paper's own small example, which the authors "carefully chose" so that the method would work well [@deerwester1990indexing, p. 396], shows the idea. Take nine titles of technical memos, five about human-computer interaction and four about graph theory, and count twelve words in them [@deerwester1990indexing, p. 396]. "Human" and "user" never appear in the same title, so the cosine of their count vectors is exactly 0. After keeping only the first two factors, it is 0.888. "Trees" and "graph", which do share titles, go from 0.667 to 0.999, while "human" and "trees" go from 0 to -0.330. The two factors keep 57% of the table's sum of squares, and they separate the two topics.

![Deerwester and colleagues' twelve terms in the two-factor space computed from their nine-title example. The graph-theory terms (red) sit together at the top right; the other terms (blue) lie lower down. "Human" and "user" point in nearly the same direction from the origin (the right end of the horizontal line), although they never appear in the same title. "Response" and "time" occur in exactly the same titles, so they fall on the same point.](../../figures/embeddings-lsa.svg){#fig:embeddings-lsa}

On real collections the results were mixed. On 1,033 medical abstracts, LSA's precision was 0.51 against 0.45 for plain word matching, an improvement of 13% [@deerwester1990indexing, p. 400]; on a second collection the two tied, and an established retrieval system did reliably better than LSA [@deerwester1990indexing, p. 403].

The authors also named the method's built-in weakness, and it applies to everything in this chapter. "[E]very term is represented as just one point in the space. That is, a word with more than one entirely different meaning (e.g., "bank"), is represented as a weighted average of the different meanings" [@deerwester1990indexing, p. 405].

## Learning vectors by predicting

A second route to word vectors came from neural networks, and it began before words. In the 1986 Nature letter that made backpropagation famous, Rumelhart, Hinton, and Williams trained a network on family relationships, with each person given as a one-hot input. The network's first hidden layer learned "distributed representations of people": one unit separated the English family from its Italian counterpart, another encoded generation, a third the branch of the family [@rumelhart1986learning, p. 535]. Nobody had told the network about nationality or generation. It found them because they helped with the task. The representations are called **distributed** because each person's code is spread across several units, each standing for a feature, rather than held in a single unit as in a one-hot code [@lecun2015deep, p. 441].

Yoshua Bengio and colleagues applied the idea to the problem [the previous chapter](#prediction) ended on, in a conference paper of 2000 (printed in the 2001 proceedings) [@bengio2000neural] and a journal version in 2003. Their answer to the curse of dimensionality was to learn a distributed representation for words: then each sentence in the training data teaches the model something about an exponential number of sentences close to it in meaning [@bengio2003neural, p. 1137]. Their recipe had three steps: give every word in the vocabulary a feature vector, compute the probability of the next word from the vectors of the previous words, and learn the vectors and the parameters of the probability function together [@bengio2003neural, p. 1139]. A sentence the model has never seen then gets a high probability if its words have vectors near those of the words in a sentence it has seen [@bengio2003neural, p. 1137].

The vectors were stored in a table of free parameters with |V| rows and m columns, where |V| is the size of the vocabulary and m the number of entries in each vector: one row per word, shared by every position in the context [@bengio2003neural, p. 1141]. That is the embedding table of [the destination chapter](#destination), and in Bengio's model, as in an LLM, it starts random and is trained with everything else. The vectors were short: m = 30, 60, or 100 numbers, against a vocabulary of about 17,000 words [@bengio2003neural, p. 1139].

Here the link from LSA to learned vectors comes with a twist. The authors tried, without success, to use fixed features for each word instead: the first principal components of how often the word co-occurs with the words around it in text, which they likened to what LSI does with documents [@bengio2003neural, p. 1141]. In their model, vectors learned for the task worked better than fixed vectors from PCA of counts.

The model beat the best smoothed n-grams. On about a million words of the Brown corpus, its best version had a test perplexity of 252, against 312 for the best n-gram model [@bengio2003neural, p. 1149], a difference the authors put at about 24% [@bengio2003neural, p. 1148]. On about 14 million words of news, the figures were 109 against 117 [@bengio2003neural, p. 1150]. (The best neural results averaged the network's predictions with a trigram's, and the authors' figure of about 8% for the news corpus is a little more than the printed perplexities give, about 7%.) It was expensive: on the news corpus, the authors ran only five epochs, which took over three weeks on 40 CPUs [@bengio2003neural, p. 1148].

## Vectors as the goal

Bengio and colleagues wanted a better language model, and got word vectors along the way. In 2008, Ronan Collobert and Jason Weston turned that around: they wanted the vectors. They trained a network on every window of text in the English Wikipedia to tell a real window from one in which the middle word had been replaced by a random word [@collobert2008unified, p. 5]. The vectors it learned could then be used as features for other tasks, such as tagging parts of speech. Training took about a week on one computer, and the authors judged the vectors in the network's lookup table excellent, even for rare words [@collobert2008unified, p. 7]. Their table of nearest neighbors shows why: the closest words to "france" were spain, italy, russia, poland, england, and denmark, and those to "xbox" included playstation, dreamcast, and snes [@collobert2008unified, p. 6].

Accounts differ on who should get credit for neural word vectors; [the credit appendix](#credit-embeddings) sets them out.

## word2vec: cheap at scale

In 2013, Tomas Mikolov and colleagues asked what happens if most of the network is thrown away. Most of the cost of a neural language model, they observed, comes from its non-linear hidden layer, the very part that gives neural networks their appeal; so they tried simpler models, which might fit the data less precisely but could possibly be trained on far more of it [@mikolov2013efficient, p. 4]. Their two models are little more than two tables of word vectors, one for the input words and one for the output. One, **skip-gram**, uses a word's vector to predict the words around it; the other, **CBOW** (continuous bag of words), predicts a word from the average of its neighbors' vectors [@mikolov2013efficient, pp. 4-5]. The software was released as word2vec.

The result was speed: high-quality word vectors from a data set of 1.6 billion words in less than a day [@mikolov2013efficient, p. 1]. Their largest runs used a Google News corpus of about 6 billion tokens with a vocabulary of a million words [@mikolov2013efficient, p. 6]. A follow-up paper reported that an optimized implementation on a single machine could train on more than 100 billion words in one day [@mikolov2013distributed, p. 1].

One trick in the follow-up reuses a classic model. Predicting which of a million words appears next to a given word means a softmax over a million classes, which is slow. **Negative sampling** replaces it with a set of yes-or-no questions: is this word a real neighbor, or a word drawn at random? Each question is answered with logistic regression on the two words' vectors, except that here the vectors themselves are learned too, not fixed features, with k random words drawn for each real one [@mikolov2013distributed, pp. 3-4], k typically between 2 and 20. Ordinary logistic regression, applied a few billion times, produces word vectors. The authors also explained why their vectors capture meaning, in terms Harris would have recognized: because the vectors are trained to predict the surrounding words, they "can be seen as representing the distribution of the context in which a word appears" [@mikolov2013distributed, p. 7].

## GloVe: counting and predicting together

Were the predicted vectors really different from counted ones? In 2014, the authors of a model called GloVe argued that the two approaches were closer than they looked, and combined them. They described GloVe as combining the advantages of the two main families of models, global matrix factorization (such as LSA) and local context windows (such as skip-gram), and trained it on the counts of how often each pair of words appears near each other [@pennington2014glove, p. 1532]. They trained it on several corpora, including one of 6 billion tokens from Wikipedia and a news archive, with a vocabulary of the 400,000 most frequent words [@pennington2014glove, p. 1538].

The evidence went both ways. Earlier in 2014, a comparison had reported that predicting models won "a thorough and resounding victory" over counting ones [@baroni2014dont, p. 238]. But later the same year an analysis showed that skip-gram with negative sampling implicitly factorizes a word-context matrix of counts, suitably weighted [@levy2014neural, p. 1], and in 2015 a study found that much of the newer embeddings' advantage came from design choices and the tuning of hyperparameters, not from the embedding algorithms themselves, and that no single approach was better across the board [@levy2015improving, p. 211].

So the link between LSA and learned vectors needs one correction. LSA factorizes a table of word-by-document counts. Skip-gram with negative sampling and GloVe work, in effect, with a table of word-by-neighboring-word counts, weighted with care, and they learn from far more text. The idea of a word's meaning as its position, found from the company it keeps, runs through all three.

## Analogies and their limits

The most famous property of word vectors is the analogy. In 2013, Mikolov and two colleagues reported that their vectors learned the male/female relationship automatically, so that "King - Man + Woman" gives a vector very close to "Queen" [@mikolov2013linguistic, p. 746]. Their vectors came from a recurrent network language model, not from word2vec. The word2vec paper adopted the idea and built a test set of 19,544 questions of the form "a is to b as c is to what?" [@mikolov2013efficient, p. 6]; its best vectors answered 65.6% of them exactly right [@mikolov2013efficient, p. 9].

The famous equation is not quite what happens. <!-- D-009: code/checks/embeddings.py, Examples 4 and 8 --> Compute king minus man plus woman with the GloVe vectors, and the nearest word to the result is "king" itself (cosine 0.886); "queen" comes second (0.861). The word2vec authors searched for the nearest word but discarded the input question words from the search [@mikolov2013efficient, p. 5]. The reason is simple: if the difference between "woman" and "man" is small compared with the spread of the vectors, adding it barely moves "king". A made-up example with three numbers per word shows the effect. If the step from "man" to "woman" is as large as the step from "king" to "queen", the result lands exactly on "queen". If it is a third of that size, the result's cosine is 0.958 with "king" and 0.904 with "queen": "king" is nearest. A 2016 analysis found that without the exclusion, the nearest neighbor of the result was the word the offset was added to (here, "king") in 93% of the cases [@linzen2016issues, p. 16], and a 2020 study found that allowing the input words to be returned dropped accuracy on the standard test from 0.71 to 0.21 [@nissim2020fair, p. 491].

Analogies also work better for some relations than others. On the word2vec test set, accuracy varied by category from 0.13 to 0.90, and for plurals, simply returning the nearest neighbor of the word the offset is added to, ignoring the other two, was right 70% of the time [@linzen2016issues, p. 15]. Another 2014 analysis showed that traditional distributional representations, built from counts, recover these relations about as well [@levy2014linguistic, p. 171]. On a harder test set with more kinds of relation, as one 2017 study reports, even the best-performing GloVe model scores under 30% [@rogers2017too, p. 136]. Analogies show that the vectors encode some relations as consistent directions. They do not show that the vectors understand relations.

The same arithmetic exposed something less welcome. In 2016, a study showed that word2vec vectors trained on news text exhibit gender stereotypes to what its authors called a disturbing extent, and that the analogy method answered "man is to computer programmer as woman is to x" with x = homemaker [@bolukbasi2016man, pp. 1, 3]. With the GloVe vectors, doctor minus man plus woman gives "doctor" (0.896) first and "nurse" (0.842) second. The 2020 study pointed out that because the standard method cannot return an input word, it cannot answer "doctor", so such examples overstate the bias [@nissim2020fair, p. 492]. Its authors also stressed that human biases are present in word embeddings and need to be addressed, but that analogies are not an accurate tool for the job [@nissim2020fair, p. 487]. Both points hold. Vectors learned from human text absorb the associations in that text, and the most quoted demonstrations exaggerate them. As of a 2026-08 textbook draft, methods to remove such bias may lessen it but do not get rid of it, and the problem is still open [@jurafsky2026embeddings, p. 20].

## One vector per word

The weakness Deerwester named in 1990 survived every improvement in this chapter. In the GloVe vectors, the nearest words to "bank" are banks, securities, banking, investment, exchange, and financial: the riverbank is nowhere. One word, one vector, whatever the sentence. The textbook draft cited above calls these **static embeddings**: each word in the vocabulary gets one fixed embedding [@jurafsky2026embeddings, p. 11].

The fix arrives with the transformer. The embedding table of an LLM still gives each token one starting vector, but the layers above it change that vector according to the surrounding text, so that "bank" in a sentence about rivers ends up somewhere different from "bank" in a sentence about loans. [The chapters on attention](#attention) and [the anatomy of a transformer](#anatomy) show how. Two other questions are left open here. What counts as a "word" in a model's vocabulary is the subject of [the chapter on tokens](#tokens). How a sentence, rather than a word, gets a vector is the subject of [the next chapter](#seq2seq).

## What to carry forward

- One-hot vectors make every word equally different from every other. Dense vectors (embeddings) place words in a space where similar words are close, usually measured by cosine.
- Words used in similar contexts tend to have similar meanings. This is the distributional hypothesis, a later name for ideas that Harris (1954) and Firth (1957) stated in different forms.
- Latent semantic analysis (1990) got word vectors by applying the SVD behind PCA to word-by-document counts. It helped retrieval on one test collection but not another, and its authors named the method's weakness: one point per word.
- Neural language models (Bengio and colleagues, 2000; journal version 2003) learned an embedding table along with the rest of the model, as a side effect of predicting words. Collobert and Weston (2008) made the vectors the goal; word2vec (2013) made them cheap to train on billions of words.
- Predicting and counting turned out to be closer than they looked: skip-gram with negative sampling in effect factorizes a weighted table of word-neighbor counts, and GloVe does so explicitly.
- Vector arithmetic captures some relations (the nearest word to "king - man + woman", other than the three inputs, is "queen"), works unevenly, and also reflects the biases in the training text.
- Static embeddings give each word one vector regardless of context; transformers fix that by adjusting each token's vector in context.

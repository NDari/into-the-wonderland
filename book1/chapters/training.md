# How an LLM is trained {#training}

[The chapter on scale](#scale) treated a training run as three numbers: parameters, tokens, and compute. This chapter opens the run itself. It follows the process from raw web pages to a finished set of weights, as a step-by-step procedure: collect text, clean it, remove duplicates, cut it into tokens, and then repeat one training step tens or hundreds of thousands of times on hundreds or thousands of GPUs, while things break. It ends with a real loss curve and with a natural question: how much of the training text ends up inside the model.

This is pretraining only. What is done to a pretrained model afterward, to make it follow instructions, is the subject of [the chapter on teaching models to follow instructions](#instructions). The optimizer, learning-rate schedules, and the training loop in general are in [the toolkit chapter](#toolkit); this chapter shows them at scale.

## Collecting text

Most of the text comes from the web, and most web text used for training comes from one source. Common Crawl, a non-profit founded in 2007, gives its archive of crawled web pages away, free to anyone [@commoncrawl2026home]. It crawls the web regularly and publishes what it finds. As of 2026-11, its latest crawl, from October 2026, holds 2.16 billion web pages [@commoncrawl2026october]. That is a sample of the web at one moment, not the web itself.

Raw crawl text is mostly not usable prose. In 2019 the T5 authors reported that most of it is not natural language at all; what it largely holds is gibberish and boilerplate, such as menus, error messages, and text repeated from elsewhere [@raffel2020exploring, p. 6]. Every model builder filters it, and the filters come from the ordinary toolkit of text processing and classification.

**Rules.** The C4 corpus behind T5 used plain rules anyone could apply to a page by hand. Drop every line that does not end with a mark that closes a sentence, such as a period or a question mark. Drop every page of fewer than 5 sentences and every line of fewer than 3 words. Drop every page that contains a word from a list of offensive words, and every page that contains a curly bracket, a character that many programming languages use and ordinary prose does not [@raffel2020exploring, p. 6]. The result was about 750 GB of English text [@raffel2020exploring, p. 7].

**Classifiers.** GPT-3's filter was an ordinary logistic regression. Its builders let the original WebText stand for high-quality documents and trained the classifier to separate such documents from unfiltered Common Crawl, on features computed with Spark's standard tokenizer and HashingTF [@brown2020language, p. 43]: hashed word counts in, a probability of "looks like good text" out. Documents were then kept at random, with a probability that rose with the classifier's score, so that some unusual text survived [@brown2020language, p. 43]. LLaMA's pipeline used fastText, a fast linear text classifier, to identify languages, an n-gram language model to filter out low-quality content, the model of [the prediction chapter](#prediction) now used as a judge, and a linear model trained to tell pages cited by Wikipedia from random ones [@touvron2023llama, p. 2]. By 2024, Llama 3's pipeline also asked an earlier language model to judge quality: the authors described quality requirements and had Llama 2's chat model label web documents, then trained classifiers on those labels [@grattafiori2024llama, p. 5].

**A mixture, not a pile.** The sources are not used in proportion to their size. GPT-3's training drew 60% of its examples from filtered Common Crawl, 22% from WebText2, 8% from each of two book collections, and 3% from Wikipedia; WebText2, one of the book collections, and Wikipedia were read two to three times, while Common Crawl and the other book collection were read less than once. In the authors' view, this amounts to tolerating a little overfitting to get better training data [@brown2020language, pp. 8-9]. It is stratified sampling with chosen, not proportional, strata. Llama 3's final mix, counted in tokens, was roughly 50% general knowledge, 25% mathematics and reasoning, 17% code, and 8% multilingual text [@grattafiori2024llama, p. 6].

Filters have side effects. A 2021 audit of C4 clustered the excluded documents with k-means on a PCA projection of their word-frequency vectors, and found that the list of offensive words had removed far more than offensive text. Of the identity terms the audit tracked, those for sexual orientations (lesbian, gay, heterosexual, homosexual, bisexual) made a document more likely to be filtered out than those for racial and ethnic identities. Documents that a classifier assigned to African American English and to Hispanic-aligned English were filtered out at rates of 42% and 32%, far higher than for those assigned to White-aligned English and to other English (6.2% and 7.2%) [@dodge2021documenting, pp. 1291-1292]. The audit also found that the most common website in the cleaned corpus was a patent site, which uses machine translation for patents filed in other languages [@dodge2021documenting, pp. 1288-1289].

The largest commercial models do not publish their data. Open groups have since rebuilt the process in public. FineWeb, a 15-trillion-token English data set from 96 Common Crawl snapshots, documents every step and tests each one by training small models [@penedo2024fineweb, pp. 1, 3-4]. Its authors point out that even leading open models, Llama 3 and Mixtral among them, have not released their pretraining data, and that outsiders know very little about how those data were assembled [@penedo2024fineweb, p. 1]. Dolma, three trillion tokens, started from about 200 TB of raw text, which curation reduced to 11 TB [@soldaini2024dolma, p. 15726].

## Removing duplicates

The web repeats itself. A 2022 study found one 61-word sequence that appeared 61,036 times, word for word, in C4's training set [@lee2022deduplicating, p. 8424]; most of the repeats the authors looked at were pages produced by software, such as sports results [@lee2022deduplicating, p. 8428]. **Deduplication** finds and removes such copies, either as exact repeated strings or as near-duplicate documents, found with a hashing technique, MinHash, that makes similar documents likely to get matching fingerprints [@lee2022deduplicating, pp. 8424, 8426]. Statisticians call the same problem record linkage. Models trained on the deduplicated data reproduced memorized text a tenth as often, and reached the same accuracy or better in fewer training steps [@lee2022deduplicating, p. 8424]; the authors saw no drawbacks [@lee2022deduplicating, p. 8425].

More deduplication is not always better, though. FineWeb's builders deduplicated all 96 snapshots against each other and found little improvement over not deduplicating; in one old snapshot, the 10% of the data that survived turned out to be worse than the 90% that was thrown away [@penedo2024fineweb, p. 5]. Deduplicating each snapshot separately worked better. Llama 3's team removed lines that appeared more than six times in a group of 30 million documents, and noted that this threw out often-repeated good text along with leftover boilerplate, yet still improved results [@grattafiori2024llama, p. 5].

## From text to training examples

The cleaned text is tokenized once ([the chapter on tokens](#tokens)), and from then on the corpus is measured in tokens: about 1.4 trillion for LLaMA [@touvron2023llama, pp. 2-3], about 15 trillion for Llama 3 [@grattafiori2024llama, p. 1]. Counts made with different tokenizers are not in the same unit.

Training examples are windows of a fixed length. Documents are joined end to end, separated by an end-of-text token, and cut into windows: GPT-3 always trained on full windows of 2,048 tokens, and put several short documents together in one window to fill it [@brown2020language, p. 43]. GPT-3 did not stop attention from crossing between documents in a window and relied on the separator token; Llama 3 masked attention so that a token could not attend to tokens of another document in the same window, and found this mattered mainly for very long sequences [@grattafiori2024llama, p. 6]. Practice differs.

## One training step

![One training step, end to end. A batch of windows of packed text goes through the model, which predicts the next token at every position; the cross-entropy at every position is averaged into one loss; backpropagation gives a gradient for every parameter; the gradients are averaged across the GPUs that each processed part of the batch; the optimizer updates the parameters; and every so often a checkpoint is saved.](../../figures/training-step.svg){#fig:training-step}

A step is the minibatch gradient descent of [the toolkit chapter](#toolkit), with large numbers. The model reads a batch of windows. Thanks to the causal mask of [the anatomy chapter](#anatomy), one forward pass gives a prediction for the next token at every position of every window. The loss at each position is the cross-entropy of [the prediction chapter](#prediction): minus the log of the probability the model gave to the token that actually came next. The losses are averaged into one number. Training logs usually use natural logarithms, so the loss is in nats; dividing by 0.693 (the natural log of 2) turns it into bits. Backpropagation gives a gradient for every parameter, and the optimizer updates them all.

Batches are measured in tokens. At its full batch size, BLOOM, a 176-billion-parameter model trained in 2022, took 2,048 windows of 2,048 tokens per step: 4,194,304 predictions in one update [@lescao2022bloom, p. 21]. LLaMA's models used batches of 4 million tokens [@touvron2023llama, p. 3]; at that size, LLaMA's 1.4 trillion tokens take 350,000 steps. Llama 3's largest model ended with batches of 16 million tokens [@grattafiori2024llama, p. 10]. Batches often start smaller and grow: Llama 3's team used a smaller batch early in training for stability, and a larger one later for efficiency [@grattafiori2024llama, p. 14]. Learning-rate schedules are likewise set in tokens: GPT-3 warmed up over the first 375 million tokens and decayed over 260 billion [@brown2020language, p. 43].

## Numbers and memory

<!-- D-009: code/checks/training.py, Example 4 -->

Every parameter, gradient, and activation is a floating-point number, and how many bits each one takes matters at this scale. Statistics software of the 2000s used 64-bit or 32-bit numbers. Training (as of 2024) uses 16-bit numbers wherever it safely can, because they take half the memory of 32-bit ones and, on the GPUs of 2017-2018, were computed two to eight times faster [@micikevicius2018mixed, p. 1]. Sixteen bits are not much. The standard 16-bit format, float16, cannot represent any number larger than 65,504, and near 1 its values are about 0.001 apart: adding 1/4096 to 1 gives exactly 1 [@micikevicius2018mixed, pp. 3, 5]. A small weight update can simply vanish.

Two fixes are standard (as of 2024). **Mixed precision** keeps a 32-bit master copy of the weights that receives the updates, and does the bulk of the arithmetic in 16 bits [@micikevicius2018mixed, p. 1]. And a second 16-bit format, **bfloat16**, gives up precision to keep the range of 32-bit numbers: it can represent numbers as large as $3 \times 10^{38}$, at the cost of a spacing of about 0.008 near 1 [@kalamkar2019study, pp. 1, 4]. Google named it after its research group, Google Brain, and reported that, in its experience on its own chips, it did the job as well as 32-bit numbers, faster and in less memory [@wang2019bfloat16]. In earlier trials of a 104-billion-parameter model on older GPUs, run in float16, BLOOM's team saw the arithmetic become unstable and training diverge beyond recovery, and hypothesized that float16 was the cause. The final run, in bfloat16, had no such trouble, though the team listed several other changes that might also have helped [@lescao2022bloom, p. 20; @bigscience2022chronicles].

<!-- D-009: code/checks/training.py, Example 3 -->

Memory sets hard limits. The accounting of ZeRO, a 2020 paper on reducing memory in training, for training with Adam in mixed precision is 16 bytes per parameter: 2 for the 16-bit weights, 2 for their gradients, and 12 for the 32-bit master copy and Adam's two running averages [@rajbhandari2020zero, pp. 7-8]. For GPT-2's 1.5 billion parameters that is 24 GB, more than fit on a single 32 GB GPU of 2019 once working memory is added [@rajbhandari2020zero, p. 7]. For a 405-billion-parameter model it is 6,480 GB, the memory of at least 81 GPUs of 80 GB each, before counting the activations of a single batch.

## Many GPUs

So the work is split, in three basic ways. Data parallelism gives each of several devices its own full copy of the model and its own share of each batch, and brings the copies back into agreement after every step: the minibatch of [the toolkit chapter](#toolkit), spread over machines, with gradients averaged. Tensor parallelism cuts each layer into pieces held on different devices. Pipeline parallelism divides the stack of layers among GPUs, so that each holds only some of them [@lescao2022bloom, p. 19]. Large runs combine all three, and Llama 3 added a fourth, splitting long inputs [@grattafiori2024llama, p. 10].

The hardware for a few runs, each with its own date:

- LLaMA's 65-billion-parameter model (2023) ran on 2,048 NVIDIA A100 GPUs at about 380 tokens per second per GPU, which works out to approximately 21 days for its 1.4 trillion tokens [@touvron2023llama, p. 4]. <!-- D-009: code/checks/training.py, Example 1 --> The arithmetic checks out at 20.8 days.
- BLOOM (2022) took about 3.5 months and 1,082,990 compute hours, on 384 NVIDIA A100 GPUs with spare nodes kept in reserve [@lescao2022bloom, p. 18].
- Llama 3's 405-billion-parameter model (2024) trained on up to 16,384 H100 GPUs, each rated at 700 watts [@grattafiori2024llama, p. 9].

Things break. <!-- D-009: code/checks/training.py, Example 5 --> During a 54-day stretch of Llama 3's pretraining, its team counted 466 job interruptions. Of these, 419 were unexpected, about eight a day, and about 78% of those were traced to confirmed hardware faults, with faulty GPUs the largest single cause [@grattafiori2024llama, p. 13]. Because all GPUs work in lockstep, one failed GPU may mean starting the whole job again [@grattafiori2024llama, p. 13]. Even so, effective training time stayed above 90% [@grattafiori2024llama, p. 13]. When the GPUs paused and resumed together, the data center's power draw could swing by tens of megawatts in an instant, testing what the power grid could take [@grattafiori2024llama, p. 14]. BLOOM's team saw one or two GPU failures a week on average [@lescao2022bloom, p. 21].

The defense is the **checkpoint**: a saved copy of the weights and optimizer state, written regularly so that after a crash the run resumes from the last one. BLOOM saved one every three hours, so a crash cost at most three hours of work [@lescao2022bloom, p. 21; @bigscience2022chronicles].

## A real loss curve

<!-- D-009: code/checks/training.py, Example 8 -->

![BLOOM-176B's training loss, from the team's public training log. (a) The whole run: 95,281 steps over 366.5 billion tokens, with the one large spike, the point where the batch reached its full size, and the end of the first pass over the data. (b) The first 2 billion tokens on a logarithmic scale, with the learning rate (right axis): the loss falls from 61.5 to below the level of uniform guessing within 16 steps, while the learning rate warms up. This is training loss on the batches being trained, in nats per BLOOM token; it cannot be compared with any other model's loss.](../../figures/training-loss.svg){#fig:training-loss}

BLOOM was trained by the BigScience Workshop, a research collaboration, and its team published the log of every step [@bigscience2022logs]. The figure shows the training loss: the cross-entropy, in nats, on each batch as it was trained. It is not a held-out validation loss, and since it is measured per token of BLOOM's own vocabulary, its values cannot be compared with any other model's.

The run began at a loss of 61.5, far worse than the 12.4 a model would get by guessing uniformly among BLOOM's 250,680 tokens [@lescao2022bloom, p. 21]; this freshly initialized network started out confidently wrong. Within 16 steps it was below that line. The learning rate rose to its peak of 0.00006 over the first 375 million tokens, as the paper specifies [@lescao2022bloom, pp. 21-22]. The batch grew from 192 windows to its full 2,048 by about 20 billion tokens, a week into the run [@bigscience2022chronicles]. The loss was 2.65 at 10 billion tokens, and after that it fell slowly and steadily: 2.21 at 100 billion, 1.99 at 300 billion, and 1.92 at the end, after 366.5 billion tokens. In bits, the final loss is 2.76 per token.

The one large disturbance came at 97.85 billion tokens, when the loss jumped from 2.35 to 5.10 in a single step, and the size of the gradient jumped to almost a thousand. The team's chronicle recorded it on 12 April 2022 as the run's first loss spike, and noted that the loss recovered in about 30 iterations [@bigscience2022chronicles]. The paper reports "only one loss spike, which the model swiftly recovered from" [@lescao2022bloom, p. 21]; the log also shows a few smaller blips. The log also shows the run's restarts: 39 times, the step number goes back, after an interruption and a resumption from a checkpoint.

## What the weights contain

<!-- D-009: code/checks/training.py, Example 6 -->

A finished model is far smaller than its training text. Llama 3's largest model has 810 GB of weights in 16-bit numbers; its 15.6 trillion training tokens [@grattafiori2024llama, p. 3], at about 3.94 characters each (the figure for English [@grattafiori2024llama, p. 7]), are roughly 61 trillion characters; at about a byte per character, that is about 76 times as large, a rough estimate. The weights cannot hold the corpus word for word. Yet they hold some of it.

In 2021, researchers sampled text from GPT-2 and recovered hundreds of passages copied word for word from its training data, among them public personal information such as names, phone numbers, and email addresses, even though each of those passages occurred in only a single training document [@carlini2021extracting, p. 2633]. This was not ordinary overfitting: GPT-2's loss on its training data was only about 10% below its loss on test data, so a standard train/test check showed nothing alarming [@carlini2021extracting, p. 2635]. And larger models were easier to extract from than smaller ones [@carlini2021extracting, p. 2633]. A 2023 follow-up found that measured memorization rises significantly in three ways: with a larger model, with more copies of an example in the training data, and with a longer prompt in front of the text to be recovered. A 6-billion-parameter model, it found, memorizes at least 1% of its training data [@carlini2023quantifying, p. 1]. Deduplication helps, but some text is memorized even when it appears only a few times, so it cannot stop such leaks entirely [@carlini2023quantifying, p. 5]. Llama 3's authors measured memorization rates for their own models, which they describe as low; their table shows the rates rising with size [@grattafiori2024llama, p. 42]. These studies measure different things: a share of the training data, a share of tests, a share of output. Their numbers should not be set side by side as one statistic.

So the honest answer to "does the model contain its training data?" is neither yes nor no. The weights are far too small to be a copy, and most of what they encode is regularities rather than text. But some text, especially text repeated many times, can be recovered word for word, and the risk grows with the size of the model.

## What to carry forward

- Training text comes mostly from web crawls, filtered by rules, by classifiers (including plain logistic regression), and by earlier language models, then mixed from several sources in chosen proportions. Filters remove more than intended.
- Deduplication removes repeated text and reduces memorization, but more of it is not always better.
- The corpus is tokenized once and cut into fixed-length windows. One step predicts the next token at every position of every window in a batch of millions of tokens, averages the cross-entropy (in nats), and updates every parameter.
- Training uses 16-bit numbers with a 32-bit master copy; bfloat16 keeps the range of 32-bit numbers. Training with Adam needs about 16 bytes per parameter.
- The work is split across thousands of GPUs by data, tensor, and pipeline parallelism. Hardware fails often; checkpoints limit the loss.
- A real loss curve falls fast at first, then slowly and steadily, with occasional spikes.
- The weights are much smaller than the text, yet some training text, especially repeated text, can be extracted verbatim, and more so from larger models.

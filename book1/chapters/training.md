# How an LLM is trained {#training}

[The chapter on scale](#scale) treated a training run as three numbers: parameters, tokens, and compute. This chapter opens the run itself. It follows the process from raw web pages to a finished set of weights, as a procedure the reader could describe to someone else: collect text, clean it, remove duplicates, cut it into tokens, and then repeat one training step a few hundred thousand times on thousands of GPUs, while things break. It ends with a real loss curve and with a question the reader will have: how much of the training text ends up inside the model.

This is pretraining only. What is done to a pretrained model afterward, to make it follow instructions, is the subject of [the chapter on instruction tuning](#instructions). The optimizer, learning-rate schedules, and the training loop in general are in [the toolkit chapter](#toolkit); this chapter shows them at scale.

## Collecting text

Most of the text comes from the web, and most web text used for training comes from one source. Common Crawl "maintains a free, open repository of web crawl data that can be used by anyone", and "is a 501(c)(3) non-profit founded in 2007" [@commoncrawl2026home]. It crawls the web regularly and publishes what it finds. Its October 2026 crawl "contains 2.16 billion web pages" [@commoncrawl2026october]. That is a sample of the web at one moment, not the web itself.

Raw crawl text is mostly not usable prose. As the T5 authors put it in 2019, it "largely comprises gibberish or boiler-plate text like menus, error messages, or duplicate text" [@raffel2020exploring, p. 6]. Every model builder filters it, and the filters are a set of tools the reader will recognize.

**Rules.** The C4 corpus behind T5 used plain rules anyone could apply to a page by hand: "We only retained lines that ended in a terminal punctuation mark"; "We discarded any page with fewer than 5 sentences and only retained lines that contained at least 3 words"; pages containing a word from a list of offensive words were removed, and so was any page containing a curly bracket, since it "appears in many programming languages ... but not in natural text" [@raffel2020exploring, p. 6]. The result was about 750 GB of English text [@raffel2020exploring, p. 7].

**Classifiers.** GPT-3's filter was the reader's own logistic regression. "Using the original WebText as a proxy for high-quality documents, we trained a classifier to distinguish these from raw Common Crawl. ... The classifier is trained using logistic regression classifier with features from Spark's standard tokenizer and HashingTF" [@brown2020language, p. 43]: hashed word counts in, a probability of "looks like good text" out. Documents were then kept at random, with a probability that rose with the classifier's score, so that some unusual text survived [@brown2020language, p. 43]. LLaMA's pipeline used a fastText classifier to identify languages, "an n-gram language model" to filter out low-quality content, the model of [the prediction chapter](#prediction) now used as a judge, and a linear model trained to tell pages cited by Wikipedia from random ones [@touvron2023llama, p. 2]. By 2024, Llama 3's pipeline also asked an earlier language model to judge quality: the authors described quality requirements and had Llama 2's chat model label web documents, then trained classifiers on those labels [@grattafiori2024llama, p. 5].

**A mixture, not a pile.** The sources are not used in proportion to their size. GPT-3's training drew 60% of its examples from filtered Common Crawl, 22% from WebText2, 8% from each of two book collections, and 3% from Wikipedia; the higher-quality sources were read two to three times while Common Crawl was read less than once, which "essentially accepts a small amount of overfitting in exchange for higher quality training data" [@brown2020language, pp. 8-9]. The reader can think of it as stratified sampling with chosen, not proportional, strata. Llama 3's final mix was "roughly 50% of tokens corresponding to general knowledge, 25% of mathematical and reasoning tokens, 17% code tokens, and 8% multilingual tokens" [@grattafiori2024llama, p. 6].

Filters have side effects. Jesse Dodge and colleagues audited C4 in 2021, clustering the excluded documents with k-means on a PCA projection of their word-frequency vectors, and found that the list of offensive words had removed far more than offensive text: "mentions of sexual orientations (lesbian, gay, heterosexual, homosexual, bisexual) have the highest likelihood of being filtered out", and documents in African American English and Hispanic-aligned English were removed "at substantially higher rates (42% and 32%, respectively)" than others (6.2% and 7.2%) [@dodge2021documenting, pp. 1291-1292]. They also found that the most common website in the cleaned corpus was a patent site, which uses machine translation for patents filed in other languages [@dodge2021documenting, pp. 1288-1289].

The largest commercial models do not publish their data. Open groups have since rebuilt the process in public. FineWeb, a 15-trillion-token English data set from 96 Common Crawl snapshots, documents every step and tests each one by training small models [@penedo2024fineweb, pp. 1, 3]; its authors note that "the pretraining datasets for state-of-the-art open LLMs like Llama 3 and Mixtral are not publicly available and very little is known about how they were created" [@penedo2024fineweb, p. 1]. Dolma, three trillion tokens, was "sourced from approximately 200 TB of raw text before curation down to an 11 TB dataset" [@soldaini2024dolma, p. 15726].

## Removing duplicates

The web repeats itself. Katherine Lee and colleagues found "a 61-word sequence in C4 ... that is repeated 61,036 times verbatim in the training dataset" [@lee2022deduplicating, p. 8424]; most duplicates "were automatically generated pages, such as the outcomes of sports games" [@lee2022deduplicating, p. 8428]. **Deduplication** finds and removes such copies, either as exact repeated strings or as near-duplicate documents, found with a hashing technique, MinHash, that makes similar documents likely to get matching fingerprints [@lee2022deduplicating, p. 8424]. The reader may know the problem as record linkage. Lee and colleagues found that deduplication let models "emit memorized text ten times less frequently and require fewer training steps to achieve the same or better accuracy" [@lee2022deduplicating, p. 8424], and reported "no observed disadvantages" [@lee2022deduplicating, p. 8425].

More deduplication is not always better, though. FineWeb's builders deduplicated all 96 snapshots against each other and found "little improvement" over not deduplicating; in one old snapshot, the 10% of data that survived "was actually of worse quality than the 90% of data that was removed" [@penedo2024fineweb, p. 5]. Deduplicating each snapshot separately worked better. Llama 3's team removed lines that appeared more than six times in a group of 30 million documents, and noted that this removed "not only leftover boilerplate ... but also frequent high-quality text", yet still improved results [@grattafiori2024llama, p. 5].

## From text to training examples

The cleaned text is tokenized once ([the chapter on tokens](#tokens)), and from then on the corpus is measured in tokens: about 1.4 trillion for LLaMA [@touvron2023llama, p. 2], about 15 trillion for Llama 3 [@grattafiori2024llama, p. 1]. Counts made with different tokenizers are not in the same unit.

Training examples are windows of a fixed length. Documents are joined end to end, separated by an end-of-text token, and cut into windows: GPT-3 always trained on full windows of 2,048 tokens, "packing multiple documents into a single sequence when documents are shorter than 2048" [@brown2020language, p. 43]. GPT-3 did not stop attention from crossing between documents in a window and relied on the separator token; Llama 3 used "an attention mask that prevents self-attention between different documents within the same sequence", and found it mattered mainly for very long sequences [@grattafiori2024llama, p. 6]. Practice differs.

## One training step

![One training step, end to end. A batch of windows of packed text goes through the model, which predicts the next token at every position; the cross-entropy at every position is averaged into one loss; backpropagation gives a gradient for every parameter; the gradients are averaged across the GPUs that each processed part of the batch; the optimizer updates the parameters; and every so often a checkpoint is saved.](../../figures/training-step.svg){#fig:training-step}

A step is the minibatch gradient descent of [the toolkit chapter](#toolkit), with large numbers. The model reads a batch of windows. Thanks to the causal mask of [the anatomy chapter](#anatomy), one forward pass gives a prediction for the next token at every position of every window. The loss at each position is the cross-entropy of [the prediction chapter](#prediction): minus the log of the probability the model gave to the token that actually came next. The losses are averaged into one number. Training logs usually use natural logarithms, so the loss is in nats; dividing by 0.693 (the natural log of 2) turns it into bits. Backpropagation gives a gradient for every parameter, and the optimizer updates them all.

Batches are measured in tokens. At its full batch size, BLOOM, a 176-billion-parameter model trained in 2022, took 2,048 windows of 2,048 tokens per step: 4,194,304 predictions in one update [@lescao2022bloom, p. 21]. LLaMA's models used batches of 4 million tokens [@touvron2023llama, p. 3]; at that size, LLaMA's 1.4 trillion tokens take 350,000 steps. Llama 3's largest model ended with batches of 16 million tokens [@grattafiori2024llama, p. 10]. Batches often start smaller and grow: "We use a lower batch size early in training to improve training stability, and increase it subsequently to improve efficiency" [@grattafiori2024llama, p. 14]. Learning-rate schedules are likewise set in tokens: GPT-3 warmed up over the first 375 million tokens and decayed over 260 billion [@brown2020language, p. 43].

## Numbers and memory

<!-- D-009: code/checks/training.py, Example 4 -->

Every parameter, gradient, and activation is a floating-point number, and how many bits each one takes matters at this scale. The reader's statistics software used 64-bit or 32-bit numbers. Training now uses 16-bit numbers wherever it safely can, because they take half the memory of 32-bit ones and GPUs compute with them several times faster [@micikevicius2018mixed, p. 1]. Sixteen bits are not much. The standard 16-bit format, float16, cannot represent any number larger than 65,504, and near 1 its values are about 0.001 apart: adding 1/4096 to 1 gives exactly 1 [@micikevicius2018mixed, pp. 3-4]. A small weight update can simply vanish.

Two fixes are now standard. **Mixed precision** keeps a 32-bit master copy of the weights that receives the updates, and does the bulk of the arithmetic in 16 bits [@micikevicius2018mixed, p. 1]. And a second 16-bit format, **bfloat16**, gives up precision to keep the range of 32-bit numbers: it can represent numbers as large as 3 x 10^38, at the cost of a spacing of about 0.008 near 1 [@kalamkar2019study, pp. 1, 4]. Google named it after its research group, Google Brain, and reported that it "works as well as the FP32 format while delivering increased performance and reducing memory usage" [@wang2019bfloat16]. BLOOM's team saw the difference in practice: earlier trials with float16 had "numerical instabilities that caused irreversible training divergences", and the final run in bfloat16 had none, though the team listed several other changes that might also have helped [@lescao2022bloom, p. 20; @bigscience2022chronicles].

<!-- D-009: code/checks/training.py, Example 3 -->

Memory sets hard limits. The ZeRO paper's accounting for training with Adam in mixed precision is 16 bytes per parameter: 2 for the 16-bit weights, 2 for their gradients, and 12 for the 32-bit master copy and Adam's two running averages [@rajbhandari2020zero, pp. 7-8]. For GPT-2's 1.5 billion parameters that is 24 GB, more than fit on a single 32 GB GPU of 2019 once working memory is added [@rajbhandari2020zero, p. 7]. For a 405-billion-parameter model it is 6,480 GB, the memory of at least 81 GPUs of 80 GB each, before counting the activations of a single batch.

## Many GPUs

So the work is split. There are three basic ways, and BLOOM's paper describes them plainly. "Data parallelism (DP) replicates the model multiple times, with each replica placed on a different device and fed a slice of the data. ... all model replicas are synchronized at the end of each training step": the minibatch of [the toolkit chapter](#toolkit), spread over machines, with gradients averaged. "Tensor parallelism (TP) partitions individual layers of the model across multiple devices." "Pipeline parallelism (PP) splits up the model's layers across multiple GPUs, so that only a fraction of the layers of the model are placed on each GPU" [@lescao2022bloom, p. 19]. Large runs combine all three, and Llama 3 added a fourth, splitting long inputs [@grattafiori2024llama, p. 10].

The hardware for a few runs, each with its own date:

- LLaMA's 65-billion-parameter model (2023) ran on 2,048 A100 GPUs at about 380 tokens per second per GPU: "training over our dataset containing 1.4T tokens takes approximately 21 days" [@touvron2023llama, p. 4]. <!-- D-009: code/checks/training.py, Example 1 --> The arithmetic checks out at 20.8 days.
- BLOOM (2022) "took about 3.5 months to complete and consumed 1,082,990 compute hours", on 384 A100 GPUs with spare nodes kept in reserve [@lescao2022bloom, p. 18].
- Llama 3's 405-billion-parameter model (2024) trained "on up to 16K H100 GPUs, each running at 700W" [@grattafiori2024llama, p. 9].

Things break. <!-- D-009: code/checks/training.py, Example 5 --> "During a 54-day snapshot period of pre-training, we experienced a total of 466 job interruptions", the Llama 3 paper reports; 419 were unexpected, about eight a day, and "Approximately 78% of the unexpected interruptions are attributed to confirmed hardware issues", with faulty GPUs the largest single cause [@grattafiori2024llama, p. 13]. Because all GPUs work in lockstep, "a single GPU failure may require a restart of the entire job" [@grattafiori2024llama, p. 13]. Even so they kept "higher than 90% effective training time" [@grattafiori2024llama, p. 13]. Their GPUs pausing and resuming together could swing the data center's power draw "on the order of tens of megawatts, stretching the limits of the power grid" [@grattafiori2024llama, p. 14]. BLOOM's team saw "1-2 GPU failures" a week [@lescao2022bloom, p. 21].

The defense is the **checkpoint**: a saved copy of the weights and optimizer state, written regularly so that after a crash the run resumes from the last one. BLOOM saved one every three hours, so a crash cost at most three hours of work [@lescao2022bloom, p. 21; @bigscience2022chronicles].

## A real loss curve

<!-- D-009: code/checks/training.py, Example 8 -->

![BLOOM-176B's training loss, from the team's public training log. (a) The whole run: 95,281 steps over 366.5 billion tokens, with the one large spike, the point where the batch reached its full size, and the end of the first pass over the data. (b) The first 2 billion tokens on a logarithmic scale, with the learning rate (right axis): the loss falls from 61.5 to below the level of uniform guessing within 16 steps, while the learning rate warms up. This is training loss on the batches being trained, in nats per BLOOM token; it cannot be compared with any other model's loss.](../../figures/training-loss.svg){#fig:training-loss}

BLOOM was trained by the BigScience Workshop, a research collaboration, and its team published the log of every step [@bigscience2022logs]. The figure shows the training loss: the cross-entropy, in nats, on each batch as it was trained. It is not a held-out validation loss, and since it is measured per token of BLOOM's own vocabulary, its values cannot be compared with any other model's.

The run began at a loss of 61.5, far worse than the 12.4 a model would get by guessing uniformly among BLOOM's 250,680 tokens; a freshly initialized network is confidently wrong. Within 16 steps it was below that line. The learning rate rose to its peak of 0.00006 over the first 375 million tokens, as the paper specifies [@lescao2022bloom, p. 22]. The batch grew from 192 windows to its full 2,048 by about 20 billion tokens, a week into the run [@bigscience2022chronicles]. After that the loss fell slowly and steadily: 2.65 at 10 billion tokens, 2.21 at 100 billion, 1.99 at 300 billion, and 1.92 at the end, 366.5 billion tokens later. In bits, the final loss is 2.76 per token.

The one large disturbance came at 97.85 billion tokens, when the loss jumped from 2.35 to 5.10 in a single step, and the size of the gradient jumped to almost a thousand. The team's chronicle recorded it on 12 April 2022: "this is the first time we had an lm loss spike. Luckily it recovered in ~30 iterations" [@bigscience2022chronicles]. The paper reports "only one loss spike, which the model swiftly recovered from" [@lescao2022bloom, p. 21]; the log also shows a few smaller blips. The log also shows the run's restarts: 39 times, the step number goes back, after a crash and a resumption from a checkpoint.

## What the weights contain

<!-- D-009: code/checks/training.py, Example 6 -->

A finished model is far smaller than its training text. Llama 3's largest model has 810 GB of weights in 16-bit numbers; its 15.6 trillion training tokens, at about 3.94 characters each in English [@grattafiori2024llama, p. 7], are roughly 61 trillion characters, about 76 times as large. The weights cannot hold the corpus word for word. Yet they hold some of it.

In 2021, Nicholas Carlini and colleagues showed that they could "extract hundreds of verbatim text sequences" from GPT-2's training data by sampling from the model, including "(public) personally identifiable information (names, phone numbers, and email addresses)", even though "each of the above sequences are included in just one document in the training data" [@carlini2021extracting, p. 2633]. This was not ordinary overfitting: GPT-2's training loss was "only roughly 10% smaller than the test loss", so the train/test check the reader would run showed nothing alarming [@carlini2021extracting, p. 2635]. And "larger models are more vulnerable than smaller models" [@carlini2021extracting, p. 2633]. A 2023 follow-up found that "Memorization significantly grows as we increase (1) the capacity of a model, (2) the number of times an example has been duplicated, and (3) the number of tokens of context used to prompt the model", and that a 6-billion-parameter model "memorizes at least 1% of its training dataset" [@carlini2023quantifying, p. 1]. Deduplication helps, but "will not perfectly prevent leakage" [@carlini2023quantifying, p. 5]. Llama 3's authors measured memorization rates for their own models and found that they rise with size [@grattafiori2024llama, p. 42]. These studies measure different things: a share of the training data, a share of tests, a share of output. Their numbers should not be set side by side as one statistic.

So the honest answer to "does the model contain its training data?" is neither yes nor no. The weights are far too small to be a copy, and most of what they encode is regularities rather than text. But some text, especially text repeated many times, can be recovered word for word, and the risk grows with the size of the model.

## What to carry forward

- Training text comes mostly from web crawls, filtered by rules, by classifiers (including the reader's logistic regression), and by earlier language models, then mixed from several sources in chosen proportions. Filters remove more than intended.
- Deduplication removes repeated text and reduces memorization, but more of it is not always better.
- The corpus is tokenized once and cut into fixed-length windows. One step predicts the next token at every position of every window in a batch of millions of tokens, averages the cross-entropy (in nats), and updates every parameter.
- Training uses 16-bit numbers with a 32-bit master copy; bfloat16 keeps the range of 32-bit numbers. Training with Adam needs about 16 bytes per parameter.
- The work is split across thousands of GPUs by data, tensor, and pipeline parallelism. Hardware fails often; checkpoints limit the loss.
- A real loss curve falls fast at first, then slowly and steadily, with occasional spikes.
- The weights are much smaller than the text, yet some training text, especially repeated text, can be extracted verbatim, and more so from larger models.

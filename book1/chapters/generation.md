# Generating text {#generation}

<!-- D-002: intuition only; one formula (softmax with temperature), every symbol explained. D-003: no code; parameter names appear as words, not syntax. D-009: numbers from code/checks/generation.py, which imports the destination chapter's scores. D-014: figures from figures/generation.py. -->

[The destination chapter](#destination) described what happens after a model computes its probabilities: pick one token, append it, and run again. This chapter is about that loop in practice, what happens between pressing enter and reading a reply. It covers how a token is chosen from the probabilities, why the same prompt (the text the model is given) gives different answers, how generation stops, why the first word of a reply takes longer than the rest, and why a fluent model can state falsehoods with confidence.

The running example is the destination chapter's. After "The cat sat on the", the model gives scores of 3.0 to "mat", 2.0 to "floor", 1.5 to "sofa", 1.0 to "roof", -1.0 to "dog", and -2.0 to "the"; the softmax turns them into probabilities of 0.571, 0.210, 0.127, 0.077, 0.010, and 0.004.

## Reading fast, writing slow

A reply comes in two phases, now called **prefill** and **decode**. First the model reads the prompt. Every token of the prompt is already known, so, as with training in [the anatomy chapter](#anatomy), all of them pass through the network together in one forward pass. Reiner Pope and colleagues used these names for the two phases in 2022: the time "to process the input tokens present at the start of the inference (which we call "prefill")" and the time "to autoregressively generate output tokens (which we term "decode")" [@pope2022efficiently, p. 2]. Decoding is a loop: "Each step consists of a single forwards pass through the model, after which we sample one new token" [@pope2022efficiently, p. 3]. The delay before the first word appears is called the **time to first token** [@anthropic2026glossary]; after that, a chat reply streams out one token at a time.

Each decoding step is slow for a reason the reader may not expect: not the arithmetic but the memory. To produce one token, the chip must load every weight of the model, and the stored intermediate results of earlier tokens, from its memory, then do only a little arithmetic with them [@pope2022efficiently, p. 1]. Noam Shazeer pointed this out in 2019: generating one token at a time "is often slow, due to the memory-bandwidth cost of repeatedly loading the large 'keys' and 'values' tensors" [@shazeer2019fast, p. 1], stored results explained below. <!-- D-009: code/checks/generation.py, Example 6 --> In his measurements on a 2019 translation model, averaged over a batch of 1,024 sequences, reading the input cost 1.7 microseconds per token and writing the output 46, about 27 times as much [@shazeer2019fast, p. 7]. A later paper put it plainly: the decoding phase "severely underutilizes GPU computation and becomes memory-bound" [@kwon2023efficient, p. 3].

## Choosing a token

The rule that turns each set of probabilities into a choice is the **decoding strategy**. Ari Holtzman and colleagues described generation as producing text "token-by-token using a particular decoding strategy" [@holtzman2020curious, p. 3]. There are two families: pick the most probable, or draw at random.

**Greedy decoding** always takes the most probable token, "mat". [The chapter on sequences](#seq2seq) described a refinement for translation, beam search, which keeps the few best partial outputs at each step and returns the most probable whole sequence it finds. For tasks with one right answer it worked well; GPT-3's authors still used it in 2020 for tasks with free-form answers [@brown2020language, p. 10]. Holtzman and colleagues called such tasks **directed generation**, where "the output is a constrained transformation of the input", like translation or summarization, as opposed to **open-ended generation**, such as continuing a story [@holtzman2020curious, p. 3].

For open-ended text, looking for the most probable continuation fails badly. In 2019, Holtzman and colleagues found that "maximization-based decoding methods such as beam search lead to degeneration - output text that is bland, incoherent, or gets stuck in repetitive loops" [@holtzman2020curious, p. 1]. They had GPT-2 continue 5,000 passages of web text and measured the results [@holtzman2020curious, pp. 6-7]:

| Decoding | Perplexity of the generated text | Repetition |
|---|---:|---:|
| Human-written text | 12.38 | 0.28% |
| Greedy | 1.50 | 73.66% |
| Beam search, width 16 | 1.48 | 28.94% |
| Pure sampling | 22.73 | 0.22% |
| Nucleus sampling, p = 0.95 | 13.13 | 0.36% |

The perplexity is the model's own perplexity on the text produced, the measure of [the prediction chapter](#prediction); "repetition" counts texts that end with a phrase repeated at least three times [@holtzman2020curious, p. 9]. Greedy and beam-search text is far more predictable than human text, and nearly three-quarters of greedy outputs ended in a loop. Once a phrase repeats, the model rates it more likely to repeat again: "The probability of a repeated phrase increases with each repetition, creating a positive feedback loop" [@holtzman2020curious, p. 5].

The deeper point is that people do not write the most probable text. "Natural language rarely remains in a high probability zone for multiple consecutive time steps, instead veering into lower-probability but more informative tokens", the authors wrote, and conjectured that this is "an intrinsic property of human language": people avoid stating the obvious [@holtzman2020curious, p. 7]. Shannon's guessing game in [the prediction chapter](#prediction) showed the same thing from the other side: the informative letters were the ones that were hard to guess. Even in translation, finding the truly most probable output can go wrong. When Felix Stahlberg and Bill Byrne searched exhaustively in 2019, they found that "For more than 50% of the sentences, the model in fact assigns its global best score to the empty translation" [@stahlberg2019nmt, p. 3356]. <!-- D-009: code/checks/generation.py, Example 4 --> A sequence's probability is a product of numbers below one, so every extra token lowers it, and a model that is not careful about length prefers to say nothing.

The alternative is to draw at random, as [the destination chapter](#destination)'s ruler did. But **pure sampling**, drawing from the full distribution, has its own failure: text "that is incoherent and almost unrelated to the context" [@holtzman2020curious, p. 2]. The problem is the tail. A real vocabulary has tens of thousands of entries, and the many unlikely ones, each with a tiny probability, together hold a large share: an "unreliable tail ... composed of tens of thousands of candidate tokens with relatively low probability that are over-represented in the aggregate" [@holtzman2020curious, p. 2]. Draw often enough and an odd token appears, after which the model must continue from its own mistake.

## Cutting the tail

![The destination chapter's distribution at temperature 1, cut by top-p with p = 0.9 (or, here, equivalently by top-k with k = 3). (a) Before the cut: the three tokens kept hold 0.908 of the probability. (b) After: the three are rescaled to sum to 1, and the others can no longer be drawn.](../../figures/generation-truncation.svg){#fig:generation-truncation}

<!-- D-009: code/checks/generation.py, Examples 2 and 3 -->

The fix is to sample only from the head. **Top-k sampling** keeps the k most probable tokens, rescales their probabilities to sum to one, and draws among them. Angela Fan and colleagues used it in 2018 to write stories, with k = 10, finding it "substantially more effective than beam search, which tends to produce common phrases and repetitive text" [@fan2018hierarchical, p. 893]. GPT-2's published samples used k = 40, including the article about talking unicorns, which the paper labels a "Cherry pick of 10 samples" [@radford2019language, p. 20]: the best of ten tries. On the running example, k = 3 keeps "mat", "floor", and "sofa", which together hold 0.908 of the probability, and rescales them to 0.629, 0.231, and 0.140.

A fixed k is a blunt tool, because the shape of the distribution changes from step to step. Sometimes dozens of words are reasonable; sometimes one is almost certain. "If k is small, in some contexts there is a risk of generating bland or generic text, while if k is large the top-k vocabulary will include inappropriate candidates" [@holtzman2020curious, p. 6]. Holtzman and colleagues proposed **nucleus sampling**, usually called **top-p**: keep the smallest set of most probable tokens whose probabilities add up to at least p, then rescale and draw [@holtzman2020curious, pp. 4-5]. "The size of the sampling set will adjust dynamically based on the shape of the probability distribution at each time step" [@holtzman2020curious, p. 5]. On the running example, p = 0.9 keeps three tokens, the same as k = 3. On a made-up flat distribution with 20 equally plausible words and a light tail of 1,000 unlikely ones, top-p = 0.9 keeps 19 of the 20 plausible words and none of the tail, while k = 3 discards 17 of them; on a sharply peaked distribution, top-p keeps only two. In their 2019 tests with GPT-2, nucleus sampling scored best in a combined human and statistical evaluation [@holtzman2020curious, p. 10].

## Temperature

![The destination chapter's distribution at three temperatures. Lower temperature concentrates probability on the top token; higher temperature spreads it out. The ranking never changes. The same random draw, 0.80, picks a different token at each temperature (highlighted): "mat", "sofa", "roof". Entropy, from the prediction chapter, measures how spread out each distribution is.](../../figures/generation-temperature.svg){#fig:generation-temperature}

<!-- D-009: code/checks/generation.py, Example 1 -->

The third knob reshapes the distribution before drawing. Divide every score by a number $T$, the **temperature**, before the softmax:

$$p_i = \frac{e^{z_i / T}}{\sum_j e^{z_j / T}}$$

where $z_i$ is the score of token $i$, $p_i$ its probability, and the sum runs over the whole vocabulary [@holtzman2020curious, p. 6]. At $T = 1$ this is the softmax of [the destination chapter](#destination). A lower temperature stretches the differences between scores, so the top token gains; a higher one shrinks them, so the distribution flattens. At $T = 0.5$, "mat" gets 0.831; at $T = 1$, 0.571; at $T = 2$, 0.375. Measured as entropy, the distribution goes from 0.86 bits to 1.70 to 2.23 (six equally likely tokens would be 2.58). The same random draw of 0.80 that picked "sofa" in the destination chapter picks "mat" at temperature 0.5 and "roof" at temperature 2.

Temperature never changes the order. Dividing all the scores by the same number keeps the largest score largest, so the most probable token is the same at every temperature, and greedy decoding ignores temperature entirely. As $T$ approaches zero, the top token's probability approaches one: at 0.25 it is already 0.979. Software treats "temperature 0" as greedy decoding; the formula itself cannot divide by zero.

The word comes from physics. In 1985, David Ackley, Geoffrey Hinton, and Terrence Sejnowski gave their Boltzmann machine a parameter "that acts like temperature" in a logistic function, the reader's own logistic regression with its input divided by $T$, and linked it to the Boltzmann distribution of statistical physics and to annealing, the gradual cooling the reader may know from simulated annealing [@ackley1985learning, pp. 151-152]. In a 2015 paper, Hinton and colleagues gave the modern statement: "T is a temperature that is normally set to 1. Using a higher value for T produces a softer probability distribution over classes" [@hinton2015distilling, p. 3].

Real systems combine these knobs. As of 2026-11, OpenAI's API lets users set a temperature between 0 and 2 (default 1) or a top-p value, and recommends changing one but not both [@openai2026chatcreate]. A popular local runtime, llama.cpp, by default (as of 2026-11) applies top-k 40, top-p 0.95, and temperature 0.8, among other filters [@llamacpp2026server]. Software differs in whether temperature is applied before or after the cut [@holtzman2020curious, p. 6; @llamacpp2026server], which changes how many tokens top-p keeps: on the running example, with p = 0.9, temperature 0.5 applied first keeps two tokens, temperature 2 applied first keeps four. And as of 2026-11, Anthropic's newest models no longer accept temperature, top-p, or top-k settings at all; its documentation recommends guiding the model through the prompt instead [@anthropic2026opus55migration].

## The same prompt, different answers

[The destination chapter](#destination) explained why the same question can get different answers: the random draw. With sampling, every reply is one draw among many possible ones. <!-- D-009: code/checks/generation.py, Example 4 --> Even on a distribution as peaked as the running example's, the chance that twenty draws all pick the top token is about one in 74,000 at temperature 1.

In principle, temperature 0 removes the randomness. In practice it does not quite. Vendors say so without saying why: "Even with temperature set to 0, the results will not be fully deterministic, and identical inputs might produce different outputs across API calls" [@anthropic2026glossary]. In September 2025, researchers at Thinking Machines reported an experiment and an explanation. They asked one model for 1,000 completions of "Tell me about Richard Feynman" at temperature 0 and got 80 different texts; all agreed for the first 102 tokens, then 992 continued "Queens, New York" and 8 "New York City" [@he2025defeating]. Their account: a server processes many users' requests together, the number varies with load, and that changes the order in which the GPU adds up numbers; floating-point addition gives slightly different results in different orders, and when two tokens are nearly tied, a difference in the last decimal places can flip the choice [@he2025defeating]. They showed that arithmetic arranged not to depend on the batch removed the effect, at some cost in speed [@he2025defeating].

## Stopping, and the full window

Generation ends in one of three ways. The model can choose the end-of-text token of [the chapter on tokens](#tokens), or, for a chat model, an end-of-turn marker; the reply can reach a length limit set by the user or the system; or the surrounding software can watch for a stop sequence, a string chosen by the user, and cut the text there [@kwon2023efficient, p. 3; @anthropic2026messages]. The first is the model's decision; the other two are the software's. A reply cut by the length limit stops mid-thought, and interfaces report which way it ended [@anthropic2026stopreasons].

The context window of [the anatomy chapter](#anatomy) is the model's whole working memory: one vendor's glossary calls it "a "working memory" for the model", distinct from the training data [@anthropic2026glossary]. In a conversation, every message and every reply stays in the window, so it fills up. Systems do one of three things when it is full: refuse the request, drop the oldest text, or summarize the earlier part and continue [@anthropic2026contextwindows; @llamacpp2026server]. The model does not forget gradually; text is either inside the window or absent.

## Not repeating work: the KV cache

A naive loop would recompute everything at every step: to write token 501, pass all 500 earlier tokens through every layer again. It does not need to. In each layer, every token produces a key and a value ([the attention chapter](#attention)) that later tokens consult, and because a token only looks backward, its keys and values never change once computed. So they are stored, and each new token computes only its own. Pope and colleagues called the store by the name now used, the **KV cache**: "The attention key and value tensors of each layer, which we refer to as the KV cache, must also be stored in memory for the duration of decoding" [@pope2022efficiently, p. 1]. The technique was already in use in 2019 code [@shazeer2019fast, pp. 3-4]. <!-- D-009: code/checks/generation.py, Example 5 --> Writing 500 tokens after a 1,000-token prompt, the cache cuts the token positions processed from 625,250 to 1,500.

The cost is memory, and it grows with every token. Each token stores two vectors in every layer. <!-- D-009: code/checks/generation.py, Example 5 --> For GPT-2 small, in 16-bit numbers, that is 36,864 bytes per token, about 38 MB for a full 1,024-token window. For Llama 3 8B, which, as [the anatomy chapter](#anatomy) noted, keeps only 8 key-value heads in each of 32 layers [@grattafiori2024llama, p. 7], each of 128 numbers (its width of 4,096 divided by its 32 query heads), it is 131,072 bytes per token; a full 131,072-token window, which the paper's 8B model (released as Llama 3.1) supports [@grattafiori2024llama, pp. 2-3], needs 17.2 GB, more than the model's own weights (16.1 GB in 16-bit numbers). Without that grouping, with 32 key-value heads, it would be four times as much. That is why Llama 3 uses fewer key and value heads, "to reduce the size of key-value caches during decoding" [@grattafiori2024llama, p. 6].

Two later tricks build on this. **Prompt caching**, offered by vendors as of 2026-11, keeps the cache of a prompt prefix that many requests share, such as a long set of instructions, so it need not be recomputed: one vendor describes it as a way to "Avoid recalculating a prompt prefix that the model has already processed" [@openai2026promptcaching]. It works only for an identical prefix, because every key and value depends on everything before it. **Speculative decoding**, proposed independently by two groups in 2022 and 2023, uses a small, fast model to guess several tokens ahead and the large model to check all the guesses in one parallel pass, which costs about as much as producing a single token [@leviathan2023fast, p. 1; @chen2023accelerating, pp. 1-2]. Accepted guesses are kept; the method is arranged so that the output has exactly the large model's probabilities, "without any changes to the outputs" [@leviathan2023fast, p. 1], up to the tiny rounding differences discussed above [@chen2023accelerating, p. 1]. The reported speed-ups were two to three times [@leviathan2023fast, p. 1; @chen2023accelerating, p. 1].

## Hallucination

Language models sometimes state falsehoods fluently and with confidence. The usual word is **hallucination**: in a survey's definition, "generated content that is nonsensical or unfaithful to the provided source content" [@ji2023survey, p. 4]. OpenAI's GPT-4 report used the word while noting that "this framing may suggest anthropomorphization, which in turn can lead to harms or incorrect mental models of how the model learns" [@openai2023gpt4, p. 46]. It is a label for a kind of error, not a diagnosis.

It was common long before chat assistants. A 2020 study of systems that write one-sentence summaries of news articles found unsupported content "in more than 70% of single-sentence summaries" [@maynez2020faithfulness, p. 1907]. The human-written reference summaries in that data set were no better, because they often added background not in the article [@maynez2020faithfulness, p. 1911]: models learn from the text they are given. Its authors also made a point that matters for this chapter: "Open-ended generation - the task of generating text that forms a natural continuation from the input text - requires the model to hallucinate text" [@maynez2020faithfulness, p. 1907]. Continuing text is inventing text; the question is whether the invention is true.

Why does it happen? Several accounts, not all compatible, are on offer.

- **Sampling adds risk.** The survey conjectures that "deliberately added "randomness" by sampling from the top-k samples instead of choosing the most probable token increase the unexpected nature of the generation, leading to a higher chance of containing hallucinated content" [@ji2023survey, p. 8]. It also names **exposure bias**: in training, the model always predicts from real text; when generating, it predicts from its own output, earlier mistakes included, and continues them fluently [@ji2023survey, p. 8].
- **It follows from how models are trained and graded.** Adam Tauman Kalai and colleagues argued in a 2025 paper that hallucinations "originate simply as errors in binary classification", and that a model trained to match the statistics of text will produce some plausible falsehoods even from error-free data [@kalai2025why, pp. 1-2]. Their example is facts seen only once: "if 20% of birthday facts appear exactly once in the pretraining data, then one expects base models to hallucinate on at least 20% of birthday facts" [@kalai2025why, p. 3], the same sparsity that troubled the n-gram models of [the prediction chapter](#prediction). They add that tests scoring a right answer 1 and "I don't know" 0 reward guessing [@kalai2025why, p. 4].
- **It cannot be eliminated.** Ziwei Xu and colleagues argued in 2024, in a formal model, that it is "impossible to eliminate hallucination in LLMs" [@xu2024hallucination, p. 1]. Kalai and colleagues disagree in part: a system allowed to answer "I don't know" need not hallucinate [@kalai2025why, p. 8].

Two facts cut through the dispute. Decoding without randomness does not prevent hallucination: the 2020 summarizers above used beam search [@maynez2020faithfulness, p. 1918]. And on multiple-choice questions, a pretrained model's probabilities are often honest about its uncertainty. A model is **calibrated** if, of all the answers it gives with 70% confidence, about 70% are right, like a weather forecaster whose 70% days are rainy 70% of the time. The 2023 GPT-4 report found "the pre-trained model is highly calibrated", but "after the post-training process, the calibration is reduced" [@openai2023gpt4, pp. 10-11], a hand-off to [the chapter on teaching models to follow instructions](#instructions). The model's confidence, when it is calibrated, says how likely it is to be right; it does not make the answer true.

## What to carry forward

- A reply is generated in two phases: the prompt is read in one parallel pass (prefill), and the reply is written one token at a time (decode), each step limited by memory traffic more than arithmetic.
- A decoding strategy picks each token. Taking the most probable (greedy, beam search) suits tasks with one right answer but produces bland, repetitive open-ended text; human writing is not the most probable text.
- Sampling from the full distribution draws from an unreliable tail. Top-k and top-p cut the tail and rescale; temperature sharpens or flattens the distribution without changing its order.
- Sampling is why the same prompt gives different answers. Temperature 0 means greedy decoding, but real servers are still not perfectly repeatable.
- Generation stops at an end token, a length limit, or a stop sequence. A full context window forces refusing, dropping old text, or summarizing.
- The KV cache stores each token's keys and values so they are computed once; it grows with every token and can exceed the model's own size.
- Hallucination, fluent but false or unsupported text, is a predictable property of models trained to continue text. Why, and whether it can be eliminated, is disputed; decoding without randomness does not prevent it.

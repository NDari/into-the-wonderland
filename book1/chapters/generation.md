[The destination chapter](#destination) described what happens after a model computes its probabilities: pick one token, append it, and run again. This chapter is about that loop in practice, what happens between pressing enter and reading a reply. It covers how a token is chosen from the probabilities, why the same prompt (the text the model is given) gives different answers, how generation stops, why the first word of a reply takes longer than the rest, and why a fluent model can state falsehoods with confidence.

The running example is the destination chapter's. After "The cat sat on the", the model gives scores of 3.0 to "mat", 2.0 to "floor", 1.5 to "sofa", 1.0 to "roof", -1.0 to "dog", and -2.0 to "the"; the softmax turns them into probabilities of 0.571, 0.210, 0.127, 0.077, 0.010, and 0.004.

## Reading fast, writing slow

A reply comes in two phases, now called **prefill** and **decode**. First the model reads the prompt. Every token of the prompt is already known, so, as with training in [the anatomy chapter](#anatomy), all of them pass through the network together in one forward pass. A 2022 paper on running large models efficiently used these names: "prefill" for working through the tokens of the prompt, "decode" for producing the reply one token after another [@pope2022efficiently, p. 2]. Decoding is a loop: at each step the whole model runs once and one new token is drawn [@pope2022efficiently, p. 3]. The delay before the first word appears is called the **time to first token** [@anthropic2026glossary]; after that, a chat reply streams out one token at a time.

Each decoding step is slow for an unexpected reason: not the arithmetic but the memory. To produce one token, the chip must load every weight of the model, and the stored intermediate results of earlier tokens, from its memory, then do only a little arithmetic with them [@pope2022efficiently, p. 1]. In 2019, Noam Shazeer wrote that generating one token at a time is often slow, because the large stored keys and values have to be loaded from memory over and over [@shazeer2019fast, p. 1]; these stored results are explained below. <!-- D-009: code/checks/generation.py, Example 6 --> In his measurements on a 2019 translation model, averaged over a batch of 1,024 sequences, reading the input cost 1.7 microseconds per token and writing the output 46, about 27 times as much [@shazeer2019fast, p. 7]. A later paper made the same point: in the decoding phase the GPU's computing power is severely underused, and the work is memory-bound, its pace set by memory rather than arithmetic [@kwon2023efficient, p. 3].

## Choosing a token

The rule that turns each set of probabilities into a choice is the **decoding strategy**: text is produced one token at a time, each chosen by that rule [@holtzman2020curious, p. 3]. There are two families: pick the most probable, or draw at random.

**Greedy decoding** always takes the most probable token, "mat". [The chapter on sequences](#seq2seq) described a refinement for translation, beam search, which keeps the few best partial outputs at each step and returns the most probable whole sequence it finds. For tasks with one right answer it worked well; GPT-3's authors still used it in 2020 for tasks with free-form answers [@brown2020language, p. 10]. A 2019 study called such tasks **directed generation**, where "the output is a constrained transformation of the input", like translation or summarization, as opposed to **open-ended generation**, such as continuing a story [@holtzman2020curious, p. 3].

For open-ended text, looking for the most probable continuation fails badly. The same study found that decoding methods that maximize probability, beam search among them, lead to what it called "degeneration": text that is bland, incoherent, or caught in a loop of repetition [@holtzman2020curious, p. 1]. Its authors had GPT-2 continue 5,000 passages of web text and measured the results [@holtzman2020curious, pp. 6-7]:

| Decoding | Perplexity of the generated text | Repetition |
|---|---:|---:|
| Human-written text | 12.38 | 0.28% |
| Greedy | 1.50 | 73.66% |
| Beam search, width 16 | 1.48 | 28.94% |
| Pure sampling | 22.73 | 0.22% |
| Nucleus sampling, p = 0.95 | 13.13 | 0.36% |

The perplexity is the model's own perplexity on the text produced, the measure of [the prediction chapter](#prediction); "repetition" counts texts that end with a phrase repeated at least three times [@holtzman2020curious, p. 9]. Greedy and beam-search text is far more predictable than human text, and nearly three-quarters of greedy outputs ended in a loop. Once a phrase repeats, the model rates it more likely to repeat again, and each repetition raises that probability further: a positive feedback loop [@holtzman2020curious, p. 5].

The deeper point is that people do not write the most probable text. Human text, the authors observed, seldom keeps to the most probable tokens for long; again and again it takes a less likely but more informative turn. They conjectured that this is a property of human language itself: people avoid stating the obvious [@holtzman2020curious, p. 7]. Shannon's guessing game in [the prediction chapter](#prediction) showed the same thing from the other side: the informative letters were the ones that were hard to guess. Even in translation, finding the truly most probable output can go wrong. In 2019, an exact search through a translation model's possible outputs found that for more than half of the sentences, the model gave its best score of all to the empty translation [@stahlberg2019nmt, p. 3356]. <!-- D-009: code/checks/generation.py, Example 4 --> A sequence's probability is a product of numbers below one, so every extra token lowers it, and a model that is not careful about length prefers to say nothing.

The alternative is to draw at random, as [the destination chapter](#destination)'s ruler did. But **pure sampling**, drawing from the full distribution, has its own failure: text that is incoherent and barely connected to what came before [@holtzman2020curious, p. 2]. The problem is the tail. A real vocabulary has tens of thousands of entries, and the many unlikely ones, each with a tiny probability, together hold a large share. The 2019 study called this the "unreliable tail": tens of thousands of candidate tokens, each of relatively low probability, which taken together get more than their due [@holtzman2020curious, p. 2]. Draw often enough and an odd token appears, after which the model must continue from its own mistake.

## Cutting the tail

![The destination chapter's distribution at temperature 1, cut by top-p with p = 0.9 (or, here, equivalently by top-k with k = 3). (a) Before the cut: the three tokens kept hold 0.908 of the probability. (b) After: the three are rescaled to sum to 1, and the others can no longer be drawn.](../../figures/generation-truncation.svg){#fig:generation-truncation}

<!-- D-009: code/checks/generation.py, Examples 2 and 3 -->

The fix is to sample only from the head. **Top-k sampling** keeps the k most probable tokens, rescales their probabilities to sum to one, and draws among them. The builders of a 2018 story-writing system used it with k = 10 and found it worked far better than beam search, which tended to fall back on common phrases and repeated text [@fan2018hierarchical, p. 893]. GPT-2's published samples used k = 40, including the article about talking unicorns, which the paper labels a "Cherry pick of 10 samples" [@radford2019language, p. 20]: the best of ten tries. On the running example, k = 3 keeps "mat", "floor", and "sofa", which together hold 0.908 of the probability, and rescales them to 0.629, 0.231, and 0.140.

A fixed k is a blunt tool, because the shape of the distribution changes from step to step. Sometimes dozens of words are reasonable; sometimes one is almost certain. With a small k, text risks turning bland or generic where many words would fit; with a large k, unsuitable words get into the kept set where few would [@holtzman2020curious, p. 6]. The 2019 study proposed **nucleus sampling**, usually called **top-p**: keep the smallest set of most probable tokens whose probabilities add up to at least p, then rescale and draw [@holtzman2020curious, pp. 4-5]. The number of tokens kept then changes from step to step with the shape of the distribution [@holtzman2020curious, p. 5]. On the running example, p = 0.9 keeps three tokens, the same as k = 3. On a made-up flat distribution with 20 equally plausible words and a light tail of 1,000 unlikely ones, top-p = 0.9 keeps 19 of the 20 plausible words and none of the tail, while k = 3 discards 17 of them; on a sharply peaked distribution, top-p keeps only two. In the study's 2019 tests with GPT-2, nucleus sampling scored best in a combined human and statistical evaluation [@holtzman2020curious, p. 10].

## Temperature

![The destination chapter's distribution at three temperatures. Lower temperature concentrates probability on the top token; higher temperature spreads it out. The ranking never changes. The same random draw, 0.80, picks a different token at each temperature (highlighted): "mat", "sofa", "roof". Entropy, from the prediction chapter, measures how spread out each distribution is.](../../figures/generation-temperature.svg){#fig:generation-temperature}

<!-- D-009: code/checks/generation.py, Example 1 -->

The third knob reshapes the distribution before drawing. Divide every score by a number $T$, the **temperature**, before the softmax:

$$p_i = \frac{e^{z_i / T}}{\sum_j e^{z_j / T}}$$

where $z_i$ is the score of token $i$, $p_i$ its probability, and the sum runs over the whole vocabulary [@holtzman2020curious, p. 6]. At $T = 1$ this is the softmax of [the destination chapter](#destination). A lower temperature stretches the differences between scores, so the top token gains; a higher one shrinks them, so the distribution flattens. At $T = 0.5$, "mat" gets 0.831; at $T = 1$, 0.571; at $T = 2$, 0.375. Measured as entropy, the distribution goes from 0.86 bits to 1.70 to 2.23 (six equally likely tokens would be 2.58). The same random draw of 0.80 that picked "sofa" in the destination chapter picks "mat" at temperature 0.5 and "roof" at temperature 2.

Temperature never changes the order. Dividing all the scores by the same number keeps the largest score largest, so the most probable token is the same at every temperature, and greedy decoding ignores temperature entirely. As $T$ approaches zero, the top token's probability approaches one: at 0.25 it is already 0.979. Software treats "temperature 0" as greedy decoding; the formula itself cannot divide by zero.

The word comes from physics. In 1985, a network called the Boltzmann machine was given a parameter "that acts like temperature" in a logistic function, an ordinary logistic regression with its input divided by $T$, and its authors linked it to the Boltzmann distribution of statistical physics and to annealing, the gradual cooling behind the optimization method simulated annealing [@ackley1985learning, pp. 151-152]. In 2015, Geoffrey Hinton, one of those authors, and colleagues gave the modern statement: $T$ is normally set to 1, and a higher value makes the probabilities over the classes softer [@hinton2015distilling, p. 3].

Real systems combine these knobs. As of 2026-11, OpenAI's API lets users set a temperature between 0 and 2 (default 1) or a top-p value, and recommends changing one but not both [@openai2026chatcreate]. A popular local runtime, llama.cpp, by default (as of 2026-11) applies top-k 40, top-p 0.95, and temperature 0.8, among other filters [@llamacpp2026server]. Software differs in whether temperature is applied before or after the cut [@holtzman2020curious, p. 6; @llamacpp2026server], which changes how many tokens top-p keeps: on the running example, with p = 0.9, temperature 0.5 applied first keeps two tokens, temperature 2 applied first keeps four. And as of 2026-11, Anthropic's newest models no longer accept temperature, top-p, or top-k settings at all; its documentation recommends guiding the model through the prompt instead [@anthropic2026opus55migration].

## The same prompt, different answers

[The destination chapter](#destination) explained why the same question can get different answers: the random draw. With sampling, every reply is one draw among many possible ones. <!-- D-009: code/checks/generation.py, Example 4 --> Even on a distribution as peaked as the running example's, the chance that twenty draws all pick the top token is about one in 74,000 at temperature 1.

In principle, temperature 0 removes the randomness. In practice it does not quite. Vendors say so without saying why: one vendor's glossary warns that even at temperature 0 the results are not fully repeatable, and identical inputs may give different outputs from one call to the next [@anthropic2026glossary]. In September 2025, researchers at Thinking Machines reported an experiment and an explanation. They asked one model for 1,000 completions of "Tell me about Richard Feynman" at temperature 0 and got 80 different texts; all agreed for the first 102 tokens, then 992 continued "Queens, New York" and 8 "New York City" [@he2025defeating]. Their account: a server processes many users' requests together, the number varies with load, and that changes the order in which the GPU adds up numbers; floating-point addition gives slightly different results in different orders, and when two tokens are nearly tied, a difference in the last decimal places can flip the choice [@he2025defeating]. They showed that arithmetic arranged not to depend on the batch removed the effect, at some cost in speed [@he2025defeating].

## Stopping, and the full window

Generation ends in one of three ways. The model can choose the end-of-text token of [the chapter on tokens](#tokens), or, for a chat model, an end-of-turn marker; the reply can reach a length limit set by the user or the system; or the surrounding software can watch for a stop sequence, a string chosen by the user, and cut the text there [@kwon2023efficient, p. 3; @anthropic2026messages]. The first is the model's decision; the other two are the software's. A reply cut by the length limit stops mid-thought, and interfaces report which way it ended [@anthropic2026stopreasons].

The context window of [the anatomy chapter](#anatomy) is the model's whole working memory: one vendor's glossary calls it the model's "working memory", as distinct from the data it was trained on [@anthropic2026glossary]. In a conversation, every message and every reply stays in the window, so it fills up. Systems do one of three things when it is full: refuse the request, drop the oldest text, or summarize the earlier part and continue [@anthropic2026contextwindows; @llamacpp2026server]. The model does not forget gradually; text is either inside the window or absent.

## Not repeating work: the KV cache

A naive loop would recompute everything at every step: to write token 501, pass all 500 earlier tokens through every layer again. It does not need to. In each layer, every token produces a key and a value ([the attention chapter](#attention)) that later tokens consult, and because a token only looks backward, its keys and values never change once computed. So they are stored, and each new token computes only its own. The 2022 paper on efficient inference called the store by the name now used, the **KV cache**: each layer's attention keys and values, held in memory for as long as decoding lasts [@pope2022efficiently, p. 1]. The technique was already in use in 2019 code, before it had this name [@shazeer2019fast, pp. 3-4]; [the credit appendix](#credit-generation) sets out what is known of its origin. <!-- D-009: code/checks/generation.py, Example 5 --> Writing 500 tokens after a 1,000-token prompt, the cache cuts the token positions processed from 625,250 to 1,500.

The cost is memory, and it grows with every token. Each token stores two vectors in every layer. <!-- D-009: code/checks/generation.py, Example 5 --> For GPT-2 small, in 16-bit numbers, that is 36,864 bytes per token, about 38 MB for a full 1,024-token window. For Llama 3 8B, which, as [the anatomy chapter](#anatomy) noted, keeps only 8 key-value heads in each of 32 layers [@grattafiori2024llama, p. 7], each of 128 numbers (its width of 4,096 divided by its 32 query heads), it is 131,072 bytes per token; a full 131,072-token window, which the paper's 8B model (released as Llama 3.1) supports [@grattafiori2024llama, pp. 2-3], needs 17.2 GB, more than the model's own weights (16.1 GB in 16-bit numbers). Without that grouping, with 32 key-value heads, it would be four times as much. That is why Llama 3 uses fewer key and value heads: to speed up inference and to shrink the cache during decoding [@grattafiori2024llama, p. 6].

Two later tricks build on this. **Prompt caching**, offered by vendors as of 2026-11, keeps the cache of a prompt prefix that many requests share, such as a long set of instructions, so it need not be recomputed: one vendor describes it as a way to skip redoing the work on a prefix the model has seen before [@openai2026promptcaching]. It works only for an identical prefix, because every key and value depends on everything before it. **Speculative decoding**, from 2022 and 2023, uses a small, fast model to guess several tokens ahead and the large model to check all the guesses in one parallel pass, which costs about as much as producing a single token [@leviathan2023fast, p. 1; @chen2023accelerating, pp. 1-2]. Accepted guesses are kept; the method is arranged so that the output is unchanged, with exactly the large model's probabilities [@leviathan2023fast, p. 1], up to the tiny rounding differences discussed above [@chen2023accelerating, p. 1]. The reported speed-ups were two to three times [@leviathan2023fast, p. 1; @chen2023accelerating, p. 1]. Two groups published the idea within months of each other; [the credit appendix](#credit-generation) sets out the dates.

## Hallucination

Language models sometimes state falsehoods fluently and with confidence. The usual word is **hallucination**: in a survey's definition, "generated content that is nonsensical or unfaithful to the provided source content" [@ji2023survey, p. 4]. OpenAI's GPT-4 report used the word while cautioning that it may cast the model as a person (anthropomorphization), which can do harm and give people a wrong picture of how the model learns [@openai2023gpt4, p. 46]. It is a label for a kind of error, not a diagnosis.

It was common long before chat assistants. A 2020 study of systems that write one-sentence summaries of news articles found unsupported content in more than 70% of single-sentence summaries [@maynez2020faithfulness, p. 1907]. The human-written reference summaries in that data set were no better, because they often added background not in the article [@maynez2020faithfulness, p. 1911]: models learn from the text they are given. Its authors also made a point that matters for this chapter: "Open-ended generation - the task of generating text that forms a natural continuation from the input text - requires the model to hallucinate text" [@maynez2020faithfulness, p. 1907]. Continuing text is inventing text; the question is whether the invention is true.

Why does it happen? Several accounts, not all compatible, are on offer.

- **Sampling adds risk.** The survey conjectures that sampling, by deliberately picking among the top k tokens rather than always the most probable one, makes the text less predictable and so more prone to hallucination [@ji2023survey, p. 8]. It also names **exposure bias**: in training, the model always predicts from real text; when generating, it predicts from its own output, earlier mistakes included, and continues them fluently [@ji2023survey, p. 8].
- **It follows from how models are trained and graded.** Adam Tauman Kalai and colleagues argued in a 2025 paper that there is nothing mysterious about hallucinations: at root they are ordinary errors of binary classification, and a model trained to match the statistics of text will produce some plausible falsehoods even from error-free data [@kalai2025why, pp. 1-2]. Their example is facts seen only once: "if 20% of birthday facts appear exactly once in the pretraining data, then one expects base models to hallucinate on at least 20% of birthday facts" [@kalai2025why, p. 3], the same sparsity that troubled the n-gram models of [the prediction chapter](#prediction). They add that tests scoring a right answer 1 and "I don't know" 0 reward guessing [@kalai2025why, p. 4].
- **It cannot be eliminated.** A 2024 paper argued, in a formal model, that hallucination cannot be eliminated from large language models [@xu2024hallucination, p. 1]. Kalai and colleagues disagree in part: a system allowed to answer "I don't know" need not hallucinate [@kalai2025why, p. 8].

Two facts cut through the dispute. Decoding without randomness does not prevent hallucination: the 2020 summarizers above used beam search [@maynez2020faithfulness, p. 1918]. And on multiple-choice questions, a pretrained model's probabilities are often honest about its uncertainty. A model is **calibrated** if, of all the answers it gives with 70% confidence, about 70% are right, like a weather forecaster whose 70% days are rainy 70% of the time. The 2023 GPT-4 report found the pre-trained model highly calibrated and the post-trained model less so [@openai2023gpt4, pp. 10-11], a hand-off to [the chapter on teaching models to follow instructions](#instructions). The model's confidence, when it is calibrated, says how likely it is to be right; it does not make the answer true.

## What to carry forward

- A reply is generated in two phases: the prompt is read in one parallel pass (prefill), and the reply is written one token at a time (decode), each step limited by memory traffic more than arithmetic.
- A decoding strategy picks each token. Taking the most probable (greedy, beam search) suits tasks with one right answer but produces bland, repetitive open-ended text; human writing is not the most probable text.
- Sampling from the full distribution draws from an unreliable tail. Top-k and top-p cut the tail and rescale; temperature sharpens or flattens the distribution without changing its order.
- Sampling is why the same prompt gives different answers. Temperature 0 means greedy decoding, but real servers are still not perfectly repeatable.
- Generation stops at an end token, a length limit, or a stop sequence. A full context window forces refusing, dropping old text, or summarizing.
- The KV cache stores each token's keys and values so they are computed once; it grows with every token and can exceed the model's own size.
- Hallucination, fluent but false or unsupported text, is a predictable property of models trained to continue text. Why, and whether it can be eliminated, is disputed; decoding without randomness does not prevent it.

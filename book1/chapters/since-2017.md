[The anatomy chapter](#anatomy) ended with a short list of what changed in the transformer after 2017. This chapter takes that list item by item and adds what it left out, asking of each change three questions: what problem it answered, what changed, and what it cost or bought. The aim is that every part of the architecture section of a current model's paper becomes recognizable.

"Current" in this chapter means what the primary documents of open-weight models say: Meta's Llama 3 paper of July 2024, DeepSeek-V3's report of December 2024, and Meta's Llama 4 announcement of April 2025. The makers of closed models, such as Anthropic, OpenAI, and Google, publish few architectural details of their current models (as of 2026-11), and none of the sources read for this chapter says which of these techniques they use.

## Where the normalization sits

**The problem.** The 2017 transformer normalized the residual stream after each addition, "LayerNorm(x + Sublayer(x))" [@vaswani2017attention, p. 3], an arrangement now called **post-norm**. Deep post-norm stacks were hard to train. A 2020 analysis showed that at the start of training the gradients near the output layer are large, so a large learning rate makes training unstable; in practice, the warmup of [the toolkit chapter](#toolkit) helps to avoid this [@xiong2020layer, p. 1].

**The change.** In **pre-norm**, the residual stream itself is never normalized: each sub-layer reads a normalized copy, and one last normalization comes before the output layer. The same analysis showed that with this placement the gradients start out well behaved [@xiong2020layer, p. 1]. OpenAI's GPT-2 and Sparse Transformer used it by 2019 [@radford2019language, p. 4; @child2019generating, p. 6], and LLaMA (2023) adopted it to make training more stable [@touvron2023llama, p. 3].

**What it cost.** Possibly a little quality. A 2020 study of translation models tried 15 settings: pre-norm converged in all of them and post-norm diverged in 7, but in 7 of the 8 settings where post-norm did converge, it ended up better than pre-norm [@liu2020understanding, p. 5749]. When one run costs millions, robustness wins: GPT-2, GPT-3, T5, PaLM, LLaMA, Llama 2, and Llama 3 all use pre-norm [@brown2020language, p. 8; @raffel2020exploring, p. 4; @chowdhery2022palm, p. 5; @touvron2023llama2, p. 5; @meta2024llama3code]. That is a choice for reliability, not a proof that pre-norm is better.

**A cheaper normalization.** Layer normalization, from [the chapter on depth](#depth), is a z-score across the entries of one vector: subtract their mean, divide by their standard deviation, apply a learned scale. In 2019 a paper pointed out that it costs time: small, shallow networks hardly notice, but the cost grows serious as networks get larger and deeper [@zhang2019root, p. 1], above all in recurrent networks, which normalize at every step. Its authors hypothesized that the rescaling, not the centering, was what mattered, and proposed **RMSNorm** [@zhang2019root, p. 3]:

$$\bar{a}_i = \frac{a_i}{\text{RMS}(a)}\, g_i, \qquad \text{RMS}(a) = \sqrt{\frac{1}{d} \sum_{j=1}^{d} a_j^2}$$

where $a$ is the vector being normalized, $a_i$ its $i$-th entry, $d$ its number of entries, $g_i$ a learned gain, and $\text{RMS}(a)$ its **root mean square**, the square root of the average squared entry. Nothing is subtracted.

<!-- D-009: code/checks/since-2017.py, Example 1 -->

Take the numbers 2, -1, 3, 0, with gains of 1. Their mean is 1 and standard deviation 1.5811, so layer normalization gives 0.6325, -1.2649, 1.2649, -0.6325. Their root mean square is 1.8708, so RMSNorm gives 1.0690, -0.5345, 1.6036, 0. Add 2 to every entry, and layer normalization gives exactly the same output, because it removes any shift; RMSNorm gives 1.1795, 0.2949, 1.4744, 0.5898. For a vector whose mean is already zero, such as 3, 0, -3, 0, the two agree; as the paper notes, with a zero mean they are exactly equal [@zhang2019root, p. 3].

The paper's abstract reports running times cut by 7% to 64% [@zhang2019root, p. 1], a range that mixes very different settings. <!-- D-009: code/checks/since-2017.py, Example 1 --> On a 2017-style translation transformer, a thousand training steps took 231 seconds instead of 248, 6.9% faster, with comparable BLEU scores [@zhang2019root, p. 7]; on a recurrent translation model, 24.7% [@zhang2019root, p. 6]. The top of the range is a partial variant on an image-caption model [@zhang2019root, p. 8], and the authors note that the gain varies with the software framework, the hardware, and the design of the network [@zhang2019root, p. 9]. No source read for this book measures RMSNorm's speed inside a modern LLM. LLaMA, Llama 2, and DeepSeek-V3 use it [@touvron2023llama, p. 3; @touvron2023llama2, p. 5; @deepseekai2024deepseekv3, p. 22].

## The feed-forward network

**GELU.** The 2017 feed-forward network put a ReLU between its two matrices [@vaswani2017attention, p. 5]. GPT (2018) used the **Gaussian Error Linear Unit**, or **GELU**, instead [@radford2018improving, p. 5], and BERT followed [@devlin2019bert, p. 4183]. Proposed in 2016, the GELU multiplies its input x by Φ(x), the cumulative distribution function of the standard normal distribution [@hendrycks2016gaussian, p. 1]: the probability that a standard normal variable is below x. <!-- D-009: code/checks/since-2017.py, Example 2 --> An input of 2 passes almost unchanged (1.954), an input of -2 is almost zeroed (-0.046), and in between the switch is gradual. Its authors presented it as a smoothed ReLU, much as the sigmoid smoothed the step function [@hendrycks2016gaussian, pp. 5-6]. It answered no named problem; it measured slightly better.

**A gate.** In 2020 Noam Shazeer tested variants of the **gated linear unit**, which takes two linear projections of the input, passes one of them through a sigmoid, and multiplies the two entry by entry [@shazeer2020glu, p. 1]. One projection says what to pass; the other, squashed into a soft on-off switch, says how much. That is a gate, the idea behind the LSTM's gates in [the chapter on structure](#structure), here on each hidden unit. In regression terms it is an interaction term: the product of two learned linear functions of the input. The variant that stuck is **SwiGLU**, whose switch is the **Swish** function, $x\,\sigma(x)$ with $\sigma$ the logistic sigmoid, also called **SiLU** [@shazeer2020glu, pp. 1-2; @hendrycks2016gaussian, p. 2]:

$$\text{FFN}_{\text{SwiGLU}}(x) = \big(\text{Swish}(x W) \otimes x V\big)\, W_2$$

where $x$ is one position's vector, $W$ and $V$ are two input matrices of the same shape, $\otimes$ multiplies two vectors entry by entry, and $W_2$ maps the result back to the width of the residual stream.

Three matrices instead of two would add parameters, so Shazeer cut the hidden layer to two-thirds of its width [@shazeer2020glu, p. 2]. <!-- D-009: code/checks/since-2017.py, Example 2 --> In his T5 setup, two matrices of 768 by 3,072 and three of 768 by 2,048 both hold 4,718,592 weights. At equal cost, held-out log-perplexity (the logarithm of the perplexity of [the prediction chapter](#prediction), which is the average loss per token) fell from 1.677 with ReLU to 1.636 with SwiGLU, 0.041 or 2.4% lower [@shazeer2020glu, p. 2]. On downstream tasks the results were noisy, and Shazeer offered "no explanation as to why these architectures seem to work" [@shazeer2020glu, p. 3].

It was adopted anyway: by PaLM in 2022, which accepted a third matrix multiplication in the feed-forward network [@chowdhery2022palm, p. 5], by LLaMA, with a hidden layer two-thirds as wide as the usual one, which is four times the model's width [@touvron2023llama, p. 3], and by Llama 3 [@grattafiori2024llama, p. 7]. <!-- D-009: code/checks/since-2017.py, Example 2 --> The two-thirds rule is a convention, not a law: Llama 3 8B's hidden width is 14,336, 3.5 times its width of 4,096, where the rule would give about 10,923.

## Positions

Attention ignores order. The 2017 transformer added sine waves to each token's vector once, at the bottom; GPT and BERT used a learned table ([the attention chapter](#attention)). A learned table of L rows simply has no entry for any position past L. The 2017 paper had hoped that sine waves would carry a model past its training length. A 2022 paper, the one that introduced ALiBi below, tested this: its sine-wave model coped with only a few dozen tokens beyond L [@press2022train, p. 3]. That paper defined **extrapolation** as the ability to go on doing well when the inputs at validation are longer than those seen in training [@press2022train, p. 1].

So there were two problems: the model should use distances ("three tokens back") rather than absolute slots, and its window should be extendable after training.

**Relative positions.** In 2018, a paper gave attention a learned vector for each distance between query and key, clipped at a maximum, on the hypothesis that exact relative positions stop being useful beyond some distance [@shaw2018self, p. 466]. <!-- D-009: code/checks/since-2017.py, Example 3 --> With its clipping distance of 16 [@shaw2018self, p. 467], that is 33 learned distances per layer and head, each with one vector for keys and one for values, whatever the length of the text, at the cost of 7% fewer steps per second, a slowdown its authors called modest [@shaw2018self, p. 466]. T5 (2019) simplified this to a **relative position bias**: one learned number per range of distances, added to the attention score before the softmax [@raffel2020exploring, p. 5].

![Left and middle: rotary positions on one pair of numbers. The same query (1, 2) and key (3, -1) are turned by 0.5 radians per position. At positions 3 and 1, and again at 10 and 8, the angle between them is the same, so the score is the same, -5.35. Right: ALiBi's bias for one head with slope 1/2, added to each attention score in a five-token text; the further back the key, the larger the penalty.](../../figures/since-2017-positions.svg){#fig:since-2017-positions}

**Rotary positions.** In 2021 a paper proposed **rotary position embeddings**, or **RoPE**, aiming for a score between a query at position m and a key at position n that depends only on the two words and the gap m - n between them [@su2021roformer, p. 4]. Split each query and key into pairs of numbers and treat each pair as an arrow. Turn the query's arrows by an angle proportional to m and the key's by the same angle per position times n. The dot product of two arrows depends only on their lengths and the angle between them, and that angle changes by the gap between the positions; where they sit in absolute terms drops out. <!-- D-009: code/checks/since-2017.py, Example 3 --> In the figure, a query of (1, 2) and a key of (3, -1), turned 0.5 radians per position, score -5.35 at positions 3 and 1, at 10 and 8, and at 100 and 98. Direction matters: with the query at 1 and the key at 3 the score is 6.43.

Different pairs turn at different speeds, $\theta_i = 10000^{-2(i-1)/d_k}$ radians per position for the $i$-th of the $d_k/2$ pairs in a head of $d_k$ numbers (the $d_k$ of [the attention chapter](#attention)) [@su2021roformer, p. 5]: the 2017 sine waves' family of wavelengths, like clock hands running at different rates. <!-- D-009: code/checks/since-2017.py, Example 3 --> In a head of 128 numbers, the fastest pair turns once every 6.28 positions and the slowest once every 54,410. RoPE acts inside every attention layer, on queries and keys but not values [@press2022train, p. 4]. Its authors admitted that they lacked a thorough account of why it converged faster than baselines using other ways of encoding position [@su2021roformer, p. 12]. They also proved a bound by which scores tend to decay with distance [@su2021roformer, p. 5], which a 2023 Meta paper argued may be so loose that it says nothing [@chen2023extending, p. 4]; how scores behave at large gaps depends on the **base frequency**, the 10,000 in the formula, which later models changed. RoPE was used by PaLM (2022), LLaMA (2023), and Llama 3 [@chowdhery2022palm, p. 5; @touvron2023llama, p. 3; @grattafiori2024llama, p. 7].

**ALiBi.** The 2022 paper asked how a model trained on short texts could cope, once in use, with texts longer than any it had seen [@press2022train, p. 1]. Its answer, **ALiBi** (attention with linear biases, ICLR 2022), adds no position vectors at all: after each query-key score it adds a fixed penalty, minus $m$ times the distance, where $m$ is a slope set for each head before training [@press2022train, p. 5]. <!-- D-009: code/checks/since-2017.py, Example 4 --> For 8 heads the slopes are 1/2, 1/4, and so on to 1/256, picked after trying around ten sets of slopes by hand [@press2022train, p. 5]. Adding minus $m$ times the distance before the softmax multiplies each weight by $e^{-m \times \text{distance}}$ before rescaling, so attention decays exponentially with distance, the shape of exponential smoothing in time series. With equal raw scores and a slope of 1/2, a token's weights on itself and the four tokens before it are 0.4287, 0.2600, 0.1577, 0.0956, and 0.0580. A 1.3-billion-parameter model trained on 1,024 tokens matched the perplexity of a sine-wave model trained on 2,048, while training 11% faster and with 11% less memory [@press2022train, p. 1].

The paper's own appendix qualifies what this means: it attributes most of ALiBi's improvement on longer inputs to better avoiding what it calls the "early token curse" [@press2022train, p. 22]. When a long text is scored in chunks, the first tokens of each chunk have little context, and longer chunks have fewer of them. So ALiBi models keep working on texts longer than they were trained on, but mostly because early tokens get more context, not because the model uses distant text.

## Stretching the window after training

A RoPE model has no table to run out of, but one trained on 2,048 positions has never seen the angles of position 3,000. LLaMA's inputs had to be shorter than 2,048 tokens. When a team at Meta gave it longer texts, perplexity could shoot past 1,000, comparable to an untrained model; and simply training on longer texts, for more than 10,000 batches, moved the usable window only from 2,048 to 2,560 [@chen2023extending, pp. 1, 3].

Their fix, **position interpolation** (June 2023), shrinks the position numbers in proportion so that every one falls inside the window the model was trained on and none lies beyond it [@chen2023extending, p. 1]. <!-- D-009: code/checks/since-2017.py, Example 5 --> To double the window from 2,048 to 4,096, position 3,000 is treated as 1,500, a position the model knows; to reach 32,768, every position is multiplied by 1/16. This is the standard regression lesson: a fitted curve is trustworthy between its data points and wild outside them, and the Meta team make that argument themselves [@chen2023extending, p. 4]. With at most 1,000 steps of fine-tuning they extended LLaMA's window to as much as 32,768 tokens, at the cost of a slight drop on several standard benchmarks within the original 2,048 tokens [@chen2023extending, pp. 1-2]. Such extra training to lengthen a window is called **context extension**.

Squeezing every pair equally also slows the fast hands that tell neighbors apart. **YaRN** (2023) stretches only the slow pairs, and extended Llama 2 to 128K tokens with a few hundred fine-tuning steps [@peng2023yarn, p. 7] on less than about 0.1% of the original pretraining data [@peng2023yarn, p. 2]. A third route slows the whole clock by raising the base frequency. Another Meta team raised it from 10,000 to 500,000 in September 2023 [@xiong2023effective, p. 7], and Llama 3 uses 500,000 [@grattafiori2024llama, p. 7]. <!-- D-009: code/checks/since-2017.py, Example 3 --> The slowest pair of a 128-number head then turns once every 2,559,196 positions instead of 54,410. How RoPE spread, and the community posts in which some of these stretching methods also appeared, are set out in [the credit appendix](#credit-since-2017).

By 2024, context extension was planned into training: most tokens at a short length, which is cheap, then a short final stage at the full length. Llama 3 405B was pretrained at 8K tokens, then its window was raised in six gradual stages from 8K to 128K, using about 800 billion tokens [@grattafiori2024llama, p. 14]. DeepSeek-V3 (December 2024) pretrained at 4K tokens, then used YaRN in two phases of 1,000 steps each, first to 32K and then to 128K [@deepseekai2024deepseekv3, pp. 22-23]. <!-- D-009: code/checks/since-2017.py, Example 5 --> By its published GPU hours, context extension took 119,000 of 2,788,000, 4.3% [@deepseekai2024deepseekv3, p. 5].

## Fewer key and value heads

[The chapter on generation](#generation) explained the KV cache: while writing, a model keeps the keys and values of every earlier token and reads them all again for each new one. In 2019 Noam Shazeer proposed **multi-query attention**, in which all the attention heads share a single set of keys and values [@shazeer2019fast, p. 1]. Each head still asks its own question with its own query, but all consult one set of keys and values. A set of keys and values used by one or more query heads is a **key-value head**.

In Shazeer's tests on a translation model, run on Google's TPUv2 chips, the parameter count was held equal by widening the feed-forward layers [@shazeer2019fast, p. 7]. <!-- D-009: code/checks/since-2017.py, Example 6 --> Decoding took 3.8 microseconds per token instead of 46, 12.1 times faster, while BLEU fell from 26.7 to 26.5; cutting the number of heads instead cost far more (one head gave 25.8) [@shazeer2019fast, p. 8]. Google's PaLM used it at 540 billion parameters in 2022 and found that it neither helped nor hurt model quality or training speed [@chowdhery2022palm, p. 5].

Others found a quality cost. A 2023 paper warned that multi-query attention can degrade quality [@ainslie2023gqa, p. 4895] and proposed **grouped-query attention**: the query heads are split into groups, and the heads in each group share one key head and one value head [@ainslie2023gqa, p. 4896]. One group is multi-query attention; as many groups as heads is the 2017 design. Who else arrived at the same design is noted in [the credit appendix](#credit-since-2017). <!-- D-009: code/checks/since-2017.py, Example 6 --> On the paper's largest T5 model, eight groups cut the time per sample from 1.51 to 0.28 milliseconds, 5.4 times faster, with an average score of 47.1 against 47.2 for full multi-head attention and 46.6 for multi-query; the grouped and multi-query models were converted from the multi-head model with 5% extra training [@ainslie2023gqa, p. 4897]. In Meta's own comparison for Llama 2, the grouped version came close to the multi-head baseline on most tasks and, on average, beat the multi-query version [@touvron2023llama2, p. 47].

Llama 3 uses 8 key-value heads at every size [@grattafiori2024llama, pp. 6-7]. <!-- D-009: code/checks/since-2017.py, Example 6 --> The generation chapter computed its cache: 131,072 bytes per token in 16-bit numbers, four times that with 32 key-value heads. With a single key-value head it would be 16,384 bytes. For a full 131,072-token window: 68.7 GB with 32 key-value heads, 17.2 GB with 8, and 2.1 GB with one. DeepSeek-V3 shrinks the cache another way, by compressing keys and values into a smaller vector, a scheme it calls multi-head latent attention [@deepseekai2024deepseekv3, p. 7].

## Faster attention with the same answer

**The problem.** By 2022 many methods had tried to make attention cheaper by approximating it. Tri Dao and colleagues observed that these often gave no speed-up in actual running time, because they counted arithmetic operations and mostly left out the time spent moving data in and out of memory [@dao2022flashattention, p. 1]. A GPU has large main memory and a small, fast on-chip memory. On NVIDIA's A100, main memory held 40 to 80 GB, read at 1.5 to 2.0 terabytes per second, while each of 108 processing units had 192 kB on chip, read at an estimated 19 terabytes per second [@dao2022flashattention, p. 3]: <!-- D-009: code/checks/since-2017.py, Example 7 --> about 21 MB of fast memory in all. Operations such as softmax do little arithmetic per number, so their speed is set by how fast numbers move, the memory-bound situation of [the generation chapter](#generation).

Standard attention writes the whole table of scores to main memory, reads it back for the softmax, writes the weights, and reads them again. <!-- D-009: code/checks/since-2017.py, Example 7 --> For one head in one layer, in 16-bit numbers, that table is 2.1 MB at 1,024 tokens, 134.2 MB at 8,192, and 34.4 GB at 131,072, while the output it is used to make is only 33.6 MB.

**The change.** **FlashAttention** (NeurIPS 2022) is **IO-aware**: it is organized around reads and writes, not arithmetic, and its main goal is to avoid writing the table of attention scores to main memory (HBM, in the paper) and reading it back [@dao2022flashattention, p. 2]. It splits queries, keys, and values into blocks that fit on chip and works through them block by block, a technique called **tiling**, familiar from blocked algorithms in numerical linear algebra [@dao2022flashattention, p. 2]. The difficulty is the softmax, which needs a sum over a whole row before any weight is known. FlashAttention computes it incrementally, with a running maximum and a running sum corrected as each block arrives, much as a mean can be computed in one pass over a stream. For training, it recomputes the scores in the backward pass instead of storing them [@dao2022flashattention, p. 2].

The result is **exact attention**, in the paper's word: mathematically the same output as the standard method, unlike the approximations [@dao2022flashattention, p. 1]. The scores are still computed, and their number still grows with the square of the length; what disappears is the table in main memory and the traffic to and from it.

**What it bought** was training GPT-2 three times faster at 1K tokens, up to nine times fewer reads and writes of main memory, and memory use that grows only in proportion to the length [@dao2022flashattention, pp. 1-2]. Speed bought length: it trained GPT-2 at 4K tokens faster than an earlier implementation trained it at 1K, and reached a perplexity 0.7 better [@dao2022flashattention, p. 7]. **What it cost** was engineering: in the authors' approach, each new variant of attention needed its own GPU program, written in a language much lower-level than PyTorch [@dao2022flashattention, p. 10]. LLaMA was trained with an efficient attention implementation that used FlashAttention's backward pass [@touvron2023llama, p. 3]. How FlashAttention relates to an earlier tiled method is set out in [the credit appendix](#credit-since-2017).

## Not looking at everything

Another answer to the square is to compute fewer scores. In **sparse attention**, each position attends to a structured subset. In OpenAI's Sparse Transformer (April 2019), one head looked at the l positions just before and the other at every lth position, with l near the square root of the length [@child2019generating, p. 4], so the work grows like the length times its square root. <!-- D-009: code/checks/since-2017.py, Example 8 --> At 16,384 tokens that is on the order of 2 million scores instead of about 268 million, roughly a hundred times fewer. The pattern must suit the data: its authors found that on data with no periodic structure, text among them, the strided pattern could fail to get information to where it was needed [@child2019generating, p. 4]. GPT-3 alternated ordinary layers with layers of locally banded sparse attention [@brown2020language, p. 8].

**Sliding-window attention** keeps only a local band: each token attends to a fixed number of tokens before it. Stacked layers widen the reach, as in convolutional networks [@beltagy2020longformer, p. 3]: the receptive field of stacked convolutions in [the structure chapter](#structure). Mistral 7B (2023) used a window of 4,096 in each of 32 layers, which its paper says gives a "theoretical" attention span of about 131K tokens [@jiang2023mistral, p. 2]. The paper does not show how much information survives that many hops. <!-- D-009: code/checks/since-2017.py, Example 8 --> The practical gain is a cache that never holds more than 4,096 positions, 8 times smaller at 32,768 tokens [@jiang2023mistral, p. 2]. Mistral AI's Mixtral 8x7B (released December 2023, paper January 2024) [@mistral2023mixtral] instead used full, dense attention over a context of 32K tokens [@jiang2024mixtral, p. 2].

A larger window is not the same as a well-used one. A common test, the needle in a haystack of [the chapter on prompting](#prompting), plants one fact in a long text and asks the model to find it; Llama 3's team used it to judge its context extension [@grattafiori2024llama, p. 14]. The vendors warn that it is a low bar. Google's documentation (as of 2026-11) says the model is less accurate when it must find several "needles" instead of one [@google2026longcontext]; Anthropic's says that adding context does not by itself make results better, since accuracy and recall decline as the token count rises, an effect known as *context rot* [@anthropic2026contextwindows]. The window is an architectural and training fact; how well a model uses it is measured separately, as in the studies of [the chapter on prompting](#prompting).

## Mixture of experts

**The problem.** In an ordinary, **dense** network (dense in a different sense from the dense vectors of [the embeddings chapter](#embeddings)), every parameter is used for every token, so more parameters always mean more computation. In 2017 Shazeer and colleagues started from the point that the number of parameters limits how much information a network can absorb. **Conditional computation**, in which each example uses only some parts of the network, had been put forward, in theory, as a route to far more capacity without a matching rise in computation [@shazeer2017outrageously, p. 1].

**The change.** The **mixture of experts** is a statistical idea from the early 1990s [@shazeer2017outrageously, p. 3]: several models, the **experts**, and a gating model that decides how much to trust each one for a given input. Shazeer and colleagues made it a layer inside a network and made it sparse: the layer holds many experts, each a simple feed-forward network, and a gating network, trained along with them, chooses for each input a few experts and how much weight to give each [@shazeer2017outrageously, p. 2]. The gating network, now usually called the **router**, is a softmax classifier, a multinomial logistic regression, with one class per expert. **Top-k routing** keeps the k highest-scoring experts and runs only those. In the standard form [@shazeer2017outrageously, p. 3; @jiang2024mixtral, p. 2]:

$$y = \sum_{i=1}^{n} G(x)_i\, E_i(x), \qquad G(x) = \text{softmax}\big(\text{TopK}(x W_g)\big)$$

where $x$ is one token's vector, $n$ the number of experts, $E_i(x)$ the output of expert $i$, $W_g$ the router's weights, which give one score per expert, and TopK keeps the k largest scores and sets the rest to minus infinity, so that their weights $G(x)_i$ are exactly zero and those experts need not run.

In the figure, the router's made-up scores are highest for experts 3 and 6, 2.1 and 1.7; the softmax over those two gives weights of 0.599 and 0.401, and the other six do nothing for this token. Think of a decision tree that stores every branch but evaluates one path.

The 2017 layer sat between recurrent layers. <!-- D-009: code/checks/since-2017.py, Example 10 --> On a benchmark of a billion words, a mixture with 4,303 million parameters but 8.9 million operations per time step reached a test perplexity of 34.1 after ten passes over the training data, against 34.7 for the best published model after the same number (it reached 30.6 when trained ten times longer), with 151 million of each [@shazeer2017outrageously, p. 7]: 5.9% of the computation. **What it cost**, by the authors' account: GPUs are much better at arithmetic than at branching, and communication bandwidth can become a bottleneck [@shazeer2017outrageously, p. 2]. Worse, the router tends to fall into favoring the same few experts, and since the favorites are trained more, it picks them still more often [@shazeer2017outrageously, p. 5]. Their remedy, an extra loss term that penalizes unequal use, is a form of **load balancing**, which every design since has had to address.

**Into the transformer.** The Switch Transformer (2021), with Shazeer again among its authors, replaced a transformer's feed-forward sub-layer with experts and sent each token to a single one [@fedus2022switch, p. 6]. Built on T5, it reported pretraining up to seven times faster for the same computational resources [@fedus2022switch, p. 1]; its largest model had 1.6 trillion parameters in 2,048 experts [@fedus2022switch, p. 23], of which each token used a small fraction. It also reported instability from the routing decisions, worse in 16-bit numbers [@fedus2022switch, p. 8].

**An open example.** Mistral AI's Mixtral 8x7B (paper January 2024) is Mistral 7B with eight feed-forward blocks, the experts, in each layer, two chosen per token [@jiang2024mixtral, p. 1]. Its authors distinguish the **total parameter count**, all the parameters stored, from the **active parameter count**, those used for one token [@jiang2024mixtral, p. 3]: a token can draw on 47 billion parameters but at inference passes through only 13 billion of them [@jiang2024mixtral, p. 1].

<!-- D-009: code/checks/since-2017.py, Example 10 -->

The name suggests 8 times 7, or 56 billion. It is less because only the feed-forward networks are copied; attention, the token tables, and the normalizations are shared. From the published shapes, each of 32 layers has 41,943,040 attention parameters and eight SwiGLU experts of 176,160,768, and the two token tables hold 262,144,000. That gives about 46.7 billion in total and 12.9 billion active, matching the printed figures, under assumptions the papers do not state (no biases, separate input and output tables). The experts hold 96.6% of all the parameters.

In its authors' own evaluation, Mixtral did as well as or better than Llama 2 70B on almost every common benchmark, with a fifth of the active parameters [@jiang2024mixtral, p. 4]. Computation per token follows the active count; memory follows the total, so serving Mixtral needs memory in proportion to all 47 billion of its parameters [@jiang2024mixtral, p. 4]. [The chapter on local inference](#local) turns this into memory arithmetic.

Whether experts specialize is disputed. The 2017 paper reported that its experts tended to take on narrow roles, defined by syntax and by meaning [@shazeer2017outrageously, p. 3]. Mixtral's authors found that the choice of experts did not obviously follow the topic: the mix of experts chosen was much the same for arXiv papers, biology abstracts, and philosophy, and the routing suggested some structured behavior tied to syntax instead [@jiang2024mixtral, p. 7]. A "mathematics expert" is not what was measured.

**Many small experts.** DeepSeek-V3 (December 2024) has 671 billion parameters in total, of which 37 billion are active for each token [@deepseekai2024deepseekv3, p. 1], <!-- D-009: code/checks/since-2017.py, Example 10 --> 5.5% active. Each of its expert layers has 1 shared expert and 256 routed experts, of which 8 are activated for each token [@deepseekai2024deepseekv3, p. 22]: smaller experts than earlier designs, plus a **shared expert** that every token uses [@deepseekai2024deepseekv3, p. 8]. Its report calls its training "remarkably stable" and gives its cost as 2.788 million GPU hours [@deepseekai2024deepseekv3, p. 1], for the final training run only, not counting earlier research and ablation experiments [@deepseekai2024deepseekv3, p. 5].

**Dense or sparse.** The reports, in time order, do not add up to one verdict. In 2021 the Switch Transformer found routing a source of instability. In July 2024 Meta kept Llama 3 a standard dense transformer instead of a mixture of experts, putting training stability first [@grattafiori2024llama, p. 2]. In December 2024 DeepSeek reported a stable mixture. In April 2025 Meta announced Llama 4 as its first models with a mixture-of-experts architecture: Maverick with 17 billion active parameters of 400 billion, and Scout with 17 billion of 109 billion [@meta2025llama4]. Its claim that, at a fixed training budget, such models give better quality than a dense model is Meta's own [@meta2025llama4]. One consequence for reading model reports: a parameter count alone no longer says how much computation a model uses, which is why GPT-3's paper in 2020 claimed to be ten times larger than any previous "non-sparse" language model, leaving sparse ones out of the comparison ([the chapter on scale](#scale)).

## Another route: state-space models

Everything above keeps attention. One line of work replaces it. Albert Gu and Tri Dao's Mamba (December 2023) is a recurrent model, in the family of [the structure chapter](#structure): it carries a fixed-size state from token to token, so its cost grows in proportion to the length rather than its square. Earlier models of this kind, **state space models**, had trailed attention on key kinds of data, language among them; Mamba lets the update depend on the current token, so that the model can choose what to carry along the sequence and what to forget [@gu2023mamba, p. 1], an echo of the LSTM's gates. Its authors reported five times the throughput of transformers, and a 3-billion-parameter model that matched transformers twice its size [@gu2023mamba, p. 1]. They also wrote that their evaluation covered only small models, and that whether Mamba would keep up at larger sizes had yet to be tested [@gu2023mamba, p. 17]. That was their evidence as of the paper's May 2024 revision.

## How much the changes matter

The changes fall into a few kinds. FlashAttention and fewer key-value heads answered a hardware problem: moving and storing data. RoPE and its extensions answered a length problem. The mixture of experts answered a cost problem, by separating parameters from computation. The normalization and activation changes were small measured gains on particular setups.

Few came from theory. Shazeer offered no explanation for his gates, RoPE's authors no thorough one for its faster training, and ALiBi's slopes came from trying about ten sets. The people who used them were clear about their weight: the Llama 3 paper says its architecture differs little from Llama and Llama 2, and that its gains came primarily from better and more diverse data and larger-scale training [@grattafiori2024llama, p. 6]. An architecture section in a current paper is mostly a list of these choices, each a small adjustment to the 2017 skeleton.

## What to carry forward

- The 2017 skeleton survives: residual stream, attention, feed-forward network, each sub-layer adding its result back. "Current" here means published open-weight models of 2024-2025; closed models disclose little of theirs.
- Pre-norm normalizes a copy at each sub-layer's input and leaves the stream alone. It trains more reliably; post-norm, when it trains, may end slightly better (one 2020 study).
- RMSNorm divides by the root mean square without subtracting the mean: about 7% faster on a 2017-style translation transformer, with comparable accuracy.
- SwiGLU adds a gate to the feed-forward network: three matrices, a hidden layer narrowed to keep the cost, 2.4% lower held-out loss in one 2020 setup, and no explanation.
- RoPE rotates pairs of numbers in queries and keys by angles proportional to position, so scores depend only on the gap. ALiBi subtracts a penalty proportional to distance. Neither makes a model use distant text well by itself.
- A rotary model can be stretched after training by squeezing positions (interpolation), stretching only the slow pairs (YaRN), or raising the base frequency (Llama 3 uses 500,000). Open models of 2024 train short and extend at the end.
- Multi-query and grouped-query attention share keys and values among query heads, shrinking the KV cache with little quality loss. Llama 3 uses 8 key-value heads.
- FlashAttention computes exact attention in tiles in fast on-chip memory. It removes the score table's memory traffic, not the square-law arithmetic.
- Sparse and sliding-window attention compute fewer scores; their reach across a long text is indirect, through stacked layers.
- Windows grew from 512 tokens (2018) to 128K in open models (2024) and about a million in vendor documentation (as of 2026-11). A large window does not prove a model uses it well.
- A mixture of experts replaces the feed-forward network with many experts and a router that picks a few per token. Memory follows total parameters, computation active ones: Mixtral 46.7B and 12.9B, DeepSeek-V3 671B and 37B. Whether experts specialize by topic, and how stable such training is, are open questions.
- Most of these changes were adopted because they measured better or ran faster. By Meta's account, data and scale, not architecture, drove most of Llama 3's gains over Llama 2.

# Who did what first {#credit}

<!-- D-026: credit and priority questions moved here from the chapters; each side is presented and cited, and no winner is picked. D-005: every claim comes from a verified chapter and its ledger. D-003: no code. -->

The chapters tell the history of the ideas as a story and leave out most questions of priority: who was first, how much a predecessor deserves, and whose account to trust. Those questions are collected here, in the order of the chapters they belong to. Each entry sets out what the sources say and where they differ, and does not choose between them. Who counts as first depends on what is counted: the first statement of an idea, the first system that used it, or the first result that made the field adopt it.

## The artificial neuron {#credit-neuron}

*From [The artificial neuron](#neuron).*

**Did *Perceptrons* cause the decline?** Interest in neural networks fell away in the period of the 1969 book *Perceptrons*, in what the deep learning textbook of Ian Goodfellow, Yoshua Bengio, and Aaron Courville calls "the first major dip in the popularity of neural networks" [@goodfellow2016deep, p. 14]. What part Marvin Minsky and Seymour Papert's book played in that decline is disputed, and there are two accounts of it.

The common account treats the book as the cause. The textbook puts it this way: "Critics who observed these flaws in linear models caused a backlash against biologically inspired learning in general (Minsky and Papert, 1969). This was the first major dip in the popularity of neural networks" [@goodfellow2016deep, p. 14]. Elsewhere it says that critics including Marvin Minsky "pointed out several of the flaws of the linear model family, such as its inability to learn the XOR function, which led to a backlash against the entire neural network approach" [@goodfellow2016deep, p. 221].

The sociological account, from Mikel Olazaran's history based on the literature and on interviews with researchers of the period, argues that this is too simple. "In popular versions of the history of neural networks," he writes, "Minsky and Papert's Perceptrons study is usually taken as the proof that neural network research had so many problems that it was not worth pursuing, and that therefore it had to be abandoned" [@olazaran1993sociological, p. 358]. His central point is about timing. The main arguments of the book, he writes, "were well known by the mid-1960s and had critically affected the development of neural network research by then"; the 1969 book was "the 'final push,' so to speak, for the closure of the controversy" [@olazaran1993sociological, p. 357]. The common view, in his words, "was the result of the closure of the perceptron controversy, and not its cause" [@olazaran1993sociological, p. 358]. In his reading, the single-layer limits were real results, but the verdict on multilayer networks was a judgment, and problems like parity and connectedness became decisive "not because of some intrinsic or necessary property" but because of how the research community of the time interpreted them [@olazaran1993sociological, p. 359]. He also places the book inside a contest for funding, describing symbolic AI researchers who "saw neural network researchers' efforts to obtain funding for building more complex machines as a direct threat to their research interests" [@olazaran1993sociological, p. 346]. One researcher he interviewed, from the Stanford Research Institute group that left neural networks in the mid-1960s, recalled moving to a robot project and having to sell it to the funding agency, "to some of the people who didn't like perceptrons" [@olazaran1993sociological, pp. 372-373].

The two accounts agree on the mathematics and on the decline. They disagree about cause and timing: whether the book produced a backlash against the whole approach, or whether it was the last word in a controversy already largely settled in the mid-1960s, in which a research community, in a contest over direction and money, came to read results about single-layer networks as a verdict on all of them.

## Learning in layers {#credit-backprop}

*From [Learning in layers](#backprop).*

**Who invented backpropagation.** The 1986 Nature letter of David Rumelhart, Geoffrey Hinton, and Ronald Williams presented back-propagation in its abstract as "a new learning procedure" [@rumelhart1986learning, p. 533], and noted that variants of it had been discovered independently by David Parker and by Yann Le Cun [@rumelhart1986learning, p. 535]. The PDP chapter of the same year put it this way: "Parker (1985) has independently derived a similar generalization", and "Le Cun (1985) has also studied a roughly similar learning scheme" [@rumelhart1986internal, p. 322]. Later histories trace the method further back. Gradient methods for multi-stage systems, computed by applying the chain rule backward stage by stage, were "discussed at least since the early 1960s" in optimal control, by Kelley, Bryson, and others [@schmidhuber2014deep, p. 11; @goodfellow2016deep, p. 221]. Andreas Griewank, in a history of automatic differentiation, recounts that Seppo Linnainmaa of Helsinki "says the idea came to him on a sunny afternoon in a Copenhagen park in 1970", and that he used it to estimate the effect of rounding errors in computations, not to train networks; Griewank adds that Gerardi Ostrowski had discovered and used it some five years earlier, in process models in chemical engineering [@griewank2012invented, p. 389]. Paul Werbos, a doctoral student in applied mathematics at Harvard in the early 1970s, considered applying a technique for propagating derivatives backward, which he called "dynamic feedback", to neural-network-like problems [@olazaran1993sociological, p. 392]; in the thesis itself he applied it to an example from political science, not to neural networks [@olazaran1993sociological, p. 394].

The accounts differ in emphasis. Jürgen Schmidhuber's survey, which states that one of its goals is "to assign credit to those who contributed to the present state of the art" [@schmidhuber2014deep, p. 1], says that efficient backpropagation in general networks "apparently was first described" in Linnainmaa's 1970 master's thesis, "albeit without reference to NNs" (neural networks). It describes Werbos's 1974 thesis as "preliminary, NN-specific discussion", dates the first neural-network application of efficient backpropagation to Werbos in 1981 ("to my knowledge", it adds), calls the 1985 work of Parker and LeCun "related work" published "several years later", and says that the 1986 work "significantly contributed to the popularisation" of backpropagation, "experimentally demonstrating the emergence of useful internal representations in hidden layers" [@schmidhuber2014deep, p. 11]. The deep learning textbook of Goodfellow, Bengio, and Courville credits Werbos (1981) with proposing the technique for training networks; in its account the idea "was finally developed in practice after being independently rediscovered in different ways", by LeCun, by Parker, and by Rumelhart and colleagues, and the PDP chapter "contributed greatly to the popularization of back-propagation" [@goodfellow2016deep, p. 221]. Mikel Olazaran's sociological history of the neural network controversy, for its part, says that "it seems that the first attempt to apply something similar to back-propagation to neural network research was Paul Werbos'" [@olazaran1993sociological, p. 392]. None of these accounts disputes the chronology. They differ on whether the credit belongs to the first statement of the mathematics, to its first proposal for neural networks, or to the work that showed it working and made the field adopt it. LeCun, one of the independent discoverers, made the deflationary point in 1988: "back-propagation is little more than an extremely judicious application of the chain rule and gradient descent" [quoted in @olazaran1993sociological, p. 392].

**Two recollections of the 1970s.** In an interview for Olazaran's history, Werbos recalled that his thesis committee doubted the work and that Marvin Minsky, whom he consulted, was discouraging [@olazaran1993sociological, pp. 392-393]. Minsky, interviewed separately, recalled the idea as "certainly trivial" and not practical, adding that "you usually get stuck at a local minimum" [@olazaran1993sociological, pp. 393-394]. These are two participants' recollections, decades later.

## Networks with structure, and the second winter {#credit-structure}

*From [Networks with structure, and the second winter](#structure).*

**Convolutional networks.** Kunihiko Fukushima's neocognitron of 1980 already had shared weights, but it was self-organized by "learning without a teacher" rather than trained by backpropagation on labeled examples [@fukushima1980neocognitron, pp. 193, 194-195]. Later accounts agree on these facts and differ on emphasis. Jürgen Schmidhuber's survey says the neocognitron "introduced convolutional NNs" [@schmidhuber2014deep, p. 10]. Yann LeCun, Yoshua Bengio, and Geoffrey Hinton say convolutional networks "have their roots in the neocognitron, the architecture of which was somewhat similar, but did not have an end-to-end supervised-learning algorithm such as backpropagation" [@lecun2015deep, p. 439]. The 1989 paper by LeCun and colleagues, which trained a convolutional network by backpropagation to read zip codes, credits the idea of weight sharing itself to Rumelhart, Hinton, and Williams in 1986 [@lecun1989backpropagation, p. 544], and backpropagation had already been used from 1988 to train time-delay networks, the one-dimensional kind, for speech [@goodfellow2016deep, pp. 361-362]. A summary that fits all the sources: the neocognitron had the architecture, and what it lacked was a way to train every weight from labeled examples.

**Recurrent networks.** Jeffrey Elman's 1990 paper credits its approach to earlier work by Jordan in 1986 [@elman1990finding, p. 179], and the deep learning textbook of Goodfellow, Bengio, and Courville traces recurrent networks to Rumelhart and colleagues in the same year [@goodfellow2016deep, p. 367]. Elman's simple recurrent network is an early example, not the first.

**The vanishing gradient.** Two analyses of the problem are each credited with identifying it. Sepp Hochreiter analyzed it in his diploma thesis at the Technical University of Munich, dated 15 June 1991 and written in German, with Jürgen Schmidhuber as his advisor [@hochreiter1991untersuchungen]. Yoshua Bengio, Patrice Simard, and Paolo Frasconi published their analysis, of the trade-off between storing information robustly and training by gradient descent, in 1994 [@bengio1994learning, pp. 157-158]. Schmidhuber's survey calls the thesis "a milestone of explicit DL research" (DL meaning deep learning) and says it "formally identified a major reason" why deep networks were hard to train [@schmidhuber2014deep, p. 16]; the LSTM paper, by Hochreiter and Schmidhuber, calls Bengio and colleagues' work "a very similar, more recent analysis" [@hochreiter1997long, p. 1740]. Goodfellow, Bengio, and Courville describe the problem as "independently discovered by separate researchers (Hochreiter, 1991; Bengio et al., 1993, 1994)" [@goodfellow2016deep, p. 398]. The two groups later wrote a joint account of the problem [@hochreiter2001gradient].

## The turn: data, compute, and depth {#credit-deep-turn}

*From [The turn: data, compute, and depth](#deep-turn).*

**Where deep learning began.** The deep learning textbook of Goodfellow, Bengio, and Courville says the current wave "began with a breakthrough in 2006", Geoffrey Hinton's layer-wise training, quickly extended by other groups affiliated with the Canadian Institute for Advanced Research [@goodfellow2016deep, p. 18], and Yann LeCun, Yoshua Bengio, and Geoffrey Hinton tell it the same way [@lecun2015deep, p. 439]. Both accounts come from members of that group. Jürgen Schmidhuber's survey stresses a longer line: deep learning research results "have been published at least since 1991", and only "the expression Deep Learning was actually coined around 2006" [@schmidhuber2014deep, p. 21].

**The 2012 ImageNet result.** Schmidhuber's history places SuperVision, the 2012 winner, in a line of GPU-trained convolutional networks, among them networks from his own laboratory that won other image-recognition contests in 2011 [@schmidhuber2014deep, pp. 23-24]. The SuperVision paper itself cites that work [@krizhevsky2012imagenet, pp. 1, 3].

## The training loop {#credit-toolkit}

*From [The training loop](#toolkit).*

**Momentum.** Later authors credit the momentum method to the numerical optimization work of Boris Polyak in 1964 [@sutskever2013importance, p. 1140; @goodfellow2016deep, p. 292]. The 1986 backpropagation papers used it without citing Polyak [@rumelhart1986learning, pp. 535-536; @rumelhart1986internal, p. 330], and nothing read for this book says they took it from him. Polyak's paper was not read for this book; it is cited here through those later accounts.

**RMSProp.** RMSProp was described by Geoffrey Hinton in lecture slides for a course on neural networks. The slide that gives its details attaches the note "Tijmen Tieleman, unpublished" to its central step, dividing the gradient by the square root of a running average of its square [@hinton2012lecture6, slide 29]. Later papers cite the method as Tieleman and Hinton, 2012 [@kingma2015adam, p. 1].

## Making deep networks trainable {#credit-depth}

*From [Making deep networks trainable](#depth).*

**Highway networks and residual connections.** In May 2015, Rupesh Srivastava, Klaus Greff, and Jürgen Schmidhuber described highway networks, which carry the input around each block through a learned gate. "In preliminary experiments, we found that highway networks as deep as 900 layers can be optimized using simple Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD) with momentum" [@srivastava2015highway, p. 1]. Kaiming He, Xiangyu Zhang, Shaoqing Ren, and Jian Sun, the authors of the residual network (ResNet), call highway networks "concurrent with our work" and stress the difference: highway gates can close, while "our identity shortcuts are never closed" [@he2015deep, p. 2]. Schmidhuber's 2022 history calls ResNet "a version (with open gates) of our earlier Highway Net (May 2015)" [@schmidhuber2022annotated, p. 12]. The dates are not in dispute: highway networks were posted in May 2015 and ResNet in December 2015, both built on older shortcut connections [@he2015deep, p. 2], and ResNet's ungated version won the competition.

## Language as prediction {#credit-prediction}

*From [Language as prediction](#prediction).*

**The word "bit".** Shannon's 1948 paper uses the unit but gives the name to someone else: "If the base 2 is used the resulting units may be called binary digits, or more briefly bits, a word suggested by J. W. Tukey" [@shannon1948mathematical, p. 380].

**"Language model" and "perplexity".** Two accounts differ in how much they give the IBM speech recognition group. Fred Jelinek, who led it, wrote in 2009 that the terms "acoustic model" and "language model", "now used throughout the field, were 'invented' at IBM, as was the term perplexity" [@jelinek2009dawn, p. 487]; the quotation marks around "invented" are his own. A current textbook puts it more narrowly: the terms "language model" and "perplexity" "were first used for this technology by the IBM group" [@jurafsky2026speech, p. 22]. Both place the first use of the terms in this technology at IBM; only Jelinek's account claims more than that.

**Who brought n-grams back.** In Jelinek's account, the IBM group turned to natural speech around 1978, and "at John Cocke's suggestion we decided to model English by trigrams" [@jelinek2009dawn, pp. 488-489]. The textbook gives the return of n-grams to two labs, Jelinek and colleagues at IBM and James Baker at Carnegie Mellon, and says that they used n-grams in their speech recognizers independently and at the same time [@jurafsky2026speech, p. 22].

## Words as vectors {#credit-embeddings}

*From [Words as vectors](#embeddings).*

**Harris, Firth, and the builders of word vectors.** Textbooks tell the distributional idea as a line from the linguists of the 1950s to modern word vectors. Daniel Jurafsky and James Martin's textbook (as of its 2026-08 draft) says the distributional hypothesis "was first formulated in the 1950s by linguists like Joos (1950), Harris (1954), and Firth (1957)" [@jurafsky2026embeddings, p. 1]. Mikael Brunila and Jack LaViolette's close reading notes that Firth and Harris are "often credited with the invention of 'distributional semantics'" [@brunila2022company, p. 1], and finds that the vector-space papers of the 1990s, such as LSA, "generally did not cite Firth or Harris" [@brunila2022company, p. 2]. A text search made for this book finds no mention of either linguist in Scott Deerwester and colleagues' LSA paper of 1990, the 2003 journal paper on the neural language model by Yoshua Bengio and colleagues, Ronan Collobert and Jason Weston's paper of 2008, the two word2vec papers of 2013 by Tomas Mikolov and colleagues, or the GloVe paper by Jeffrey Pennington, Richard Socher, and Christopher Manning. The second word2vec paper states the distributional idea in its own terms, without citing anyone for it [@mikolov2013distributed, p. 7]. The analyses that followed did cite Harris: Omer Levy and Yoav Goldberg's 2014 paper on skip-gram names "the distributional hypothesis of Harris" as the basis of "the common paradigm" for deriving word representations [@levy2014neural, p. 1], and their 2015 study with Ido Dagan describes one kind of term in a similarity measure between embeddings as "the manifestation of Harris's (1954) distributional hypothesis" [@levy2015improving, p. 216]. On this evidence, the line from Harris and Firth to word2vec was drawn mostly afterward, in textbooks, surveys, and analyses. Citations alone do not show whether the idea reached the builders of the vectors by other routes.

**Neural word vectors.** Who deserves credit for neural word vectors is told in more than one way. Yann LeCun, Yoshua Bengio, and Geoffrey Hinton's 2015 review credits the 2000 conference paper of Bengio, Réjean Ducharme, and Pascal Vincent with introducing "neural language models, which learn to convert a word symbol into a word vector or word embedding" [@lecun2015deep, p. 443]. Ronan Collobert and Jason Weston wrote in 2008 that the earlier language-model authors "were never able to obtain a good embedding of their words" [@collobert2008unified, p. 6], and the 2003 journal version by Bengio, Ducharme, Vincent, and Christian Jauvin reports perplexities but no lists of similar words. A summary that fits these sources: Bengio and colleagues introduced the neural language model with a learned vector per word; Collobert and Weston made the vectors the goal and showed them useful; and word2vec, by Tomas Mikolov and colleagues, made them cheap.

## Reading and writing sequences {#credit-seq2seq}

*From [Reading and writing sequences](#seq2seq).*

**The encoder-decoder.** Four steps can each be called the first. Models that read a sentence into a representation and wrote a translation from it were tried in 1997, but never on enough data to get past toy examples [@koehn2017neural, p. 5]. In 2013, Nal Kalchbrenner and Phil Blunsom built a translation model "purely based on continuous representations for words, phrases and sentences" [@kalchbrenner2013recurrent, p. 1700]; Ilya Sutskever, Oriol Vinyals, and Quoc Le called them "the first to map the input sentence into a vector and then back to a sentence" [@sutskever2014sequence, p. 8]. In June 2014, Kyunghyun Cho and colleagues proposed the "RNN Encoder-Decoder", used as one more score inside a phrase-based system [@cho2014learning, pp. 1724, 1727]. In September 2014, Sutskever and colleagues' network translated on its own, and in their words (SMT is statistical machine translation, MT machine translation) "it is the first time that a pure neural translation system outperforms a phrase-based SMT baseline on a large MT task by a sizeable margin" [@sutskever2014sequence, pp. 5-6].

The deep learning textbook of Goodfellow, Bengio, and Courville gives the architecture to the two 2014 groups: it "was first proposed by Cho et al. (2014a) and shortly after by Sutskever et al. (2014), who independently developed that architecture and were the first to obtain state-of-the-art translation using this approach" [@goodfellow2016deep, pp. 390, 392]. "State-of-the-art" is generous: Sutskever and colleagues' networks beat a phrase-based baseline, 34.81 BLEU against 33.30, but not the best system of the day, which scored 37.0 [@sutskever2014sequence, pp. 5-6]. A summary that fits all the sources: the idea was old; Kalchbrenner and Blunsom, then Cho and colleagues, built neural versions inside existing systems; and Sutskever and colleagues, by their own account, were the first to make a network alone beat a phrase-based baseline on a large task by a sizeable margin.

**Attention.** Dzmitry Bahdanau, Kyunghyun Cho, and Yoshua Bengio posted their attention paper in September 2014, nine days before Sutskever and colleagues posted theirs [@bahdanau2015neural, p. 1]. The mechanism had a forerunner in Alex Graves's 2013 handwriting network, whose "'soft window'" over the text "learns to decide which character to write next" [@graves2013generating, pp. 24, 26]. Sutskever and colleagues described Graves's window as "a novel differentiable attention mechanism", of which Bahdanau and colleagues applied "an elegant variant" to translation [@sutskever2014sequence, p. 2]. Bahdanau and colleagues acknowledged Graves's work and named the difference: his window could only move forward through the text, which in translation "is a severe limitation, as (long-distance) reordering is often needed" [@bahdanau2015neural, p. 8].

## Attention without recurrence {#credit-attention}

*From [Attention without recurrence](#attention).*

**Who did what in the team.** The 2017 paper has eight authors, all of whom did the work at Google, and a footnote that begins "Equal contribution. Listing order is random." It then credits individuals: "Jakob proposed replacing RNNs with self-attention and started the effort to evaluate this idea. Ashish, with Illia, designed and implemented the first Transformer models and has been crucially involved in every aspect of this work. Noam proposed scaled dot-product attention, multi-head attention and the parameter-free position representation" [@vaswani2017attention, p. 1]. Jakob is Jakob Uszkoreit, Ashish is Ashish Vaswani (listed first), Illia is Illia Polosukhin, and Noam is Noam Shazeer [@vaswani2017attention, p. 1]. The other authors are credited with model variants, the code base, and the software that made the later results possible. Since the listing order is random, the order of the names says nothing about who did what.

**The first model built from attention alone.** The paper's claim, in its own words: "To the best of our knowledge, however, the Transformer is the first transduction model relying entirely on self-attention to compute representations of its input and output without using sequence-aligned RNNs or convolution" [@vaswani2017attention, p. 2]. Each part of that sentence narrows it. Attention within a single sentence had been used before, usually inside or on top of a recurrent network: by Jianpeng Cheng and colleagues in a reading model [@cheng2016long, p. 551], by Zhouhan Lin and colleagues for sentence embeddings [@lin2017structured, p. 1], and by Romain Paulus and colleagues in a summarizer [@paulus2017deep, p. 1]. Query, keys, values, and a softmax over dot products were already the working parts of the end-to-end memory networks of Sainbayar Sukhbaatar and colleagues [@sukhbaatar2015end, p. 2], and the key-value memory networks of Alexander Miller and colleagues used the names "key" and "value" [@miller2016key, p. 1401]. Attention without any recurrence had been used in 2016 by Ankur Parikh and colleagues, with Uszkoreit as a co-author, on a task of comparing two sentences rather than translating, which made their model "trivially parallelizable" [@parikh2016decomposable, p. 2249]. The footnote above credits the same Uszkoreit with proposing, inside the 2017 team, to replace recurrent networks with self-attention [@vaswani2017attention, p. 1].

## Anatomy of a transformer {#credit-anatomy}

*From [Anatomy of a transformer](#anatomy).*

**The decoder-only transformer.** The 2017 transformer already had a masked decoder, in which no position could attend to later ones [@vaswani2017attention, p. 3]; a decoder-only model keeps that stack and drops the encoder. In January 2018, Peter Liu and colleagues at Google, working on summarizing Wikipedia sources, wrote that they "introduce a decoder-only architecture" [@liu2018generating, p. 1]: the encoder dropped, input and output joined into a single sequence, and the decoder "trained as a standard language model" [@liu2018generating, p. 5]. A few months later OpenAI's GPT used "a multi-layer Transformer decoder" for its language model, citing Liu and colleagues for it [@radford2018improving, p. 3]. Later papers credit the design in different ways: the 2020 scaling-laws paper cites both Liu and colleagues and GPT for its decoder-only models [@kaplan2020scaling, p. 6], while the T5 paper cites GPT and a 2019 character-level language model for language models built from a single transformer stack [@raffel2020exploring, p. 4].

**The term "residual stream".** A 2021 interpretability paper by Nelson Elhage and colleagues developed the term, writing that each layer adds its results into "what we call the 'residual stream'" [@elhage2021mathematical]. Jared Kaplan and colleagues had already used it in January 2020, describing the model width as the "dimension of the residual stream" [@kaplan2020scaling, p. 6]. The two papers share some of their authors.

## Pretraining: learn language first, tasks later {#credit-pretraining}

*From [Pretraining: learn language first, tasks later](#pretraining).*

**Who started pretraining for language?** Reusing what a network learned from raw text was not new in 2018. The deep learning textbook of Goodfellow, Bengio, and Courville (2016) credits word-level pretraining in language to Collobert and Weston in 2008 and to work in 2010 and 2011 [@goodfellow2016deep, p. 533]; [the embeddings chapter](#embeddings) tells that story. For pretraining a whole network and then fine-tuning it, Jeremy Howard and Sebastian Ruder, the authors of ULMFiT, wrote that "Dai and Le (2015) first proposed fine-tuning a language model" [@howard2018universal, p. 328], and OpenAI's announcement of GPT said it "builds on" Dai and Le's approach and "extends ULMFiT" [@openai2018language]. Accounts differ on when it took hold. Daniel Jurafsky and James Martin's textbook (as of its 2026-08 draft) calls ELMo "perhaps the moment when it became clear to the community that language models could be used as a general solution for NLP problems" [@jurafsky2026transformers, p. 31]; a 2021 report by Rishi Bommasani and colleagues saw "a sociological inflection point around the introduction of BERT", after which "using BERT has become the norm" [@bommasani2021opportunities, p. 5]. A summary that fits all the sources: the ideas were in place by 2015; what changed in 2018 was that pretraining worked across many tasks at once, first with LSTMs (ELMo, ULMFiT) and then with transformers (GPT, BERT).

**Who had contextual word vectors first?** ELMo, in early 2018, was not the first to produce them: its paper names earlier systems that produced word vectors depending on their sentence [@peters2018deep, p. 2228]. One of them, CoVe, in 2017, took its vectors from the encoder of a translation model [@mccann2017learned, p. 1]. Google's announcement of BERT, later in 2018, claimed a narrower first: BERT, it said, was "the first deeply bidirectional, unsupervised language representation, pre-trained using only a plain text corpus" [@devlin2018open].

## Scale {#credit-scale}

*From [Scale](#scale).*

**"Too dangerous to release."** OpenAI's announcement of GPT-2, on 14 February 2019, gave its reason for holding back the largest model in these words: "Due to our concerns about malicious applications of the technology, we are not releasing the trained model" [@openai2019better]. Zachary Lipton, writing a few days later, found the results "interesting but not surprising", "a step forward, but one along the path that the entire community is already on", and reported that Anima Anandkumar had called the "too dangerous to release" framing clickbait for media attention; he also granted that it was "possible that both sides have an element of truth" [@lipton2019openai]. The words "too dangerous to release" do not appear in OpenAI's announcement (checked for this book in the copy of the page archived on the day it was posted), and Lipton's post does not say where they came from. Anandkumar's own posts were not read for this book; her view is known here only through Lipton's account.

**Who stated "20 tokens per parameter."** The ratio is a reading of the table of compute-optimal model sizes in the Chinchilla paper of Jordan Hoffmann and colleagues, where the recommended tokens come to about 20 for every parameter [@hoffmann2022training, p. 8]. The paper never states it as a rule; a search of its text made for this book finds neither "tokens per parameter" nor "20 tokens". A 2024 reanalysis by Tamay Besiroglu and colleagues calls it a "rule of thumb" [@besiroglu2024chinchilla, p. 5]. Where the phrase was first used was not traced for this book.

## Generating text {#credit-generation}

*From [Generating text](#generation).*

**The KV cache.** No source read for this book names an inventor of the KV cache, and the technique came before the name. Noam Shazeer's 2019 paper on fast decoding already shows it in code: each step of incremental attention takes the keys and values of the earlier positions and returns them with one more position appended [@shazeer2019fast, pp. 3-4]. In 2022, Reiner Pope and colleagues wrote of "The attention key and value tensors of each layer, which we refer to as the KV cache" [@pope2022efficiently, p. 1], and in 2023 Woosuk Kwon and colleagues described these tensors as "commonly referred to as KV cache", citing Pope and colleagues [@kwon2023efficient, p. 1]. The sources establish when the technique was in use and when the name was in use; none of them says who first stored keys and values in this way.

**Speculative decoding.** Yaniv Leviathan, Matan Kalman, and Yossi Matias posted the paper that introduced "speculative decoding" on 30 November 2022 (published at ICML in 2023) [@leviathan2023fast, p. 1]. Charlie Chen and colleagues posted "speculative sampling" on 2 February 2023 [@chen2023accelerating, p. 1]. Chen and colleagues wrote that their work "was undertaken concurrently and independently of the work on speculative decoding from Leviathan et al. (2022)", and that, apart from their focus on serving large models across many chips and "some incremental optimisations", "the core underlying idea is the same" [@chen2023accelerating, p. 2]. Both papers report that the method leaves the large model's output distribution unchanged, Chen and colleagues adding "within hardware numerics" [@leviathan2023fast, p. 1; @chen2023accelerating, p. 1].

## Teaching models to follow instructions {#credit-instructions}

*From [Teaching models to follow instructions](#instructions).*

**Learning from human preferences.** Paul Christiano and colleagues, at OpenAI and DeepMind, showed in 2017 that a reward learned from people's comparisons could train deep reinforcement learning agents on Atari games and simulated robot locomotion [@christiano2017deep, p. 1]. Their paper cites "A long line of work" on reinforcement learning from human ratings or rankings, from 2011 on, and related work back to 2006, and claims novelty in scale: its behaviors were "considerably more complex than any which have been previously learned from human feedback" [@christiano2017deep, pp. 1-3]. When the InstructGPT paper of 2022 used RLHF, it cited Christiano and colleagues (2017) and Nisan Stiennon and colleagues (2020) for it [@ouyang2022training, p. 2]; OpenAI's announcement of the same work called RLHF "a method we helped pioneer in our earlier alignment research" [@openai2022aligning]. The earlier works were not read for this book; they are known here through Christiano and colleagues' account. A summary that fits all the sources: learning from human rankings had a history, and Christiano and colleagues showed in 2017 that it scaled to deep reinforcement learning.

**Instruction tuning, twice in 2021.** The FLAN paper of Jason Wei and colleagues at Google, posted in September 2021, defined instruction tuning [@wei2022finetuned, p. 1]. Victor Sanh and colleagues posted T0 six weeks later and wrote: "Concurrent to our work, Wei et al. (2021) proposes FLAN, which shares largely the same method" [@sanh2022multitask, p. 9].

## Prompting and context {#credit-prompting}

*From [Prompting and context](#prompting).*

**Who first studied the order of examples.** Two groups reported in the same months of 2021 that the order of the examples in a prompt changes the result. Tony Zhao and colleagues, whose preprint appeared in February 2021, found that the prompt format, the choice of examples, "and even the order of the training examples can cause accuracy to vary from near chance to near state-of-the-art" [@zhao2021calibrate, p. 12697]. Yao Lu and colleagues, whose preprint appeared two months later, in April 2021, wrote that "to the best of our knowledge, no work has studied the effect of the sample ordering" [@lu2022fantastically, p. 8086]. The work was near-simultaneous.

**Who found prompt injection.** The examples that Simon Willison wrote up on 12 September 2022, in the post that proposed the name prompt injection, had been posted the day before by Riley Goodside [@willison2022prompt]. Goodside's posts were not read for this book; they are known here only through Willison's post. Two months later, Fábio Perez and Ian Ribeiro published an early paper on the attack [@perez2022ignore, p. 1].

**Constrained decoding before the vendors.** Grammar-constrained generation was available in open-source tools before OpenAI's structured outputs of August 2024 [@openai2024structured]. In July 2023, Brandon Willard and Rémi Louf described guiding generation "with regular expressions and context-free grammars", with "little overhead", in the open-source library Outlines, and their paper cites earlier work on constraining generation [@willard2023efficient, p. 1]; that earlier work was not read for this book. The same month, llama.cpp merged grammar-based sampling [@llamacpp2026grammars].

**A slip in a source.** The 2021 paper of Laria Reynolds and Kyle McDonell, which showed a prompt asking GPT-3 to split a problem into steps, prints an example whose steps look orderly and whose answer, f(f(3)) = 27, is wrong (it is 81); the paper does not remark on the error [@reynolds2021prompt, p. 7].

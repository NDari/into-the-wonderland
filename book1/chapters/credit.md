# Who did what first {#credit}

<!-- D-026: credit and priority questions moved here from the chapters; each side is presented and cited, and no winner is picked. D-005: every claim comes from a verified chapter and its ledger. D-003: no code. -->

The chapters tell the history of the ideas as a story and leave out most questions of priority: who was first, how much a predecessor deserves, and whose account to trust. Those questions are collected here, in the order of the chapters they belong to. Each entry sets out what the sources say and where they differ, and does not choose between them. Who counts as first depends on what is counted: the first statement of an idea, the first system that used it, or the first result that made the field adopt it.

## The artificial neuron {#credit-neuron}

*From [The artificial neuron](#neuron).*

**Did *Perceptrons* cause the decline?** Interest in neural networks fell away in the period of the 1969 book *Perceptrons*, in what the deep learning textbook of Ian Goodfellow, Yoshua Bengio, and Aaron Courville calls "the first major dip in the popularity of neural networks" [@goodfellow2016deep, p. 14]. What part Marvin Minsky and Seymour Papert's book played in that decline is disputed, and there are two accounts of it.

The common account treats the book as the cause. The textbook puts it this way: "Critics who observed these flaws in linear models caused a backlash against biologically inspired learning in general (Minsky and Papert, 1969). This was the first major dip in the popularity of neural networks" [@goodfellow2016deep, p. 14]. Elsewhere it says that critics including Marvin Minsky "pointed out several of the flaws of the linear model family, such as its inability to learn the XOR function, which led to a backlash against the entire neural network approach" [@goodfellow2016deep, p. 221].

The sociological account, from Mikel Olazaran's history based on the literature and on interviews with researchers of the period, argues that this is too simple. "In popular versions of the history of neural networks," he writes, "Minsky and Papert's Perceptrons study is usually taken as the proof that neural network research had so many problems that it was not worth pursuing, and that therefore it had to be abandoned" [@olazaran1993sociological, p. 358]. His central point is about timing. The main arguments of the book, he writes, "were well known by the mid-1960s and had critically affected the development of neural network research by then"; the 1969 book was "the 'final push,' so to speak, for the closure of the controversy" [@olazaran1993sociological, p. 357]. The common view, in his words, "was the result of the closure of the perceptron controversy, and not its cause" [@olazaran1993sociological, p. 358]. In his reading, the single-layer limits were real results, but the verdict on multilayer networks was a judgment, and problems like parity and connectedness became decisive "not because of some intrinsic or necessary property" but because of how the research community of the time interpreted them [@olazaran1993sociological, p. 359]. He also places the book inside a contest for funding, describing symbolic AI researchers who "saw neural network researchers' efforts to obtain funding for building more complex machines as a direct threat to their research interests" [@olazaran1993sociological, p. 346]. One researcher he interviewed, from the Stanford Research Institute group that left neural networks in the mid-1960s, recalled moving to a robot project and having to sell it to the funding agency, "to some of the people who didn't like perceptrons" [@olazaran1993sociological, pp. 372-373].

The two accounts agree on the mathematics and on the decline. They disagree about cause and timing: whether the book produced a backlash against the whole approach, or whether it was the last word in a controversy already largely settled in the mid-1960s, in which a research community, in a contest over direction and money, came to read results about single-layer networks as a verdict on all of them.

## Learning in layers {#credit-backprop}

*From [Learning in layers](#backprop).*

**Who invented backpropagation.** The 1986 Nature letter of David Rumelhart, Geoffrey Hinton, and Ronald Williams presented back-propagation in its abstract as "a new learning procedure" [@rumelhart1986learning, p. 533], and noted that variants of it had been discovered independently by David Parker and by Yann Le Cun [@rumelhart1986learning, p. 535]. The PDP chapter of the same year put it this way: "Parker (1985) has independently derived a similar generalization", and "Le Cun (1985) has also studied a roughly similar learning scheme" [@rumelhart1986internal, p. 322]. Later histories trace the method further back. Gradient methods for multi-stage systems, computed by applying the chain rule backward stage by stage, were "discussed at least since the early 1960s" in optimal control, by Kelley, Bryson, and others [@schmidhuber2014deep, p. 11; @goodfellow2016deep, p. 221]. Andreas Griewank, in a history of automatic differentiation, recounts that Seppo Linnainmaa of Helsinki "says the idea came to him on a sunny afternoon in a Copenhagen park in 1970", and that he used it to estimate the effect of rounding errors in computations, not to train networks; Griewank adds that Gerardi Ostrowski had discovered and used it some five years earlier, in process models in chemical engineering [@griewank2012invented, p. 389]. Paul Werbos, a doctoral student in applied mathematics at Harvard in the early 1970s, considered applying a technique for propagating derivatives backward, which he called "dynamic feedback", to neural-network-like problems [@olazaran1993sociological, p. 392]; in the thesis itself he applied it to an example from political science, not to neural networks [@olazaran1993sociological, p. 394].

The accounts differ in emphasis. Jürgen Schmidhuber's survey, which states that one of its goals is "to assign credit to those who contributed to the present state of the art" [@schmidhuber2014deep, p. 1], says that efficient backpropagation in general networks "apparently was first described" in Linnainmaa's 1970 master's thesis, "albeit without reference to NNs" (neural networks). It describes Werbos's 1974 thesis as "preliminary, NN-specific discussion", dates the first neural-network application of efficient backpropagation to Werbos in 1981 ("to my knowledge", it adds), calls the 1985 work of Parker and LeCun "related work" published "several years later", and says that the 1986 work "significantly contributed to the popularisation" of backpropagation, "experimentally demonstrating the emergence of useful internal representations in hidden layers" [@schmidhuber2014deep, p. 11]. The deep learning textbook of Goodfellow, Bengio, and Courville credits Werbos (1981) with proposing the technique for training networks; in its account the idea "was finally developed in practice after being independently rediscovered in different ways", by LeCun, by Parker, and by Rumelhart and colleagues, and the PDP chapter "contributed greatly to the popularization of back-propagation" [@goodfellow2016deep, p. 221]. Mikel Olazaran's sociological history of the neural network controversy, for its part, says that "it seems that the first attempt to apply something similar to back-propagation to neural network research was Paul Werbos'" [@olazaran1993sociological, p. 392]. None of these accounts disputes the chronology. They differ on whether the credit belongs to the first statement of the mathematics, to its first proposal for neural networks, or to the work that showed it working and made the field adopt it. LeCun, one of the independent discoverers, made the deflationary point in 1988: "back-propagation is little more than an extremely judicious application of the chain rule and gradient descent" [quoted in @olazaran1993sociological, p. 392].

**Two recollections of the 1970s.** In an interview for Olazaran's history, Werbos recalled that his thesis committee doubted the work and that Marvin Minsky, whom he consulted, was discouraging [@olazaran1993sociological, pp. 392-393]. Minsky, interviewed separately, recalled the idea as "certainly trivial" and not practical, adding that "you usually get stuck at a local minimum" [@olazaran1993sociological, pp. 393-394]. These are two participants' recollections, decades later.

## Networks with structure, and the second winter {#credit-structure}

*From [Networks with structure, and the second winter](#structure).*

**Convolutional networks.** Kunihiko Fukushima's neocognitron of 1980 already had shared weights, but it was self-organized by "learning without a teacher" rather than trained by backpropagation on labeled examples [@fukushima1980neocognitron, pp. 193, 194-195]. Later accounts agree on these facts and differ on emphasis. Jürgen Schmidhuber's survey says the neocognitron "introduced convolutional NNs" [@schmidhuber2014deep, p. 10]. Yann LeCun, Yoshua Bengio, and Geoffrey Hinton say convolutional networks "have their roots in the neocognitron, the architecture of which was somewhat similar, but did not have an end-to-end supervised-learning algorithm such as backpropagation" [@lecun2015deep, p. 439]. The 1989 paper by LeCun and colleagues, which trained a convolutional network by backpropagation to read zip codes, credits the idea of weight sharing itself to Rumelhart, Hinton, and Williams in 1986 [@lecun1989backpropagation, p. 544], and backpropagation had already been used from 1988 to train time-delay networks, the one-dimensional kind, for speech [@goodfellow2016deep, pp. 361-362]. A summary that fits all the sources: the neocognitron had the architecture, and what it lacked was a way to train every weight from labeled examples.

**Recurrent networks.** Jeffrey Elman's 1990 paper credits its approach to earlier work by Jordan in 1986 [@elman1990finding, p. 179], and the deep learning textbook of Goodfellow, Bengio, and Courville traces recurrent networks to Rumelhart and colleagues in the same year [@goodfellow2016deep, p. 367]. Elman's simple recurrent network is an early example, not the first.

**The vanishing gradient.** Two analyses of the problem are each credited with identifying it. Sepp Hochreiter analyzed it in his diploma thesis at the Technical University of Munich, dated 15 June 1991 and written in German, with Jürgen Schmidhuber as his advisor [@hochreiter1991untersuchungen]. Yoshua Bengio, Patrice Simard, and Paolo Frasconi published their analysis, of the trade-off between storing information robustly and training by gradient descent, in 1994 [@bengio1994learning, pp. 157-158]. Schmidhuber's survey calls the thesis "a milestone of explicit DL research" (DL meaning deep learning) and says it "formally identified a major reason" why deep networks were hard to train [@schmidhuber2014deep, p. 16]; the LSTM paper, by Hochreiter and Schmidhuber, calls Bengio and colleagues' work "a very similar, more recent analysis" [@hochreiter1997long, p. 1740]. Goodfellow, Bengio, and Courville describe the problem as "independently discovered by separate researchers (Hochreiter, 1991; Bengio et al., 1993, 1994)" [@goodfellow2016deep, p. 398]. The two groups later wrote a joint account of the problem [@hochreiter2001gradient].

## The turn: data, compute, and depth {#credit-deep-turn}

*From [The turn: data, compute, and depth](#deep-turn).*

**Where deep learning began.** The deep learning textbook of Goodfellow, Bengio, and Courville says the current wave "began with a breakthrough in 2006", Geoffrey Hinton's layer-wise training, quickly extended by other groups affiliated with the Canadian Institute for Advanced Research [@goodfellow2016deep, p. 18], and Yann LeCun, Yoshua Bengio, and Geoffrey Hinton tell it the same way [@lecun2015deep, p. 439]. Both accounts come from members of that group. Jürgen Schmidhuber's survey stresses a longer line: deep learning research results "have been published at least since 1991", and only "the expression Deep Learning was actually coined around 2006" [@schmidhuber2014deep, p. 21].

**The 2012 ImageNet result.** Schmidhuber's history places SuperVision, the 2012 winner, in a line of GPU-trained convolutional networks, among them networks from his own laboratory that won other image-recognition contests in 2011 [@schmidhuber2014deep, pp. 23-24]. The SuperVision paper itself cites that work [@krizhevsky2012imagenet, pp. 1, 3].

## The training loop {#credit-toolkit}

*From [The training loop](#toolkit).*

**Momentum.** Later authors credit the momentum method to the numerical optimization work of Boris Polyak in 1964 [@sutskever2013importance, p. 1140; @goodfellow2016deep, p. 292]. The 1986 backpropagation papers used it without citing Polyak [@rumelhart1986learning, pp. 535-536; @rumelhart1986internal, p. 330], and nothing read for this book says they took it from him. Polyak's paper was not read for this book; it is cited here through those later accounts.

**RMSProp.** RMSProp was described by Geoffrey Hinton in lecture slides for a course on neural networks. The slide that gives its details attaches the note "Tijmen Tieleman, unpublished" to its central step, dividing the gradient by the square root of a running average of its square [@hinton2012lecture6, slide 29]. Later papers cite the method as Tieleman and Hinton, 2012 [@kingma2015adam, p. 1].

## Reading and writing sequences {#credit-seq2seq}

*From [Reading and writing sequences](#seq2seq).*

**The encoder-decoder.** Four steps can each be called the first. Models that read a sentence into a representation and wrote a translation from it were tried in 1997, but never on enough data to get past toy examples [@koehn2017neural, p. 5]. In 2013, Nal Kalchbrenner and Phil Blunsom built a translation model "purely based on continuous representations for words, phrases and sentences" [@kalchbrenner2013recurrent, p. 1700]; Ilya Sutskever, Oriol Vinyals, and Quoc Le called them "the first to map the input sentence into a vector and then back to a sentence" [@sutskever2014sequence, p. 8]. In June 2014, Kyunghyun Cho and colleagues proposed the "RNN Encoder-Decoder", used as one more score inside a phrase-based system [@cho2014learning, pp. 1724, 1727]. In September 2014, Sutskever and colleagues' network translated on its own, and in their words (SMT is statistical machine translation, MT machine translation) "it is the first time that a pure neural translation system outperforms a phrase-based SMT baseline on a large MT task by a sizeable margin" [@sutskever2014sequence, pp. 5-6].

The deep learning textbook of Goodfellow, Bengio, and Courville gives the architecture to the two 2014 groups: it "was first proposed by Cho et al. (2014a) and shortly after by Sutskever et al. (2014), who independently developed that architecture and were the first to obtain state-of-the-art translation using this approach" [@goodfellow2016deep, pp. 390, 392]. "State-of-the-art" is generous: Sutskever and colleagues' networks beat a phrase-based baseline, 34.81 BLEU against 33.30, but not the best system of the day, which scored 37.0 [@sutskever2014sequence, pp. 5-6]. A summary that fits all the sources: the idea was old; Kalchbrenner and Blunsom, then Cho and colleagues, built neural versions inside existing systems; and Sutskever and colleagues, by their own account, were the first to make a network alone beat a phrase-based baseline on a large task by a sizeable margin.

**Attention.** Dzmitry Bahdanau, Kyunghyun Cho, and Yoshua Bengio posted their attention paper in September 2014, nine days before Sutskever and colleagues posted theirs [@bahdanau2015neural, p. 1]. The mechanism had a forerunner in Alex Graves's 2013 handwriting network, whose "'soft window'" over the text "learns to decide which character to write next" [@graves2013generating, pp. 24, 26]. Sutskever and colleagues described Graves's window as "a novel differentiable attention mechanism", of which Bahdanau and colleagues applied "an elegant variant" to translation [@sutskever2014sequence, p. 2]. Bahdanau and colleagues acknowledged Graves's work and named the difference: his window could only move forward through the text, which in translation "is a severe limitation, as (long-distance) reordering is often needed" [@bahdanau2015neural, p. 8].

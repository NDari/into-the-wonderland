[The chapter on pretraining](#pretraining) ended with a division of labor: pretraining is expensive and done once; fine-tuning is cheap and done for each task. In 2018 that was true. A BERT model had a few hundred million parameters, and a copy of it per task was a reasonable thing to keep. By 2020 GPT-3, the largest model in [the chapter on scale](#scale), had 175 billion, and "cheap" no longer described fine-tuning it.

This chapter is about how to change what a pretrained model does without paying for a whole new model each time. It covers what fine-tuning all the weights costs and what it can break; the small add-ons that replaced it for many purposes, above all LoRA, which rests on the low-rank idea behind PCA; distillation, which trains a smaller model to imitate a larger one; and, finally, when changing the weights is the right tool at all. The data used for the most important kind of adaptation, turning a text-continuing model into an assistant, is the subject of [the chapter on following instructions](#instructions). This chapter is about the machinery: which weights change, and how much memory that takes.

## What full fine-tuning costs

**Full fine-tuning** means continuing to train every parameter of a pretrained model on new data. It is what the models of [the pretraining chapter](#pretraining) did. Its first cost is storage: the fine-tuned model has as many parameters as the one it started from [@hu2021lora, p. 1]. One task needs one full copy; a hundred tasks need a hundred. For GPT-3, the LoRA paper of 2021 judged the cost of deploying separate fine-tuned copies, of 175 billion parameters each, to be prohibitive [@hu2021lora, p. 1].

The second cost is memory during training. [The chapter on training](#training) gave the bill for training with Adam in mixed precision: about 16 bytes per parameter, for the weights, their gradients, a 32-bit master copy, and Adam's two running averages, before counting the intermediate results of the forward pass. Fine-tuning pays the same bill per parameter as pretraining; it just runs for far fewer steps. <!-- D-009: code/checks/adapting.py, Example 4 --> For an 8-billion-parameter model such as Llama 3 8B, that is about 128.5 GB. <!-- D-009: code/checks/adapting.py, Example 5 --> Published figures for real setups differ, because they count different things: the QLoRA paper of 2023 put ordinary 16-bit fine-tuning of the 65-billion-parameter LLaMA model at more than 780 GB of GPU memory [@dettmers2023qlora, p. 1], about 12 bytes per parameter, and the LoRA paper reported 1.2 TB for GPT-3 [@hu2021lora, p. 5].

There were cheaper options even in 2019, and they lost. The T5 team, whose systematic comparisons appear in [the pretraining chapter](#pretraining), tried two methods that update only part of the model. Updating every parameter cost the most, they concluded, but it also did better than the methods built to update fewer [@raffel2020exploring, p. 42]. On English-to-French translation, a model with small add-on layers scored 17.88 BLEU against 39.82 for full fine-tuning [@raffel2020exploring, p. 30]. The rest of this chapter is largely about how that verdict was reversed for the models that came after.

## What fine-tuning can break

The third cost is subtler. Training a network on new data can erase what it knew. The effect was brought to wide attention in 1989 and 1990, in studies of the backpropagation networks of the time [@french1999catastrophic, pp. 128-129]. In the 1989 experiment, a network learned the 17 "ones" addition facts (1 + 1 through 9 + 1, and 1 + 2 through 1 + 9) until it had them all right. Then it was trained on the 17 "twos" facts. After one to five rounds of training on the twos, it got only 20% of the ones right, down from 100%; five rounds later, 1%; and by the fifteenth round it could answer none of them [@french1999catastrophic, pp. 128-129]. People learning a second list forget the first gradually; this network forgot it all at once.

The 1989 study called it catastrophic interference; **catastrophic forgetting** is the name that stuck, and the one [the pretraining chapter](#pretraining) quoted from the ULMFiT paper [@french1999catastrophic, p. 128]. According to a 1999 review, the researchers who described it suggested that the cause was the very thing that makes these networks generalize: one set of weights, shared by everything the network learns [@french1999catastrophic, p. 128]. Every fact is stored across all the weights, so changing the weights for new facts disturbs the old ones. Who described the effect and who named it are set out in [the credit appendix](#credit-adapting).

The obvious remedy is to train on the old and new data together. When the data of several tasks is mixed during training, a 2017 paper noted, nothing is forgotten, because the weights can be fitted to all the tasks at once; but storing and replaying all the old data becomes impractical as tasks accumulate [@kirkpatrick2017overcoming, p. 1]. That paper proposed instead to anchor the weights that mattered for the old task. A penalty pulls each weight back toward its old value, like a spring tied to the earlier solution, and the spring is stiffer for the weights that matter most [@kirkpatrick2017overcoming, p. 2]. This is ridge regression, with the penalty centered on the old weights instead of on zero and a different strength for each weight. The spring is the "elastic" in the method's name, elastic weight consolidation [@kirkpatrick2017overcoming, p. 2]. It was demonstrated on handwritten digits and Atari games, not on language models.

Large language models forget too. A 2023 study, still a preprint as of its January 2025 revision, fine-tuned models of 1 to 7 billion parameters on a sequence of instruction tasks and then re-measured their general knowledge. Catastrophic forgetting, it found, was generally observed across that range [@luo2023empirical, p. 1]. <!-- D-009: code/checks/adapting.py, Example 8 --> One model's score on a set of social-science questions fell from 36.18% to 26.06% [@luo2023empirical, p. 7]. Within that range, larger models forgot more, which the authors suggest may be because they had more to lose [@luo2023empirical, p. 1]: one study's finding, not a law.

## Small add-ons to a frozen model

One alternative leaves the pretrained model untouched. In 2019, a team proposed **adapters**: small extra layers inserted into each block of a pretrained transformer, trained while the original weights stay frozen [@houlsby2019parameter, p. 2790]. An adapter is a bottleneck. It squeezes the vector of width d at that point down to a much smaller width m, applies a nonlinearity, and expands it back to d, like a tiny autoencoder inside the network; a skip connection adds the result to what came in [@houlsby2019parameter, p. 2792]. The adapters start out very close to the identity, passing their input through nearly unchanged, so that training begins from the original network as it was [@houlsby2019parameter, p. 2791].

The setting they had in mind was a service that receives new tasks one after another: one frozen model, many small add-ons, one per task [@houlsby2019parameter, p. 2790]. On the GLUE benchmark, with BERT-large, adapters reached an average score of 80.0 against 80.4 for full fine-tuning, while adding 3.6% of the model's parameters per task [@houlsby2019parameter, pp. 2793-2794]. <!-- D-009: code/checks/adapting.py, Example 6 --> The paper calls that "within 0.4%"; it is 0.4 points, about half a percent. For all nine GLUE tasks, full fine-tuning needed nine copies of BERT; adapters needed 1.3 times one copy [@houlsby2019parameter, p. 2793]. And because the shared weights never change, the model remembers its earlier tasks perfectly [@houlsby2019parameter, p. 2791]: catastrophic forgetting cannot happen to weights that are not trained.

Methods that adapt a small part of a model while leaving the rest frozen became known as **parameter-efficient fine-tuning**. Two more appeared in 2021, both of which add trainable vectors as extra positions at the front of the sequence, instead of new layers. **Prefix tuning** prepends a short sequence of learned vectors at every layer, which later tokens attend to as if they were "virtual tokens", and trains only 0.1% of the parameters [@li2021prefix, pp. 4582, 4585]. **Prompt tuning** simplified this to a few learned vectors at the input only, a **soft prompt**: a handful of extra embedding vectors, found by backpropagation, that correspond to no real words [@lester2021power, p. 3045]. For an 11-billion-parameter T5 model, a soft prompt of 5 tokens is 20,480 numbers per task, more than 100,000 times fewer than a full copy of the model [@lester2021power, p. 3046]. The striking finding concerned size: prompt tuning lagged behind on small models, but the gap narrowed as the models grew, and at 11 billion parameters it matched full fine-tuning on the paper's benchmark [@lester2021power, pp. 3045, 3048]. Written prompts, the other way to steer a model with no training at all, are the subject of [the chapter on prompting](#prompting).

## How few directions does a task need?

Why should such small changes be enough? A line of work on what is called intrinsic dimension suggested an answer. In 2018, a study trained networks in a random, much smaller subspace of their parameters: instead of adjusting all the weights freely, they let only a few hundred numbers vary, mapped into the full set of weights by a fixed random projection [@li2018measuring, p. 1]. A network with 199,210 parameters for handwritten digits reached 90% of its normal performance with only about 750 numbers free [@li2018measuring, p. 4]. The smallest such count is the problem's **intrinsic dimension**.

In 2020, the same measurement was applied to fine-tuning pretrained language models. With RoBERTa, a BERT variant, training just 200 numbers, mapped into the full set of weights by a random projection, was enough to reach 90% of what full fine-tuning reached on a paraphrase task; larger and longer-pretrained models tended to have lower intrinsic dimension [@aghajanyan2021intrinsic, p. 7319]. "90%" here is the definition of the threshold, not a claim of near-equality: these are the fewest numbers that reach 90% of what full fine-tuning reaches [@aghajanyan2021intrinsic, p. 7321]. The suggestion was that pretraining does most of the work, and adapting the result to a task moves it only a little, in only a few directions.

## LoRA: a low-rank correction

The LoRA paper of 2021 took this as its starting point. Its authors hypothesized "that the change in weights during model adaptation also has a low "intrinsic rank"" [@hu2021lora, p. 2], and built a method on it: **LoRA**, low-rank adaptation.

Take one of the weight matrices in a transformer block, such as the matrix that makes queries in [the attention chapter](#attention). Call the pretrained matrix $W_0$; it has $d$ rows and $k$ columns. Fine-tuning would change it to $W_0 + \Delta W$, where $\Delta W$ is a change of the same size. LoRA restricts that change to a special form [@hu2021lora, p. 4]:

$$W = W_0 + BA$$

Here $W_0$ is frozen: it is never changed. $B$ is a tall, thin matrix with $d$ rows and $r$ columns, and $A$ is a short, wide matrix with $r$ rows and $k$ columns. The number $r$, the **rank**, is chosen small, far smaller than $d$ or $k$. Only $B$ and $A$ are trained. For an input vector $x$, the layer's output becomes $h = W_0 x + BAx$: the old output plus a correction. (In practice the correction is also multiplied by a fixed scale factor, which LoRA settings call alpha [@hu2021lora, p. 4].)

The product $BA$ is a $d$ by $k$ matrix, the same shape as $W_0$, but it is built from far fewer numbers, and its rank is at most $r$: every one of its columns is a combination of the $r$ columns of $B$. That is the bet: the change a task needs lies in a few directions. <!-- D-009: code/checks/adapting.py, Example 1 --> For a 4,096 by 4,096 matrix, the size of the query matrix in Llama 3 8B [@grattafiori2024llama, p. 7], $W_0$ has 16,777,216 weights. A rank-8 correction has 8 x (4,096 + 4,096) = 65,536 trainable numbers, 0.39% of the matrix, one 256th.

![LoRA on one weight matrix. The pretrained matrix $W_0$ (4,096 by 4,096, as in Llama 3 8B's query projection) is frozen. The trained correction is the product of $B$ (4,096 by 8) and $A$ (8 by 4,096): 65,536 numbers, 0.39% of $W_0$. $B$ starts at zero, so at the start of training the model is exactly the pretrained one. The widths of $B$ and $A$ are drawn far wider than to scale.](../../figures/adapting-lora.svg){#fig:adapting-lora}

Two details make it work in practice. First, $A$ starts with small random values and $B$ starts at zero, so the correction $\Delta W = BA$ is zero when training begins [@hu2021lora, p. 4]: training starts from the pretrained model exactly, the same idea as the adapters' near-do-nothing start. Second, once training is done, the correction can be folded in: for deployment, $W = W_0 + BA$ is computed once and stored, and the model is run as usual [@hu2021lora, p. 4]. A merged LoRA model is exactly the size of the original and runs at exactly the same speed: unlike adapters, which add layers that every token must pass through, LoRA adds no delay when the model runs [@hu2021lora, p. 5]. To switch tasks, subtract one correction and add another. What LoRA saves is training memory and per-task storage, not the cost of using the model.

<!-- D-009: code/checks/adapting.py, Example 3 --> The numbers for GPT-3 were the headline. Applying LoRA only to the query and value matrices of its 96 layers, at rank 4, gives 18.9 million trainable numbers; a task's checkpoint shrank from 350 GB to 35 MB, and storing 100 adapted models would take about 354 GB instead of 35 TB [@hu2021lora, p. 5]. Training memory fell from 1.2 TB to 350 GB, the size of the frozen 16-bit weights themselves, because no gradients or optimizer state are kept for frozen weights [@hu2021lora, p. 5]. The abstract summarizes this as 10,000 times fewer trainable parameters and 3 times less GPU memory, with quality "on-par or better than fine-tuning" on the benchmarks they tried [@hu2021lora, p. 1]. On one of them, translating questions into database queries, full fine-tuning of GPT-3 scored 73.8% and LoRA with 4.7 million parameters 73.4% [@hu2021lora, p. 8]. And the rank could be strikingly low: when queries and values were both adapted, rank 1 did about as well as rank 64 on two GPT-3 tasks [@hu2021lora, p. 10].

## The bridge: low rank is what PCA does

The idea of approximating a matrix by a low-rank one is old and has gone under other names. In 1936, Carl Eckart and Gale Young asked how to approximate a matrix of test scores, N individuals by n tests, by another of lower rank; that such an approximation is adequate was a basic assumption of factor theory [@eckart1936approximation, p. 211]. Their answer: write the matrix in what is now called its singular value decomposition, keep the r largest singular values, set the rest to zero, and multiply back. No rank-r matrix is closer, and the error is exactly the size of what was dropped: the square root of the sum of the squares of the discarded singular values [@eckart1936approximation, p. 216]. Principal component analysis is the same result applied to data: for every rank, the principal components give the best linear approximation of that rank to the data [@hastie2009elements, p. 534]. [The embeddings chapter](#embeddings) used the same tool, in latent semantic analysis, to get word vectors from a word-by-document table.

<!-- D-009: code/checks/adapting.py, Example 2 --> A small made-up example shows the pattern. Take an 8 by 6 matrix whose singular values are 10, 5, 1, 0.5, 0.2, and 0.1. Keeping only the largest gives a rank-1 approximation that holds 79% of the matrix's squared size (the sum of its squared entries); keeping two holds 99%, with an error of 1.14, which is exactly the square root of $1^2 + 0.5^2 + 0.2^2 + 0.1^2$. The rank-2 approximation can be stored as an 8 by 2 matrix times a 2 by 6 one: 28 numbers instead of 48. That is LoRA's shape, $B$ times $A$. A rank-2 approximation built on random directions instead, for comparison, has an error of 10.71, nearly the size of the whole matrix (11.24): low rank helps only if the directions are the right ones.

The bridge has a limit worth stating. Truncated SVD and PCA find the best low-rank approximation of a matrix that is known. LoRA never computes an SVD and has no matrix to approximate: it learns $B$ and $A$ by gradient descent, from zero, and bets that the change the task needs is close to some rank-$r$ matrix. The bet can lose.

## Does LoRA match full fine-tuning?

The 2021 paper said it did, on the benchmarks of its time, and the claim is still repeated. Later work found conditions. A 2024 study compared LoRA with full fine-tuning on two harder targets, programming and mathematics, with a 7-billion-parameter Llama 2 model. In the standard low-rank settings, it found, LoRA did substantially worse than full fine-tuning [@biderman2024lora, p. 1]. When training on billions of tokens of new text, the gap remained even at high rank; when training on about 100,000 instruction-and-response examples, high ranks could match full fine-tuning [@biderman2024lora, pp. 1-2]. The study also checked the bet directly: the change full fine-tuning makes to the weights had a rank 10 to 100 times that of typical LoRA settings [@biderman2024lora, p. 1]. LoRA's own authors had anticipated this. They did not expect a small rank to work for every task: if the new task were in a language other than the one used in pretraining, they wrote, full retraining of the model could certainly do better than LoRA at a small rank [@hu2021lora, p. 10].

The 2024 study's title gives the other half: "LoRA Learns Less and Forgets Less". On tasks outside the target domain, LoRA kept more of the base model's performance than full fine-tuning did, and limited forgetting better than the usual regularization methods [@biderman2024lora, p. 1]. A small change changes less, for better and for worse. A fair summary, as of 2024: LoRA does about as well as full fine-tuning when the new task is close to what the model already does, falls short when the new domain is far or the new data large, and in exchange damages less of what the model knew.

## QLoRA: a 4-bit frozen model

LoRA removed the gradients and optimizer state of the frozen weights, but the frozen weights themselves still had to sit in memory in 16 bits. In 2023, Tim Dettmers and colleagues stored them in 4. Their **QLoRA** passes gradients back through a frozen model, quantized to 4 bits (stored with fewer bits, as explained next), into LoRA matrices, and that is enough to fine-tune a 65-billion-parameter model on a single 48 GB GPU [@dettmers2023qlora, p. 1].

**Quantization** means storing numbers with fewer bits by rounding them to a small set of allowed values; [the chapter on local inference](#local) treats it in depth. QLoRA's 4-bit format, called NormalFloat or NF4, places its 16 allowed values where they suit numbers that follow a bell curve, because the weights of a pretrained network usually follow a normal distribution centered on zero [@dettmers2023qlora, p. 4]. The weights are rounded in blocks of 64, each block with its own scale factor, and the scale factors are themselves compressed, which brings the overhead to about 0.127 bits per weight [@dettmers2023qlora, p. 5]. The weights are stored in 4 bits but computed with in 16: each time a weight matrix is needed, it is converted back to 16-bit BFloat16 numbers, and the multiplication is done in 16 bits [@dettmers2023qlora, p. 4]. Only the LoRA matrices are trained, in 16-bit numbers [@dettmers2023qlora, p. 5]. A third trick moves optimizer state to ordinary computer memory when the GPU runs short [@dettmers2023qlora, p. 5].

<!-- D-009: code/checks/adapting.py, Example 4 --> For an 8-billion-parameter model, the arithmetic of this chapter gives the picture in the figure. Full fine-tuning at 16 bytes per parameter needs about 128.5 GB. LoRA at rank 16 on every weight matrix of the blocks trains 41.9 million numbers, about half a percent of the model; with the frozen weights in 16 bits, the total is about 16.7 GB. QLoRA, with the frozen weights in 4.127 bits, needs about 4.8 GB, if every weight is stored in 4 bits. These counts leave out the intermediate results of the forward pass, which grow with the length and number of training sequences. The QLoRA paper measured a real case for a 7-billion-parameter model: the 4-bit model took 5,048 MB, the LoRA parameters 26 MB, and the gradients flowing back through the network for one sequence (the paper's "input gradients") 567 MB, or 18 MB with gradient checkpointing, a standard trick that recomputes intermediate results instead of storing them [@dettmers2023qlora, p. 26]. Once the frozen model is small, the trained part is a rounding error.

Did the rounding cost quality? On the tests the authors could afford, 4-bit QLoRA matched 16-bit LoRA, and it matched full 16-bit fine-tuning at the smaller sizes they compared [@dettmers2023qlora, p. 6]. They were careful about what they had not shown: because of the immense resources it would take, they had not established that QLoRA matches 16-bit full fine-tuning at 33 and 65 billion parameters [@dettmers2023qlora, p. 9]. They also found that LoRA had to be applied to every weight matrix in the blocks, not just the queries and values as in the original paper, to match full fine-tuning [@dettmers2023qlora, p. 18]. The paper's chatbot, Guanaco, was reported to reach "99.3% of the performance level of ChatGPT", a score on 80 prompts judged by GPT-4, in an abstract that also warns that current chatbot benchmarks cannot be trusted to measure chatbots accurately [@dettmers2023qlora, pp. 1, 21]. The memory result is the lasting one: by 2023, adapting a large **open-weight** model (one whose trained weights can be downloaded; see [the chapter on local inference](#local)) no longer needed a data center.

## Distillation: a new, smaller model

The methods so far change a model while keeping its size. **Knowledge distillation** produces a different, usually smaller model, called the **student**, trained to imitate a larger one, the **teacher**.

The idea dates from the mid-2000s. In 2006, a paper on compressing models started from a practical problem: the best supervised models were often ensembles of hundreds or thousands of classifiers, such as boosted or bagged decision trees and random forests, too large and slow for uses with big test sets, little storage, or little computing power [@bucilua2006model]. Its fix was to label a large set of unlabeled examples with the ensemble's predictions and train a small neural network on those labels. The networks were smaller and faster than the ensembles they imitated by a factor of a thousand, and performed nearly as well [@bucilua2006model].

In 2015, Geoffrey Hinton and colleagues took the approach further, with a different technique that they called "distillation" [@hinton2015distilling, p. 1]. How the two papers relate is one of the questions in [the credit appendix](#credit-adapting). The insight of the 2015 paper was that the teacher's full probability distribution carries more information than its top answer. "An image of a BMW, for example, may only have a very small chance of being mistaken for a garbage truck, but that mistake is still many times more probable than mistaking it for a carrot" [@hinton2015distilling, p. 2]. Training the student on the teacher's probabilities, its **soft targets**, passes on that knowledge of what resembles what. To make the small probabilities visible, they raised the temperature of the teacher's softmax, the same temperature [the chapter on generation](#generation) introduced, and trained the student at the same raised temperature [@hinton2015distilling, p. 2]. <!-- D-009: code/checks/adapting.py, Example 7 --> With made-up teacher scores of 10 for "car", 4 for "truck", and -2 for "carrot", at temperature 1 the probabilities are 0.9975, 0.0025, and 0.000006: almost all on the right answer, with the difference between "truck" and "carrot" nearly invisible. At temperature 4 they are 0.79, 0.18, and 0.04, and the student can see that a truck is a far more reasonable mistake than a carrot.

On handwritten digits, a small network trained alone made 146 test errors; trained on a large network's soft targets at temperature 20, it made 74, close to the large network's 67 [@hinton2015distilling, p. 4]. In speech recognition they distilled an ensemble of ten models into one model of the same size as each member, which kept more than 80% of the ensemble's gain [@hinton2015distilling, pp. 4-5]. In 2019, DistilBERT applied the method during pretraining, and produced a BERT with 40% fewer parameters (66 million against 110) that kept about 97% of its benchmark score and ran about 1.6 times as fast [@sanh2019distilbert, pp. 1, 3].

In LLM writing as of 2025, "distillation" has two meanings, and both are in common use. The first is Hinton's: train the student to match the teacher's whole next-token distribution, at every position. Google's Gemma 2 models of 2024 were trained this way: their small models were trained by knowledge distillation instead of plain next-token prediction, with the one-hot target at each token replaced by a large model's probabilities for every possible next token [@gemmateam2024gemma2, p. 1]. The second is looser: fine-tune a small model on text that a large model wrote. DeepSeek's R1 report of 2025 called fine-tuning smaller open-weight models on about 800,000 examples, most of them written by an earlier stage of its large reasoning model and the rest drawn from an earlier model's data, "this straightforward distillation method" [@deepseekai2025r1, pp. 1, 10-11]. Both make a small model behave more like a large one; only the first uses the teacher's probabilities.

## When to change the weights at all

Fine-tuning changes how a model behaves. Whether it is a good way to give a model new facts is a different question, and two 2024 studies suggest it is not. The first tested three 7-billion-parameter models on multiple-choice questions about events of 2023, after the models' training data ended [@ovadia2024fine, pp. 237, 242]. It compared continued training on documents about those events with **retrieval**: finding relevant passages at question time and putting them in the model's input, the subject of [the chapter on knowledge and tools](#tools). <!-- D-009: code/checks/adapting.py, Example 8 --> For one model, the score was 0.481 without help, 0.504 after training on the documents, 0.588 after training on ten paraphrases of each, and 0.875 with retrieval [@ovadia2024fine, p. 241]. Its authors concluded that models have trouble learning new facts from this kind of unsupervised fine-tuning, and that retrieval consistently did better [@ovadia2024fine, p. 237].

The second, working with one Google model on question answering, found something worse. Fine-tuning examples that contained facts the model did not already know were learned slowly, and as the model finally learned them, its tendency to hallucinate rose linearly [@gekhman2024does, p. 7765]. The hallucinations appeared late in training, as a form of overfitting, so stopping early reduced them [@gekhman2024does, p. 7766]: ordinary early stopping, with a new reason. Their results support a view worth carrying forward: a model learns most of its facts in pretraining, and fine-tuning teaches it to make more efficient use of them [@gekhman2024does, p. 7765]. They note their experiments used a single model [@gekhman2024does, p. 7773].

The vendors' own guidance points the same way. OpenAI's documentation, as of 2026-11, says that prompting alone may be enough, and lists what fine-tuning is good for: more examples than fit in a prompt, shorter and cheaper prompts, training on data one does not want to send with every request, and making a smaller, cheaper, faster model do well at one particular task [@openai2026modeloptimization]. It advises setting up evals first, that is, tests of the model on one's own task, the subject of [the chapter on measuring models](#evaluation), and investing in fine-tuning only after that [@openai2026sft]. As of 2026-11, the two vendors of **closed models** (whose weights are not released) checked for this book offer little self-serve fine-tuning. OpenAI is "winding down the fine-tuning platform": since May 2026 it is closed to new users, and existing customers can start new training jobs only until 6 January 2027 [@openai2026sft; @openai2026deprecations]. Anthropic states that "The Claude API does not currently offer fine-tuning" [@anthropic2026glossary]. Anyone who wants to fine-tune a model today will therefore most likely start from an open-weight model, the subject of [the chapter on local inference](#local).

A rough rule follows from this chapter's evidence. To change what a model knows, give it the knowledge in its input: retrieval, or the prompt itself. To change how it behaves, its format, tone, or skill at a narrow task, consider fine-tuning, usually with LoRA or QLoRA, after measuring whether prompting already does the job. To make a model smaller and cheaper to run, distill it. These are guidelines drawn from studies of a few models up to 2024 and vendor documentation of 2026, not laws.

## What to carry forward

- Full fine-tuning trains every weight. It needs a full copy of the model per task and, with Adam, about 16 bytes of memory per parameter during training.
- Training a network on new data can erase what it knew: catastrophic forgetting, described in 1989 and still observed in language models. Mixing old data back in, or keeping weights frozen, limits it.
- Parameter-efficient methods freeze the pretrained model and train a small add-on: adapters (2019, small bottleneck layers), prefix and prompt tuning (2021, learned vectors at the front of the sequence), and LoRA.
- LoRA (2021) adds a low-rank correction $BA$ to a frozen weight matrix $W_0$. $B$ starts at zero; after training, $BA$ can be merged into $W_0$, so the model is the same size and speed as before.
- Low-rank approximation is the idea behind truncated SVD and PCA (Eckart and Young, 1936). LoRA uses the shape, but learns $B$ and $A$ by gradient descent instead of computing them.
- LoRA matches full fine-tuning on tasks close to what the model already does, and falls short on distant domains or large new data (as of 2024); it also forgets less.
- QLoRA (2023) stores the frozen model in 4 bits and trains LoRA matrices in 16, bringing the adaptation of a 65-billion-parameter model onto one 48 GB GPU.
- Distillation trains a small student to imitate a large teacher. Its modern line goes back to 2006, as a way to deploy ensembles; Hinton and colleagues (2015) trained on the teacher's soft probabilities at a raised temperature. In LLM writing it also means fine-tuning on text a large model wrote.
- Fine-tuning is a poor way to add facts (two 2024 studies) and a reasonable way to change behavior. As of 2026-11, the two closed-model vendors checked here offer little or no self-serve fine-tuning to new customers, which leaves open-weight models as the usual starting point.

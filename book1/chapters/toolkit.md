The 2012 image network of [the previous chapter](#deep-turn) was trained, in its authors' words, "using stochastic gradient descent with a batch size of 128 examples, momentum of 0.9, and weight decay of 0.0005", with a learning rate "initialized at 0.01 and reduced three times prior to termination" [@krizhevsky2012imagenet, pp. 6-7]. Most of the words in that sentence come from statistics and optimization, but "stochastic", "momentum", and "weight decay" have specific senses here, and what the whole sentence describes takes some unpacking.

This chapter decodes that kind of sentence. It covers the training loop: how examples are fed to the network, how each step is taken, how the step size changes over time, how a person can tell from a plot whether training is going well, and how the software computes all the gradients. [The next chapter](#depth) covers the other half of a recipe: the parts of the network itself that make very deep models trainable. By the end of the two chapters, the training recipe of a large language model can be read line by line.

Most of what follows is not new mathematics. It is the gradient descent of [the chapter on learning in layers](#backprop), adapted for very large data and very large models, and much of it rests on standard ideas from statistics.

## Tensors and shapes

The data and weights inside a network are arrays of numbers. In the field's vocabulary, "an array of numbers arranged on a regular grid with a variable number of axes is known as a **tensor**" [@goodfellow2016deep, p. 31]. The word has a different and more demanding meaning in physics; here it simply means an array with any number of axes. A list of numbers is a tensor with one axis, a table has two, and so on. The sizes along each axis are the tensor's **shape**.

Shapes are the bookkeeping of a network, and they make one point clear. <!-- D-009: code/checks/toolkit.py, Example 1 --> Take 4 examples with 3 features each: a 4-by-3 tensor. Multiply it by a layer's 3-by-2 weight matrix and the result is 4 by 2: two outputs for each of the four examples, computed from 6 weights. Now take a language-model-sized case: 32 text sequences, each 128 tokens long, each token a vector of 512 numbers. That is a tensor of shape (32, 128, 512), holding 2,097,152 numbers. Pass it through a weight matrix of 512 by 2,048, which has 1,048,576 weights, and out comes a tensor of shape (32, 128, 2,048), with 8,388,608 numbers. The number of weights depends only on the layer, not on how many examples go through it. The size of the activations, the intermediate values the network computes, grows with the number of examples processed at once. That distinction decides what fits in a GPU's memory.

## Minibatches and stochastic gradient descent

The 1986 Nature letter described two ways to use the gradient. "One way ... is to change the weights after every input-output case." "An alternative scheme, which we used in the research reported here, is to accumulate" the gradient "over all the input-output cases before changing the weights" [@rumelhart1986learning, p. 535]. The first is noisy: one example gives a poor estimate of the true gradient. The second is exact but slow: with a million training examples, each step requires a pass over all of them.

The modern answer is in between. Each step uses a small random sample of the training examples, called a **minibatch**, and estimates the gradient from it. The number of examples in the sample is the **batch size**. This is **stochastic gradient descent**, usually abbreviated SGD. (The vocabulary is slippery. Textbooks once reserved "stochastic" for one example at a time and called the in-between case "minibatch stochastic", but "it is now common to call them simply stochastic methods" [@goodfellow2016deep, p. 276]. And "batch size" means the size of a minibatch, while "batch gradient descent" means using the whole training set [@goodfellow2016deep, p. 275].) One update of the weights is a **step** or **iteration**; one pass through the whole training set is still an epoch, as in [the chapter on the artificial neuron](#neuron).

A minibatch gradient is an estimate from a sample, and basic statistics says how good such an estimate is. Goodfellow, Bengio, and Courville make the connection directly: the standard error of a mean estimated from $n$ samples is $\sigma/\sqrt{n}$, where $\sigma$ is the standard deviation of a single sample (here, of one example's gradient; not the logistic function of [the chapter on the artificial neuron](#neuron)), so a gradient estimated from 10,000 examples costs 100 times as much as one from 100 examples "but reduces the standard error of the mean only by a factor of 10" [@goodfellow2016deep, p. 275].

<!-- D-009: code/checks/toolkit.py, Example 2 -->

Here it is in numbers. Take 1,000 points scattered around the line $y = 2x$ and fit the line $y = w x$ by mean squared error, as in [the chapter on learning in layers](#backprop). At $w = 0$ the true gradient, over all 1,000 points, is -5.25. The gradient from a single random point scatters widely around that, with a standard deviation of 5.28. From 2,000 random minibatches of each size:

| Batch size | Average estimate | Standard deviation | Predicted by $\sigma/\sqrt{n}$ |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | -5.13 | 5.16 | 5.28 |
| 10 | -5.23 | 1.64 | 1.67 |
| 100 | -5.26 | 0.48 | 0.53 |

Table: Minibatch estimates of a gradient whose true value is -5.25. The estimates are right on average; their spread shrinks with the square root of the batch size.

The estimates are unbiased, and their noise follows the formula. That is the trade-off: a minibatch step is much cheaper than a full step, and each step is a little wrong. Over many steps, the errors average out. Some noise may even help: the textbook notes that small batches "can offer a regularizing effect ... perhaps due to the noise they add to the learning process" [@goodfellow2016deep, p. 276]. As of 2016, typical minibatch sizes were powers of 2 "from 32 to 256" [@goodfellow2016deep, p. 276].

The method itself is older than the perceptron, and it comes from statistics. In 1951 Herbert Robbins and Sutton Monro gave "a method for making successive experiments" to find where an unknown function, observed only through noisy measurements, reaches a target value [@robbins1951stochastic, p. 400]. A modern survey by Léon Bottou, Frank Curtis, and Jorge Nocedal calls the paper "a landmark in the history of numerical optimization methods" and the origin of the stochastic gradient method [@bottou2018optimization, p. 16]. Its theory explains a practical rule that comes up below. For the noisy steps to settle down, the step sizes should shrink over time: the standard convergence results ask for them to shrink slowly enough that their sum still grows without limit, but fast enough that the sum of their squares stays finite [@bottou2010large, p. 3].

Why did this old, crude method win? Bottou put it this way in 2010: "the data sizes have grown faster than the speed of processors. In this context, the capabilities of statistical machine learning methods is limited by the computing time rather than the sample size" [@bottou2010large, p. 1]. When data is plentiful and computing time is the constraint, many cheap, slightly wrong steps beat a few expensive, exact ones.

## Momentum

Gradient descent struggles in a particular kind of landscape, and the 1986 PDP chapter described it exactly: "spaces containing long ravines that are characterized by sharp curvature across the ravine and a gently sloping floor. The sharp curvature tends to cause divergent oscillations across the ravine. To prevent these it is necessary to take very small steps, but this causes very slow progress along the ravine" [@rumelhart1986internal, p. 330].

<!-- D-009: code/checks/toolkit.py, Example 3 -->

Take a loss with two weights, shaped like such a ravine: it rises 100 times more steeply across one direction than along the other. Start at a point where the loss is 100 and count the steps of gradient descent needed to bring it below one millionth. With a learning rate of 0.01, it takes 882 steps. A larger rate of 0.019 needs 463. A slightly larger rate still, 0.021, never arrives: across the ravine, the position goes 1, -1.1, 1.21, -1.331, each step overshooting by more than the last.

The fix is **momentum**: instead of stepping in the direction of the current gradient, keep a running velocity that accumulates past gradients, and step with that. The 1986 letter put it as using the gradient "to modify the velocity of the point in weight space instead of its position" [@rumelhart1986learning, p. 535]. Across the ravine, gradients alternate in sign and partly cancel in the velocity; along the floor, they point the same way every time and add up. At the same learning rate of 0.019, adding momentum of 0.5 cuts the steps needed from 463 to 221. It also calms the zig-zag: between steps 10 and 20, plain gradient descent still swings as far as 0.349 across the ravine, and the momentum path no further than 0.039.

![The first 40 steps in a narrow ravine, which rises 100 times more steeply in the vertical direction than in the horizontal one. The minimum is marked with a cross. Left: plain gradient descent zig-zags across the ravine and creeps along it. Right: at the same learning rate, momentum damps the swing across the ravine, and the steps along the floor add up, so the path gets much closer to the minimum in the same 40 steps.](../../figures/toolkit-valley.svg){#fig:toolkit-valley}

The momentum setting, usually written $\alpha$, is the share of the old velocity kept at each step. A useful way to read it is $1/(1-\alpha)$: "$\alpha = 0.9$ corresponds to multiplying the maximum speed by 10 relative to the gradient descent algorithm", and common values are 0.5, 0.9, and 0.99 [@goodfellow2016deep, p. 294]. The 1986 authors used "about 0.9" [@rumelhart1986internal, p. 330], and so did the 2012 image network.

Where momentum came from is a question the sources answer only partly. Later authors credit the method to the numerical optimization work of Boris Polyak in 1964 [@sutskever2013importance, p. 1140; @goodfellow2016deep, p. 292]. The 1986 backpropagation papers used it without citing Polyak, and nothing read for this book says they took it from him. (Polyak's paper was not read for this book; it is cited here through those later accounts.)

## Adaptive step sizes: RMSProp and Adam

One learning rate for every weight is a blunt instrument. Some weights get large gradients and some small ones, and a rate that suits one kind is wrong for the other. A family of methods gives each weight its own effective step size, based on the history of its own gradients.

One widely used method was never published as a paper. In lecture slides for a course on neural networks, Hinton described a method called **RMSProp**, credited on the slide to "Tijmen Tieleman, unpublished": "Divide the learning rate for a weight by a running average of the magnitudes of recent gradients for that weight" [@hinton2012lecture6, slides 15, 29]. (The slides are undated; later papers cite them as Tieleman and Hinton, 2012 [@kingma2015adam, p. 1].) A weight whose gradients are usually large takes proportionally smaller steps, and one whose gradients are small takes larger ones.

Diederik Kingma and Jimmy Ba combined this with momentum in **Adam**, published at the ICLR conference in 2015. Its name comes from "adaptive moment estimation": it keeps a running average of each weight's gradients (the momentum part) and of their squares (the RMSProp part), and divides one by the square root of the other [@kingma2015adam, p. 1]. Its running averages start at zero, so Adam also corrects them for that start in its first steps [@kingma2015adam, p. 2]. Its paper suggested default settings: a step size of 0.001, averaging rates of 0.9 and 0.999, and a tiny constant of $10^{-8}$ to avoid dividing by zero [@kingma2015adam, p. 2].

<!-- D-009: code/checks/toolkit.py, Example 4 -->

The effect of the division is easiest to see in numbers. Start one weight at zero and feed Adam three gradients in turn: 0.5, 0.4, and -0.1. The steps it takes are 0.001000, 0.000988, and 0.000656. The third gradient points the other way, but the weight keeps moving in the same direction, carried by the running average: that is the momentum part. Now compare first steps. With a first gradient of 0.001, Adam steps 0.001; with a first gradient of 1,000, Adam also steps 0.001. Plain gradient descent with a learning rate of 0.001 would step one millionth in the first case and 1 in the second. Adam's steps are roughly bounded by its step-size setting whatever the scale of the gradient, which the authors describe as "establishing a trust region around the current parameter value" [@kingma2015adam, p. 3]. By 2017, Ashia Wilson and colleagues could describe Adam as "the default algorithm used across many deep learning frameworks" [@wilson2017marginal, p. 1].

Adam has a more complicated record than its popularity suggests. In 2018, Sashank Reddi, Satyen Kale, and Sanjiv Kumar showed that its original convergence proof was wrong, giving "an explicit example of a simple convex optimization setting where ADAM does not converge to the optimal solution" [@reddi2018convergence, pp. 1, 5]. Wilson and colleagues also reported that on the models they tested, solutions found by adaptive methods "generalize worse (often significantly worse) than SGD, even when these solutions have better training performance" [@wilson2017marginal, p. 1]. Part of that gap was traced to a detail. **Weight decay** shrinks every weight a little at each step. For plain SGD that is the same as adding the L2 penalty of ridge regression to the loss, which is why the field often uses the two names interchangeably [@goodfellow2016deep, p. 227]. For Adam, the two are not the same [@loshchilov2019decoupled, p. 1]. Ilya Loshchilov and Frank Hutter showed that applying the shrinkage separately "substantially improves Adam's generalization performance" [@loshchilov2019decoupled, p. 1]. The resulting method is called **AdamW**, and it is the optimizer in the language-model recipe at the end of this chapter.

## Changing the step size over time

The learning rate is not usually held fixed. "In practice, it is necessary to gradually decrease the learning rate over time" [@goodfellow2016deep, p. 290], for the reason Robbins and Monro's theory gives: minibatch noise does not disappear near the minimum, and only smaller steps let the weights settle. The plan for changing the rate is the **learning-rate schedule**. The textbook is frank about how schedules are chosen: "This is more of an art than a science, and most guidance on this subject should be regarded with some skepticism" [@goodfellow2016deep, p. 291].

![Three learning-rate schedules, each drawn as a share of its own peak rate. Left: the rate divided by 10 at a few points during training (drawn schematically; in practice when progress stalls). Middle: a cosine curve from the peak down to 10% of it. Right: a short warmup, then a slow decline.](../../figures/toolkit-schedules.svg){#fig:toolkit-schedules}

Three shapes appear in the recipes from 2015 to 2024. The step schedule divides the rate by 10 when progress stalls; the 2015 ResNet image network, described in [the next chapter](#depth), started at 0.1 and was "divided by 10 when the error plateaus" [@he2015deep, p. 4]. The cosine schedule lowers the rate along a smooth half-wave [@loshchilov2017sgdr, p. 4]. <!-- D-009: code/checks/toolkit.py, Example 5 --> From a peak of 0.0003 down to a tenth of that, it passes 0.000260 a quarter of the way through training, 0.000165 halfway, and 0.0000695 at three quarters. The third shape starts with a **warmup**: the rate begins small and rises before it falls.

Warmup sounds backward. One explanation is that at the very start, with random weights, the gradients can be large and erratic, so full-size steps are risky until training settles. Its history is a chronology rather than an invention. The 2015 ResNet paper found that for a 110-layer network "the initial learning rate of 0.1 is slightly too large to start converging. So we use 0.01 to warm up the training until the training error is below 80% (about 400 iterations), and then go back to 0.1" [@he2015deep, p. 7]. In 2017 Priya Goyal and colleagues at Facebook, training an image network with very large minibatches of 8,192 images, proposed a "gradual warmup" that ramps the rate up over the first few epochs. It was one of two techniques that let them train it "on 256 GPUs in one hour, while matching small minibatch accuracy" [@goyal2017accurate, pp. 1, 3]. The other was a rule of thumb: "When the minibatch size is multiplied by k, multiply the learning rate by k" [@goyal2017accurate, p. 2]. The same year, the transformer paper of [the chapter on attention](#attention) raised its learning rate linearly over the first 4,000 steps and then lowered it in proportion to one over the square root of the step number [@vaswani2017attention, p. 7]. Its rate peaks at about 0.0007 at step 4,000 and has fallen to 0.00014 by step 100,000. By 2017, a short warmup appeared both in training with very large minibatches and in the transformer.

## Clipping the gradient

[The chapter on networks with structure](#structure) described the exploding gradient: a backward signal multiplied by large factors can grow until a single step throws the weights far away. A blunt but effective guard is **gradient clipping**. Razvan Pascanu, Tomas Mikolov, and Yoshua Bengio proposed "a gradient norm clipping strategy to deal with exploding gradients" [@pascanu2013difficulty, p. 1310]: if the gradient, viewed as one long vector, is longer than a threshold, shrink it to the threshold, keeping its direction. <!-- D-009: code/checks/toolkit.py, Example 6 --> A gradient of (3, 4) has length 5; clipped to length 1 it becomes (0.6, 0.8), pointing the same way. They used a threshold of 1 [@pascanu2013difficulty, p. 1316], and the language-model recipe below uses a threshold of 1.0 too.

## Hyperparameters, validation, and early stopping

Every setting in this chapter so far, the batch size, the momentum, the learning rate and its schedule, the clipping threshold, is a **hyperparameter**. In the textbook's words: "Most machine learning algorithms have hyperparameters, settings that we can use to control the algorithm's behavior. The values of hyperparameters are not adapted by the learning algorithm itself" [@goodfellow2016deep, p. 118]. They are chosen in the usual way, on a **validation set** held out from the training data [@goodfellow2016deep, p. 119]. Of all of them, "The learning rate is perhaps the most important hyperparameter. If you have time to tune only one hyperparameter, tune the learning rate" [@goodfellow2016deep, p. 424].

Another tool from classical practice carries over too. With a network that can fit its training data almost perfectly, the validation loss typically falls, bottoms out, and rises again as the network starts fitting noise. The remedy is to keep a copy of the weights whenever the validation loss improves, and to return the best copy at the end. This is **early stopping**, which the textbook calls "probably the most commonly used form of regularization in deep learning" [@goodfellow2016deep, p. 243]. It is more closely related to ridge regression than it looks: for a linear model with squared error trained by gradient descent, "early stopping is equivalent to L2 regularization" [@goodfellow2016deep, p. 247]. Stopping early keeps the weights small, the way a penalty would.

## Reading a loss curve

A **loss curve**, the loss plotted against training time, is the main instrument panel of training. <!-- D-009: code/checks/toolkit.py, Example 7 -->

With a learning rate of 0.00005, both losses are still falling after 300 epochs: the training loss is at 0.457 and the validation loss at 3.7. The model is learning, just slowly. With 0.08, the losses grow without limit; the training loss passes a million by epoch 15. This is the overshooting of [the chapter on learning in layers](#backprop), now in a model with 15 weights. With 0.001, the training loss falls quickly toward zero, and the validation loss falls from 6.96 at the start to its lowest point, 2.68, at epoch 82. After that it rises slowly, to 2.82 by epoch 300, while the training loss keeps shrinking. With 20 examples and 15 weights, the model has started to memorize the noise in its training data. Early stopping would return the weights from epoch 82.

Real loss curves show other patterns. [The chapter on learning in layers](#backprop) showed a long plateau followed by a sudden drop. Large-scale training also produces spikes: sudden jumps in the loss that may or may not recover. Goyal and colleagues found that leaving a warmup too abruptly "can cause the training error to spike" [@goyal2017accurate, p. 3], and the team that trained Llama 3 counted it as a success that "we observed few loss spikes" [@grattafiori2024llama, p. 14].

## Automatic differentiation and frameworks

All of this needs gradients, for networks with millions or billions of weights and arbitrary arrangements of layers. Nobody derives them by hand. **Automatic differentiation**, in the definition of a 2018 survey by Atılım Güneş Baydin and colleagues, is "a family of techniques similar to but more general than backpropagation for efficiently and accurately evaluating derivatives of numeric functions expressed as computer programs" [@baydin2018automatic, p. 1]. It is neither of the two obvious candidates. It is not symbolic differentiation, which manipulates formulas, and it is not numerical differentiation, which nudges each input and has "the disadvantages of performing O(n) evaluations" for $n$ inputs [@baydin2018automatic, pp. 3-4]. In its reverse mode, the one networks use, it records each elementary operation as the program runs forward, then applies the chain rule to those operations in reverse.

<!-- D-009: code/checks/toolkit.py, Example 8 -->

The survey's running example is a small function of two inputs: the natural logarithm of $x_1$, plus $x_1$ times $x_2$, minus the sine of $x_2$, evaluated at $x_1 = 2$ and $x_2 = 5$. The forward pass computes the pieces (0.693, 10, and -0.959) and the result, 11.652. One backward pass then gives both derivatives at once: 5.500 with respect to $x_1$ and 1.716 with respect to $x_2$ [@baydin2018automatic, p. 13]. With a million inputs, it would still take one backward pass. That is the reverse mode of [the chapter on learning in layers](#backprop), and it is why the survey calls backpropagation "only a special case" of automatic differentiation [@baydin2018automatic, p. 17].

Software that provides tensors on GPUs together with automatic differentiation is called a **framework**. Theano was described at a 2010 conference as "a compiler for mathematical expressions in Python" that could "provide symbolic differentiation" and generate GPU code [@bergstra2010theano]. (Its authors' term aside, the textbook describes Theano's approach as adding to the computation a description of its own derivatives [@goodfellow2016deep, p. 210].) Torch7, built on the Lua language, appeared in 2011 [@collobert2011torch7, p. 1]. Google open-sourced TensorFlow in November 2015, as the 2016 arXiv version of its white paper records [@abadi2015tensorflow, p. 1], and PyTorch was first released in January 2017 [@paszke2019pytorch, p. 9]. They differ in style. Some have the user first describe the whole computation as a graph and then run it; others, PyTorch among them, let the user write the forward computation as an ordinary program, and record it as it runs [@baydin2018automatic, p. 18]. By 2019, PyTorch's authors could report that "296 ICLR 2019 submissions" mentioned it [@paszke2019pytorch, p. 2]. Which framework is most used at any given time changes; Book 2 uses one and states its version.

## Reading a recipe

Here is the optimizer half of the training recipe for LLaMA, a family of language models released by Meta in 2023:

> Our models are trained using the AdamW optimizer (Loshchilov and Hutter, 2017), with the following hyper-parameters: β1 = 0.9, β2 = 0.95. We use a cosine learning rate schedule, such that the final learning rate is equal to 10% of the maximal learning rate. We use a weight decay of 0.1 and gradient clipping of 1.0. We use 2,000 warmup steps, and vary the learning rate and batch size with the size of the model (see Table 2 for details). [@touvron2023llama, p. 3]

Every term is now familiar. AdamW is Adam with weight decay applied separately. β1 and β2 are its two averaging rates; 0.9 is Kingma and Ba's default, and 0.95 is lower than their 0.999, so the running average of squared gradients forgets old steps faster. The cosine schedule is the middle curve in the figure above, here ending at a tenth of its peak. Weight decay of 0.1 is the ridge-style shrinkage. Gradient clipping of 1.0 sets the clipping threshold at 1. The 2,000 warmup steps raise the learning rate from small to its peak before the cosine decline begins. The peak learning rates in the paper's table are 0.0003 for the smaller models and 0.00015 for the larger ones [@touvron2023llama, p. 3]. The batch size, 4 million, is counted in tokens, the pieces of text introduced in [the destination chapter](#destination) and explained in [the chapter on tokens](#tokens).

The 2024 recipe for the largest Llama 3 model reads the same way: AdamW "with a peak learning rate of 8 × 10^-5, a linear warm up of 8,000 steps, and a cosine learning rate schedule decaying to 8 × 10^-7 over 1,200,000 steps" [@grattafiori2024llama, p. 14]. These two recipes are as of 2023 and 2024; recipes change, and the vocabulary changes more slowly.

## What to carry forward

- Data and weights are tensors: arrays with any number of axes. The number of weights depends on the layer, the size of the activations on how many examples go through at once.
- Stochastic gradient descent estimates the gradient from a random minibatch. The estimate is unbiased, and its noise shrinks with the square root of the batch size. The method descends from Robbins and Monro's 1951 stochastic approximation.
- Momentum accumulates a velocity, which damps zig-zagging across a ravine and speeds progress along it (221 steps instead of 463 at the same learning rate in this chapter's example).
- Adam gives each weight its own step size, roughly independent of the gradient's scale. Its original convergence proof was flawed, and a variant with separate weight decay, AdamW, trained the LLaMA models.
- Learning rates follow schedules: usually a short warmup, then a decline (in steps, along a cosine, or by a formula). Gradient clipping caps the size of any one step.
- Hyperparameters are chosen on a validation set, and early stopping (a relative of ridge regression) returns the weights from before the validation loss turned up.
- Frameworks provide tensors on GPUs and compute every gradient by reverse-mode automatic differentiation, of which backpropagation is a special case.

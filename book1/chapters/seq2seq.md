[The previous chapter](#embeddings) gave each word a vector. A sentence is more than its words, though: "dog bites man" and "man bites dog" use the same three. This chapter is about networks that read a whole sentence and write another one, and the task that drove them was machine translation. Between 2014 and 2016, translation went from statistical systems built on tables of counted phrases to neural networks trained end to end, and on the way researchers met a bottleneck and invented the mechanism, **attention**, that sits at the center of today's LLMs (as of 2026).

The tools come from [the chapter on networks with structure](#structure): the recurrent network, which reads one word at a time and carries a hidden state forward, and the LSTM, whose gates let it remember over longer stretches. That chapter left them as a 1990s technique that worked on small problems. This one shows them at scale.

## A recurrent network as a language model

[The prediction chapter](#prediction) ended with n-gram models at their limits, and [the embeddings chapter](#embeddings) showed a neural language model that predicts the next word from the vectors of a fixed number of previous words. The fixed window is its weakness: it has to be chosen before training, and in practice such networks saw only the five to ten words before the one they predicted [@mikolov2010recurrent, p. 1045]. A recurrent network has no such limit. In 2010, Tomas Mikolov and colleagues built a language model from a simple recurrent network, Elman's 1990 design from [the structure chapter](#structure), with a word as input at each step and a softmax over the vocabulary as output [@mikolov2010recurrent, p. 1046].

<!-- D-009: code/checks/seq2seq.py, Example 1 -->

The model was trained on 6.4 million words of newspaper text and tested on a Wall Street Journal speech-recognition task. A 5-gram model with Kneser-Ney smoothing, trained on the same data, had a perplexity of 221. A single recurrent network had 173, about 22% lower. A mixture of three recurrent networks that kept adjusting their weights as they read the test text, combined with the n-gram model, reached 121, and the best mixture 112, nearly half. In speech recognition, that mixture of three cut the word error rate from 13.5% to 11.1% [@mikolov2010recurrent, p. 1047]. Language modeling, the authors concluded, was not "just about counting n-grams" [@mikolov2010recurrent, p. 1048].

Two limits were plain in the same paper. Training was slow: the largest models took several weeks [@mikolov2010recurrent, p. 1047]. And the long memory was more promise than fact: the authors doubted that simple recurrent networks could capture truly long context [@mikolov2010recurrent, p. 1048], the vanishing gradient again.

## Translation before neural networks

By the late 2000s, machine translation was dominated by **statistical machine translation**, learned from a **parallel corpus**: a large collection of sentences paired with their human translations. The leading form, described in 2003, was **phrase-based translation**. The system cuts the input sentence into phrases, translates each phrase, and may reorder the translated phrases [@koehn2003statistical, p. 49].

The translations of phrases come from a **phrase table** built by counting. First the words of each sentence pair are aligned, by older statistical models, to say which word translates which. Then every pair of phrases consistent with the alignment is collected, and the probability of each translation is estimated by its relative frequency [@koehn2003statistical, p. 50]. That is the n-gram recipe of [the prediction chapter](#prediction) applied to pairs of phrases: count and divide. The tables were large: 320,000 sentence pairs yielded 3,152,000 distinct phrase pairs [@koehn2003statistical, p. 51], about ten per sentence pair. A finished system combined the phrase table with an n-gram language model for the output language and other scores, each with its own weight [@cho2014learning, pp. 1726-1727]: a weighted sum of scores, the same form as logistic regression.

How do you score a translation system? Almost every paper in this chapter uses **BLEU**, proposed in 2002 on a simple premise: the closer a machine translation is to a professional human translation, the better it is [@papineni2002bleu, p. 311]. BLEU counts how many of the system's word sequences of one to four words also appear in one or more human reference translations, combines these counts into one number, and penalizes output that is too short [@papineni2002bleu, p. 315]. A word cannot be credited more often than it appears in a reference, so seven copies of "the" against "The cat is on the mat" score 2/7, not 7/7 [@papineni2002bleu, p. 312]. Papers report BLEU on a scale of 0 to 100. Even a human translator need not score 100, because good translations differ [@papineni2002bleu, p. 315], and even researchers who reported BLEU acknowledged that it does not fully capture the quality of a translation [@wu2016google, p. 14].

Phrase-based translation worked well enough to become a product: Google Translate was built on it when it launched, about ten years before 2016 [@le2016neural]. Neural networks entered these systems first as parts. Mostly they supplied one more score to an existing system, or re-ranked its candidates: the old system proposed a list of translations, its **n-best list**, and the network picked among them [@bahdanau2015neural, p. 9].

## One vector for the whole sentence

The bolder idea was to let one network do the whole job: read the source sentence into a vector, then write the translation from that vector. The network that reads is the **encoder**, and the network that writes is the **decoder**.

The idea was not new. Models of this shape had been tried in 1997, but never on enough data to translate anything beyond toy examples, and the idea was set aside for almost two decades [@koehn2017neural, p. 5]. In 2013, a translation model built entirely from vectors for words, phrases and sentences used a convolutional network to read the source and a recurrent language model to write the target [@kalchbrenner2013recurrent, p. 1700]. It was measured by perplexity and by re-ranking candidate translations, not by translating test sentences on its own [@kalchbrenner2013recurrent, pp. 1700-1701].

In June 2014, Kyunghyun Cho and colleagues, with Yoshua Bengio, proposed an "RNN Encoder-Decoder": two recurrent networks, one encoding a sequence of symbols into a vector of fixed length, the other decoding that vector into another sequence [@cho2014learning, p. 1724]. After the encoder reads the last word, its hidden state is a summary of the whole input [@cho2014learning, p. 1725]. Like the 2013 model, theirs served as one more score inside a phrase-based system: it rated the pairs in the phrase table [@cho2014learning, p. 1727]. That raised BLEU on a standard English-French test from 33.30 to 33.87 [@cho2014learning, p. 1729].

The same paper introduced a simpler alternative to the LSTM, a unit inspired by it but much simpler to compute and implement [@cho2014learning, p. 1726]. It has two gates instead of the LSTM's three and no separate memory cell. An **update gate** decides how much of the old hidden state to keep, and a **reset gate** lets the unit ignore the old state and start again from the current input [@cho2014learning, p. 1726]. A few months later it was given its name, the **gated recurrent unit**, or GRU [@chung2014empirical, p. 2]. A comparison on music and speech data found the two comparable, with no clear winner [@chung2014empirical, pp. 1, 7].

In September 2014, Ilya Sutskever and colleagues went all the way: a deep LSTM read the input sentence into a vector of fixed size, and another deep LSTM wrote the output from it [@sutskever2014sequence, p. 1]. They called it sequence to sequence learning. The decoder is essentially a recurrent language model, like Mikolov's, except that it is conditioned on the input [@sutskever2014sequence, p. 2]: a language model that is told what to talk about. To know when to stop, every sentence ended with a special end-of-sentence symbol, "<EOS>" [@sutskever2014sequence, p. 3]. The network translated on its own, writing one word at a time. Rather than keep only the single most probable next word at each step, it kept the few best partial translations, a procedure called **beam search**; a beam of two already gave most of the benefit [@sutskever2014sequence, p. 4].

An **ensemble** of five such networks, their predictions combined, scored 34.81 BLEU on the English-French test, against 33.30 for a phrase-based system on the same data: by the authors' account, the first time a purely neural system had beaten a phrase-based baseline on a large task by a sizeable margin [@sutskever2014sequence, pp. 2, 5-6]. It did not beat the best system of the day, which scored 37.0; used to re-rank the phrase-based system's candidates, the networks reached 36.5 [@sutskever2014sequence, p. 6].

The models were large for 2014. Each had four layers of 1,000 units in both the encoder and the decoder, and 380 million parameters, of which 64 million were recurrent connections [@sutskever2014sequence, p. 5]. <!-- D-009: code/checks/seq2seq.py, Example 3 --> The paper does not say where the rest are, but a rough reconstruction shows it: the input vocabulary of 160,000 words and the output vocabulary of 80,000 [@sutskever2014sequence, p. 4], at 1,000 numbers per word in the input tables and the output layer, account for 320 million. About 83% of the model was word tables, a cost that returns in [the chapter on tokens](#tokens). Training used eight GPUs, one per layer and four more for the output softmax, and took about ten days [@sutskever2014sequence, p. 5].

One trick mattered more than its simplicity suggests: they fed the source sentence in reverse, which the authors counted among the paper's key contributions [@sutskever2014sequence, p. 2]. BLEU rose from 25.9 to 30.6 [@sutskever2014sequence, p. 4]. They had no complete explanation, but believed it helped by creating short links between input and output [@sutskever2014sequence, p. 4]. <!-- D-009: code/checks/seq2seq.py, Example 4 --> Lay out a three-word source, the end symbol, and a three-word translation in one line. In normal order, each source word is four steps from its translation. Reversed, the distances are 2, 4, and 6: the average is unchanged, but the first words of the translation are now close to their sources, as the paper says [@sutskever2014sequence, p. 4]. This book reads it as the difficulty met in [the structure chapter](#structure): long paths are hard to learn across, and short ones make learning easier.

A look inside the encoder showed what its vector held. A PCA of the final vectors for a set of short phrases showed them grouped by meaning, here mostly a matter of word order, which would be hard to capture with a bag-of-words model [@sutskever2014sequence, p. 6]. "Mary admires John" and "John admires Mary" ended up in different places.

Several groups have a claim to the encoder-decoder; [the credit appendix](#credit-seq2seq) sets out who did what.

## The bottleneck

Everything the decoder knows about the source must pass through one vector of fixed size, whether the sentence has five words or fifty. Did that hurt?

Cho and colleagues tested it directly in a second 2014 paper, with two neural models. Neural translation did relatively well on short sentences without unknown words, but got rapidly worse as sentences grew longer and unknown words more frequent [@cho2014properties, p. 103]. Their explanation, offered as the most obvious hypothesis, was that a vector of fixed length lacks the capacity to encode a long sentence with complicated structure and meaning [@cho2014properties, p. 107]. The phrase-based system showed the opposite trend, scoring higher on longer sentences [@cho2014properties, p. 107]. The neural models had one advantage: they needed only 500 MB of memory in total, where phrase-based systems often needed tens of gigabytes [@cho2014properties, p. 103].

Sutskever and colleagues, by contrast, found no degradation on sentences of fewer than 35 words and only a minor one on the longest [@sutskever2014sequence, pp. 2, 7], although they had expected the LSTM to fail on long sentences because of its limited memory [@sutskever2014sequence, p. 8]. The two findings are less contradictory than they look. Cho's recurrent model had 1,000 hidden units, a 30,000-word vocabulary, and about 110 hours of training [@cho2014properties, p. 106]; Sutskever's were four-layer ensembles with much larger vocabularies, trained for ten days on eight GPUs, with the source reversed. Whether one vector was enough depended on how much model stood behind it.

## Attention: looking back at the source

Dzmitry Bahdanau, with Cho and Bengio, posted a different answer in September 2014. They conjectured that the fixed-length vector was a bottleneck, and let the model search the source sentence, softly, for the parts relevant to the next word it writes [@bahdanau2015neural, p. 1].

Their model does not squeeze the sentence into one vector. It encodes the input as a sequence of vectors and, while translating, chooses among them [@bahdanau2015neural, p. 2]. The encoder keeps one vector for every source word. It is **bidirectional**: one recurrent network reads the sentence left to right, another right to left, and each word's vector joins the two, so that it summarizes both the words before it and the words after it [@bahdanau2015neural, p. 4].

![(a) In an encoder-decoder, the encoder's last state is the only thing passed to the decoder. (b) With attention, the encoder keeps a vector for every source word, and before writing each word the decoder takes a weighted average of all of them. The line thicknesses are the weights from the worked example in the text, for the decoder about to write "zone".](../../figures/seq2seq-bottleneck.svg){#fig:seq2seq-bottleneck}

Before writing each word of the translation, the decoder does three things.

1. **Score** each source word: how well does it match what the decoder is about to write? Bahdanau's model computed the score with a small neural network that looks at the decoder's current state and the source word's vector [@bahdanau2015neural, pp. 3, 12].
2. **Turn the scores into weights** with a softmax, the same function that ends [the destination chapter](#destination)'s pipeline, so that the weights are positive and add up to one [@bahdanau2015neural, p. 3].
3. **Average** the source vectors using those weights. The result, a "context vector", is a weighted sum of the source word vectors, and it is fed to the decoder along with its own state [@bahdanau2015neural, p. 3].

<!-- D-009: code/checks/seq2seq.py, Example 5 -->

A small example, with numbers made up for the purpose. Take the English words "the European Economic Area", each with a vector of four numbers, and score them with a plain dot product, a simpler choice used by later work [@luong2015effective, p. 1414]. Suppose the decoder, about to write the French "zone", has a state whose dot products with the four source vectors are 0.20, 0.39, 0.39, and 3.06. The softmax turns these into weights of 0.05, 0.06, 0.06, and 0.84: most of the attention goes to "Area", and the four weights sum to one (up to rounding). One step later, about to write "économique", the decoder's state has changed, the scores are 0.20, 1.20, 3.09, and 1.10, and the weights become 0.04, 0.11, 0.74, and 0.10, with most of the weight on "Economic". The same source vectors, read with different weights at each step.

The paper called the weights a **soft alignment**. Phrase-based systems used hard alignments, fixed during training, that linked each word to particular words (or none) in its translation. A soft alignment lets the gradient of the cost pass through it [@bahdanau2015neural, p. 4]: because every step is a smooth function, the whole thing is trained by backpropagation with the rest of the network. Soft alignment also helps with words whose translation depends on their neighbors. To translate "the" in "the man" into French, the model must look at the next word to choose among le, la, les and l', and it can attend to both words at once [@bahdanau2015neural, p. 7]. The word that stuck appears in only one passage of the paper, as an intuition: "this implements a mechanism of attention in the decoder. The decoder decides parts of the source sentence to pay attention to" [@bahdanau2015neural, p. 4].

The weights here are a softmax of made-up scores, not the output of a trained model. They mimic what the paper reports for its trained model: mostly along the diagonal, with the order reversed for "European Economic Area" and "zone économique européenne".](../../figures/seq2seq-attention-heatmap.svg){#fig:seq2seq-attention-heatmap}

Because the weights are numbers, they can be drawn. The paper printed its trained model's weights as grids, one row per French word and one column per English word [@bahdanau2015neural, p. 6]. The alignment ran mostly along the diagonal, with exceptions where French and English order differ: the model aligned "zone" with "Area", jumping over "European" and "Economic", then looked back one word at a time to complete "zone économique européenne" [@bahdanau2015neural, p. 7]. The figure above is a constructed version of that pattern.

The bottleneck is gone. In Bahdanau's model, each source word's vector has 2,000 numbers, 1,000 from each direction [@bahdanau2015neural, p. 5]. A 10-word sentence leaves the decoder 20,000 numbers to look at, a 50-word sentence 100,000, where an encoder-decoder of the same size passes on one state of 1,000 numbers however long the sentence. Plotted against sentence length, the basic encoder-decoder's BLEU score fell sharply as sentences grew longer, while the attention model trained on sentences of up to 50 words showed no deterioration, even on sentences of 50 words or more [@bahdanau2015neural, p. 6].

The results were striking but not yet a victory. On the whole test set, the attention model scored 26.75 BLEU, or 28.45 when trained longer, against 33.30 for the phrase-based system. On sentences with no unknown words, the longer-trained model scored 36.15 against 35.63, a match for the older system [@bahdanau2015neural, p. 7]. The gap was the vocabulary: with only the 30,000 most frequent words in each language [@bahdanau2015neural, p. 5], every rarer word became an "unknown" symbol, and the authors named rare and unknown words as a challenge for future work [@bahdanau2015neural, p. 9]. That problem is the subject of [the chapter on tokens](#tokens).

The idea had a forerunner. A 2013 network that generated handwriting from text used a soft window over the characters to decide which one to write next [@graves2013generating, pp. 24, 26]. That window could only move forward through the text. Translation needs more, because word order differs between languages and words often have to be reordered, sometimes over long distances [@bahdanau2015neural, p. 8]. How the two relate is one of the questions in [the credit appendix](#credit-seq2seq).

Attention had a cost, which the authors noted: a weight for every source word, computed again for every word of the translation [@bahdanau2015neural, p. 8]. <!-- D-009: code/checks/seq2seq.py, Example 8 --> For a 30-word sentence and a 30-word translation, that is 900 scores; for 50 and 50, 2,500. The number grows with the product of the two lengths. For translation, where most sentences have 15 to 40 words, the cost was not severe [@bahdanau2015neural, p. 8]. For the much longer inputs of today's LLMs, it becomes a central concern, as [the chapter on attention](#attention) shows.

## Replaced in about two years

Attention spread quickly. In 2015, a study of several ways to compute the scores, including the plain dot product used above, set a new best result on English-to-German translation [@luong2015effective, pp. 1412, 1414]. At the 2016 Conference on Machine Translation, the field's yearly shared evaluation (known as WMT), the University of Edinburgh's systems were attentional encoder-decoders [@sennrich2016edinburgh, p. 371]. In the official human ranking, a pure neural system was in the top group, alone or tied, for 8 of the 12 translation directions; for the other 4, including English to Russian and both directions between English and Turkish, no pure neural system was in the top group [@bojar2016findings, p. 141].

A 2017 textbook chapter recounted how fast the change was. At the 2015 shared task, only one pure neural system was submitted; it was competitive, but statistical systems beat it. A year later, neural systems won in almost all language pairs, and by 2017 almost all submissions were neural. Within a year or two, the whole research field of machine translation had gone neural [@koehn2017neural, p. 6].

Industry followed. In September 2016, Google described its Neural Machine Translation system, GNMT: a deep LSTM network with 8 encoder and 8 decoder layers, residual connections, and attention from the decoder to the encoder [@wu2016google, p. 1]. It was the 2014 recipe, scaled up with the residual connections of [the chapter on deep networks](#depth) and with words split into a limited set of common sub-word units, "wordpieces", to handle rare words [@wu2016google, p. 1]. Training a basic English-French model took around 6 days on 96 NVIDIA K80 GPUs [@wu2016google, p. 15]. As of 27 September 2016, Google Translate used it for all of its translations from Chinese to English, about 18 million a day [@le2016neural].

The headline claim was that GNMT reduced translation errors "by an average of 60%" [@wu2016google, p. 1]. What was measured is worth knowing. Human raters scored translations of 500 isolated sentences per language pair on a scale from 0 to 6, comparing the old phrase-based system, GNMT, and human translators [@wu2016google, pp. 14, 18-19]. <!-- D-009: code/checks/seq2seq.py, Example 7 --> The paper does not define its "relative improvement", but each printed value matches, to within a point, the share of the gap between the old system and the human translators that GNMT closed. For English to Spanish, the ratings were 4.885 for the old system, 5.428 for GNMT, and 5.504 for humans, so GNMT closed 87.7% of the gap; across six language pairs the printed values ranged from 58% to 87% [@wu2016google, p. 19]. It is a share of a rating gap, not a count of errors. The authors were clear about the limits: the test sentences were relatively simple and isolated, and GNMT could still make significant errors that a human translator would never make, such as dropping words and mistranslating proper names or rare terms [@wu2016google, p. 19; @le2016neural].

## Why recurrence was slow

One problem remained, and it shaped every design in this chapter. A recurrent network must finish word 1 before it can start word 2, because each step needs the previous hidden state. The paper that would replace recurrence named the cost: this sequential order rules out parallel work within a training example, which becomes critical as sequences grow longer [@vaswani2017attention, p. 2]. A GPU does thousands of things at once, but it cannot do step 2 before step 1.

<!-- D-009: code/checks/seq2seq.py, Example 8 -->

Engineers worked around it. They trained on many sentences at a time, and they put different layers on different GPUs, so that layer 2 could start on a word as soon as layer 1 had finished it: Sutskever and colleagues ran one layer per GPU [@sutskever2014sequence, p. 5]. For a 30-word sentence and four layers, that turns 120 steps done one after another into 33: 30 steps for the first layer, plus 3 for the last layer to catch up. GNMT's designers chose their architecture for parallelism: only the first encoder layer read in both directions, because more bidirectional layers would reduce parallelism, and attention connected only the bottom decoder layer to the top encoder layer, because connecting the top layers would have removed all parallelism in the decoder [@wu2016google, pp. 6-7]. Still, within one layer, 30 words meant 30 steps.

Writing the output is word by word for every model, including the transformers that came next: each new word depends on the ones already written. The difference lies in training, where the whole target sentence is known in advance. By 2017, recurrent networks with LSTM or GRU units were firmly established as the state of the art for language modeling and translation, and attention was almost always used together with a recurrent network [@vaswani2017attention, p. 2]. The next question was whether the recurrence was needed at all, and [the chapter on attention](#attention) takes it up.

## What to carry forward

- Recurrent networks became strong language models around 2010, beating smoothed n-grams on the same data, but they were slow to train and their memory was shorter than hoped.
- Statistical machine translation in the 2000s counted phrase pairs in parallel text and combined many scores with learned weights. BLEU, an overlap count against human translations, became the standard score.
- An encoder-decoder (sequence to sequence) network reads a sentence into a vector and writes the output from it. In 2014 it first beat a phrase-based baseline, with large LSTMs and the source reversed.
- The fixed vector is a bottleneck. Small models degraded on long sentences; large ones less so.
- Attention keeps a vector per source word and, before each output word, takes a weighted average of them, with weights from a softmax over learned match scores. It is trained by backpropagation like everything else, and its weights can be drawn as an alignment.
- Within about two years (2014-2016), attentional encoder-decoders replaced phrase-based systems in research and began to replace them in products.
- Recurrence forces one step after another within a sentence, which limits how much a GPU can do at once. That is the problem the transformer set out to solve.

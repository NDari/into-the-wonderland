# Reading and writing sequences (2010-2016) {#seq2seq}

[The previous chapter](#embeddings) gave each word a vector. A sentence is more than its words, though: "dog bites man" and "man bites dog" use the same three. This chapter is about networks that read a whole sentence and write another one, and the task that drove them was machine translation. Between 2014 and 2016, translation went from statistical systems built on tables of counted phrases to neural networks trained end to end, and on the way researchers met a bottleneck and invented the mechanism, **attention**, at the center of every modern LLM.

The tools come from [the chapter on networks with structure](#structure): the recurrent network, which reads one word at a time and carries a hidden state forward, and the LSTM, whose gates let it remember over longer stretches. That chapter left them as a 1990s technique that worked on small problems. This one shows them at scale.

## A recurrent network as a language model

[The prediction chapter](#prediction) ended with n-gram models at their limits, and [the embeddings chapter](#embeddings) showed Bengio and colleagues' neural language model, which predicts the next word from the vectors of a fixed number of previous words. In 2010, Tomas Mikolov and colleagues pointed out the weakness of that fixed window: "a feedforward network has to use fixed length context that needs to be specified ad hoc before training. Usually this means that neural networks see only five to ten preceding words when predicting the next one" [@mikolov2010recurrent, p. 1045]. A recurrent network has no such limit. They used "an architecture that is usually called a simple recurrent neural network or Elman network" [@mikolov2010recurrent, p. 1046], Elman's 1990 design from [the structure chapter](#structure), with a word as input at each step and a softmax over the vocabulary as output.

<!-- D-009: code/checks/seq2seq.py, Example 1 -->

On 6.4 million words of Wall Street Journal text, the best smoothed n-gram model, trained on the same data, had a perplexity of 221 [@mikolov2010recurrent, p. 1047]. A single recurrent network had 173, about 22% lower. A mixture of three recurrent networks that kept adjusting their weights as they read the test text, combined with the n-gram model, reached 121, and the best mixture 112 [@mikolov2010recurrent, p. 1047], nearly half. Used in speech recognition, that mixture of three cut the word error rate from 13.5% to 11.1% [@mikolov2010recurrent, p. 1047]. The authors concluded that their results were "breaking myth that language modeling is just about counting n-grams" [@mikolov2010recurrent, p. 1048].

Two limits were plain in the same paper. Training was slow: "it takes several weeks to train the most complex models" [@mikolov2010recurrent, p. 1047]. And the long memory was more promise than fact: "it does not seem that simple recurrent neural networks can capture truly long context information" [@mikolov2010recurrent, p. 1048], the vanishing gradient again.

## Translation before neural networks

By the late 2000s, machine translation was dominated by **statistical machine translation**, learned from a **parallel corpus**: a large collection of sentences paired with their human translations. The leading form was **phrase-based translation**, described by Philipp Koehn, Franz Josef Och, and Daniel Marcu in 2003. The system cuts the input sentence into phrases, translates each phrase, and may reorder the translated phrases [@koehn2003statistical, p. 49].

The translations of phrases come from a **phrase table** built by counting. First the words of each sentence pair are aligned, by older statistical models, to say which word translates which. Then every pair of phrases consistent with the alignment is collected, and "we estimate the phrase translation probability distribution by relative frequency" [@koehn2003statistical, p. 50]. That is the n-gram recipe of [the prediction chapter](#prediction) applied to pairs of phrases: count and divide. The tables were large. From 320,000 sentence pairs, Koehn and colleagues collected 3,152,000 distinct phrase pairs [@koehn2003statistical, p. 51], about ten per sentence pair. A finished system combined the phrase table with an n-gram language model for the output language and other scores, in "a log-linear model with additional features and corresponding weights" [@cho2014learning, p. 1727]: a weighted sum of scores, the form the reader knows from logistic regression.

How do you score a translation system? Almost every paper in this chapter uses **BLEU**, proposed by Kishore Papineni and colleagues at IBM in 2002. "The closer a machine translation is to a professional human translation, the better it is. This is the central idea behind our proposal" [@papineni2002bleu, p. 311]. BLEU counts how many of the system's word sequences of one to four words also appear in one or more human reference translations, combines these counts into one number, and penalizes output that is too short [@papineni2002bleu, p. 315]. A word cannot be credited more often than it appears in a reference, so seven copies of "the" against "The cat is on the mat" score 2/7, not 7/7 [@papineni2002bleu, p. 312]. Papers report BLEU on a scale of 0 to 100. Even a human translator does not score 100, because good translations differ [@papineni2002bleu, p. 315], and BLEU's own users warned that it "does not fully capture the quality of a translation" [@wu2016google, p. 14].

Phrase-based translation worked well enough to become a product. When Google announced its neural system in 2016, it recalled launching Google Translate "ten years ago" with "Phrase-Based Machine Translation as the key algorithm behind this service" [@le2016neural]. Neural networks entered these systems first as parts. As Bahdanau and colleagues put it in 2014, "the role of neural networks has been largely limited to simply providing a single feature to an existing statistical machine translation system or to re-rank a list of candidate translations provided by an existing system" [@bahdanau2015neural, p. 9]. Re-ranking means that the old system proposes a list of candidate translations, its **n-best list**, and the network picks among them.

## One vector for the whole sentence

The bolder idea was to let one network do the whole job: read the source sentence into a vector, then write the translation from that vector. The network that reads is the **encoder**, and the network that writes is the **decoder**.

The idea was not new. Koehn, in a later textbook chapter, notes that models from 1997 "are striking similar to the current dominant neural machine translation approaches", but "none of these models were trained on data sizes large enough to produce reasonable results for anything but toy examples", and the idea "was abandoned for almost two decades" [@koehn2017neural, p. 5]. In 2013, Nal Kalchbrenner and Phil Blunsom built a translation model "purely based on continuous representations for words, phrases and sentences", with a convolutional network reading the source and a recurrent language model writing the target [@kalchbrenner2013recurrent, p. 1700]. They used it to re-rank candidate translations rather than to translate on its own [@kalchbrenner2013recurrent, p. 1700].

In June 2014, Kyunghyun Cho and colleagues, with Yoshua Bengio, proposed the "RNN Encoder-Decoder", which "consists of two recurrent neural networks (RNN). One RNN encodes a sequence of symbols into a fixed-length vector representation, and the other decodes the representation into another sequence of symbols" [@cho2014learning, p. 1724]. After the encoder reads the last word, its hidden state "is a summary c of the whole input sequence" [@cho2014learning, p. 1725]. Like Kalchbrenner and Blunsom, they used the network as one more score inside a phrase-based system: it rated the phrase pairs in the phrase table [@cho2014learning, p. 1727]. That raised BLEU on a standard English-French test from 33.30 to 33.87 [@cho2014learning, p. 1729].

The same paper introduced a simpler alternative to the LSTM, a unit "motivated by the LSTM unit but ... much simpler to compute and implement" [@cho2014learning, p. 1726]. It has two gates instead of the LSTM's three and no separate memory cell. An **update gate** decides how much of the old hidden state to keep, and a **reset gate** lets the unit ignore the old state and start again from the current input [@cho2014learning, p. 1726]. Its name came a few months later from Junyoung Chung and colleagues: the **gated recurrent unit**, or GRU [@chung2014empirical, p. 2]. Comparing the two on music and speech data, they found the GRU "comparable to LSTM" and "could not make concrete conclusion on which of the two gating units was better" [@chung2014empirical, pp. 1, 7].

In September 2014, Ilya Sutskever, Oriol Vinyals, and Quoc Le went all the way. Their method "uses a multilayered Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) to map the input sequence to a vector of a fixed dimensionality, and then another deep LSTM to decode the target sequence from the vector" [@sutskever2014sequence, p. 1]. They called it sequence to sequence learning. The decoder, they noted, "is essentially a recurrent neural network language model ... except that it is conditioned on the input sequence" [@sutskever2014sequence, p. 2]: a language model, like Mikolov's, that is told what to talk about. To know when to stop, every sentence ended with a special end-of-sentence symbol, "<EOS>" [@sutskever2014sequence, p. 3]. The network translated on its own, writing one word at a time. Rather than keep only the single most probable next word at each step, it kept the few best partial translations, a procedure called **beam search**; a beam of two already gave "most of the benefits" [@sutskever2014sequence, p. 4].

The result was a first. An **ensemble** of five such networks, their predictions combined, scored 34.81 BLEU on the English-French test, against 33.30 for a phrase-based system on the same data. In the authors' words, "it is the first time that a pure neural translation system outperforms a phrase-based SMT baseline on a large MT task by a sizeable margin" [@sutskever2014sequence, pp. 2, 5-6]. It did not beat the best system of the day, which scored 37.0; used to re-rank the phrase-based system's candidates, the networks reached 36.5 [@sutskever2014sequence, p. 6].

The models were large for 2014. Each had four layers of 1,000 units in both the encoder and the decoder, and 380 million parameters, of which "64M are pure recurrent connections" [@sutskever2014sequence, p. 5]. <!-- D-009: code/checks/seq2seq.py, Example 3 --> The paper does not say where the rest are, but a rough reconstruction shows it: the input vocabulary of 160,000 words and the output vocabulary of 80,000 [@sutskever2014sequence, p. 4], at 1,000 numbers per word in the input tables and the output layer, account for 320 million. About 83% of the model was word tables, a cost that returns in [the chapter on tokens](#tokens). Training used eight GPUs, one per layer and four more for the output softmax, and "took about a ten days" [@sutskever2014sequence, p. 5].

One trick mattered more than its simplicity suggests. They fed the source sentence in reverse. "The simple trick of reversing the words in the source sentence is one of the key technical contributions of this work" [@sutskever2014sequence, p. 2]: BLEU rose from 25.9 to 30.6 [@sutskever2014sequence, p. 4]. The authors did "not have a complete explanation", but believed it helped by creating short links between input and output [@sutskever2014sequence, p. 4]. <!-- D-009: code/checks/seq2seq.py, Example 4 --> Lay out a three-word source, the end symbol, and a three-word translation in one line. In normal order, each source word is four steps from its translation. Reversed, the distances are 2, 4, and 6: the average is unchanged, but the first words of the translation are now close to their sources, as the paper says [@sutskever2014sequence, p. 4]. It is the vanishing gradient of [the structure chapter](#structure) again: short paths make learning easier.

Sutskever and colleagues also looked inside. A PCA of the encoder's final vectors for a set of short phrases, the method the reader knows, showed that "the phrases are clustered by meaning, which in these examples is primarily a function of word order, which would be difficult to capture with a bag-of-words model" [@sutskever2014sequence, p. 6]. "Mary admires John" and "John admires Mary" ended up in different places.

Credit for the encoder-decoder is shared. The deep learning textbook of Goodfellow, Bengio, and Courville says the architecture "was first proposed by Cho et al. (2014a) and shortly after by Sutskever et al. (2014), who independently developed that architecture and were the first to obtain state-of-the-art translation using this approach" [@goodfellow2016deep, pp. 390-391]. Sutskever and colleagues called Kalchbrenner and Blunsom "the first to map the input sentence into a vector and then back to a sentence" [@sutskever2014sequence, p. 8]. And the 1990s attempts came first. A fair summary: the idea was old; Kalchbrenner and Blunsom, then Cho and colleagues, built neural versions inside existing systems; and Sutskever and colleagues were first to make a network alone translate better than a phrase-based baseline.

## The bottleneck

Everything the decoder knows about the source must pass through one vector of fixed size, whether the sentence has five words or fifty. Did that hurt?

Cho and colleagues tested it directly in a second 2014 paper. Their encoder-decoder "performs relatively well on short sentences without unknown words, but its performance degrades rapidly as the length of the sentence and the number of unknown words increase" [@cho2014properties, p. 103]. They offered an explanation, carefully labeled as "the most obvious explanatory hypothesis": "the fixed-length vector representation does not have enough capacity to encode a long sentence with complicated structure and meaning" [@cho2014properties, p. 107]. The phrase-based system showed the opposite trend, scoring higher on longer sentences [@cho2014properties, p. 107]. The neural models had one advantage: they needed "only 500MB of memory in total", while phrase-based systems "often require tens of gigabytes of memory" [@cho2014properties, p. 103].

Sutskever and colleagues, by contrast, reported that their LSTMs "did not suffer on very long sentences": "There is no degradation on sentences with less than 35 words, there is only a minor degradation on the longest sentences" [@sutskever2014sequence, pp. 2, 7]. They had expected otherwise: "We were initially convinced that the LSTM would fail on long sentences due to its limited memory" [@sutskever2014sequence, p. 8]. The two findings are less contradictory than they look. Cho's models had one layer, a 30,000-word vocabulary, and about 110 hours of training [@cho2014properties, p. 106]; Sutskever's were four-layer ensembles with much larger vocabularies, trained for ten days on eight GPUs, with the source reversed. Whether one vector was enough depended on how much model stood behind it.

## Attention: looking back at the source

Dzmitry Bahdanau, Kyunghyun Cho, and Yoshua Bengio posted a different answer in September 2014, nine days before Sutskever and colleagues posted theirs. "We conjecture that the use of a fixed-length vector is a bottleneck in improving the performance of this basic encoder-decoder architecture, and propose to extend this by allowing a model to automatically (soft-)search for parts of a source sentence that are relevant to predicting a target word" [@bahdanau2015neural, p. 1].

Their model "does not attempt to encode a whole input sentence into a single fixed-length vector. Instead, it encodes the input sentence into a sequence of vectors and chooses a subset of these vectors adaptively while decoding the translation" [@bahdanau2015neural, p. 2]. The encoder keeps one vector for every source word. Its encoder was **bidirectional**: one recurrent network read the sentence left to right, another right to left, and each word's vector joined the two, so that it summarized "both the preceding words and the following words" [@bahdanau2015neural, p. 4].

![(a) In an encoder-decoder, the encoder's last state is the only thing passed to the decoder. (b) With attention, the encoder keeps a vector for every source word, and before writing each word the decoder takes a weighted average of all of them. The line thicknesses are the weights from the worked example in the text, for the decoder about to write "zone".](../../figures/seq2seq-bottleneck.svg){#fig:seq2seq-bottleneck}

Before writing each word of the translation, the decoder does three things.

1. **Score** each source word: how well does it match what the decoder is about to write? Bahdanau and colleagues computed the score with a small neural network that looks at the decoder's current state and the source word's vector [@bahdanau2015neural, pp. 3, 12].
2. **Turn the scores into weights** with a softmax, the same function that ends [the destination chapter](#destination)'s pipeline, so that the weights are positive and add up to one [@bahdanau2015neural, p. 3].
3. **Average** the source vectors using those weights. The result, a "context vector", is a weighted sum of the source word vectors, and it is fed to the decoder along with its own state [@bahdanau2015neural, p. 3].

<!-- D-009: code/checks/seq2seq.py, Example 5 -->

A small example, with numbers made up for the purpose. Take the English words "the European Economic Area", each with a vector of four numbers, and score them with a plain dot product, a simpler choice used by later work [@luong2015effective, p. 1414]. Suppose the decoder, about to write the French "zone", has a state whose dot products with the four source vectors are 0.20, 0.39, 0.39, and 3.06. The softmax turns these into weights of 0.05, 0.06, 0.06, and 0.84: most of the attention goes to "Area", and the four weights sum to one. One step later, about to write "économique", the decoder's state has changed, the scores are 0.20, 1.20, 3.09, and 1.10, and the weights become 0.04, 0.11, 0.74, and 0.10, with most of the weight on "Economic". The same source vectors, read with different weights at each step.

Bahdanau and colleagues described the weights as a **soft alignment**. Phrase-based systems aligned each word with a definite partner, chosen before translation. Here, "the alignment model directly computes a soft alignment, which allows the gradient of the cost function to be backpropagated through" [@bahdanau2015neural, p. 4]: because every step is a smooth function, the whole thing is trained by backpropagation with the rest of the network. Soft alignment also helps with words whose translation depends on their neighbors. Translating "the man" into French, "one must consider the word following [the] to determine whether it should be translated into [le], [la], [les] or [l']", and the model can "look at both [the] and [man]" [@bahdanau2015neural, p. 7]. Only once, as an intuition, did they use the word that stuck: "this implements a mechanism of attention in the decoder. The decoder decides parts of the source sentence to pay attention to" [@bahdanau2015neural, p. 4].

The weights here are a softmax of made-up scores, not the output of a trained model. They mimic what the paper reports for its trained model: mostly along the diagonal, with the order reversed for "European Economic Area" and "zone économique européenne".](../../figures/seq2seq-attention-heatmap.svg){#fig:seq2seq-attention-heatmap}

Because the weights are numbers, they can be drawn. Bahdanau and colleagues printed the weights of their trained model as grids, one row per French word and one column per English word [@bahdanau2015neural, p. 6]. The alignment was "largely monotonic", with "strong weights along the diagonal", but with exceptions where French and English order differ: the model "was able to correctly align [zone] with [Area], jumping over the two words ([European] and [Economic]), and then looked one word back at a time to complete the whole phrase [zone économique européenne]" [@bahdanau2015neural, p. 7]. The figure above is a constructed version of that pattern.

The bottleneck is gone. In Bahdanau and colleagues' model, each source word's vector has 2,000 numbers, 1,000 from each direction [@bahdanau2015neural, p. 5]. A 10-word sentence leaves the decoder 20,000 numbers to look at, a 50-word sentence 100,000, where an encoder-decoder of the same size passes on one state of 1,000 numbers however long the sentence. Their plot of BLEU against sentence length showed the basic encoder-decoder's score falling "dramatically" as sentences grew longer, while the attention model "shows no performance deterioration even with sentences of length 50 or more" [@bahdanau2015neural, p. 6].

The results were striking but not yet a victory. On the whole test set, the attention model scored 26.75 BLEU, or 28.45 when trained longer, against 33.30 for the phrase-based system. On sentences with no unknown words, the longer-trained model scored 36.15 against 35.63, a match for the older system [@bahdanau2015neural, p. 7]. The gap was the vocabulary: with only the 30,000 most frequent words in each language [@bahdanau2015neural, p. 5], every rarer word became an "unknown" symbol, and the authors named "to better handle unknown, or rare words" as a challenge for the future [@bahdanau2015neural, p. 9]. That problem is the subject of [the chapter on tokens](#tokens).

Attention had a predecessor. In 2013, Alex Graves built a network that generated handwriting from text, using a "'soft window'" over the characters that "learns to decide which character to write next" [@graves2013generating, pp. 24, 26]. Sutskever and colleagues described it as "a novel differentiable attention mechanism", of which Bahdanau and colleagues applied "an elegant variant" to translation [@sutskever2014sequence, p. 2]. Bahdanau and colleagues acknowledged it and drew the difference: Graves's window could only move forward through the text, which in translation "is a severe limitation, as (long-distance) reordering is often needed" [@bahdanau2015neural, p. 8].

Attention had a cost, which the authors noted. Their approach "requires computing the annotation weight of every word in the source sentence for each word in the translation" [@bahdanau2015neural, p. 8], their word "annotation" meaning the encoder's vector for a source word. <!-- D-009: code/checks/seq2seq.py, Example 8 --> For a 30-word sentence and a 30-word translation, that is 900 scores; for 50 and 50, 2,500. The number grows with the product of the two lengths. For translation, where most sentences are "only 15-40 words", this was "not severe" [@bahdanau2015neural, p. 8]. For the much longer inputs of today's LLMs, it becomes a central concern, as [the chapter on attention](#attention) shows.

## Replaced in about two years

Attention spread quickly. In 2015, Minh-Thang Luong and colleagues tested several ways of computing the scores, including the plain dot product used above, and set a new best result on English-to-German translation [@luong2015effective, pp. 1412, 1414]. At the 2016 Conference on Machine Translation, the field's yearly shared evaluation (known as WMT), the University of Edinburgh's systems were "attentional encoder-decoder networks" [@sennrich2016edinburgh, p. 371]. In the official human ranking, a pure neural system was in the top group, alone or tied, for 8 of the 12 translation directions; for the other 4, including English to Russian and both directions between English and Turkish, older systems still led [@bojar2016findings, p. 141].

Koehn, first author of the 2003 phrase-based paper, summarized the speed of the change: "Within a year or two, the entire research field of machine translation went neural." At the 2015 shared task, "only one pure neural machine translation system was submitted ... It was competitive, but outperformed by traditional statistical systems. A year later, in 2016, a neural machine translation system won in almost all language pairs. In 2017, almost all submissions were neural machine translation systems" [@koehn2017neural, p. 6].

Industry followed. In September 2016, Google described its Neural Machine Translation system, GNMT: "a deep LSTM network with 8 encoder and 8 decoder layers using residual connections as well as attention connections from the decoder network to the encoder" [@wu2016google, p. 1]. It was the 2014 recipe, scaled up with the residual connections of [the chapter on deep networks](#depth) and with words split into "a limited set of common sub-word units ("wordpieces")" to handle rare words [@wu2016google, p. 1]. Training a basic English-French model took "around 6 days ... using 96 NVIDIA K80 GPUs" [@wu2016google, p. 15]. Announcing the paper, two of its authors wrote that Google Translate was using it "for 100% of machine translations from Chinese to English", about 18 million translations a day, as of 27 September 2016 [@le2016neural].

The headline claim was that GNMT "reduces translation errors by an average of 60%" [@wu2016google, p. 1]. What was measured is worth knowing. Human raters scored translations of 500 isolated sentences per language pair on a scale from 0 to 6, comparing the old phrase-based system, GNMT, and human translators [@wu2016google, pp. 14, 18-19]. <!-- D-009: code/checks/seq2seq.py, Example 7 --> The paper does not define its "relative improvement", but each printed value matches, to within a point, the share of the gap between the old system and the human translators that GNMT closed. For English to Spanish, the ratings were 4.885 for the old system, 5.428 for GNMT, and 5.504 for humans, so GNMT closed 87.7% of the gap; across six language pairs the printed values ranged from 58% to 87% [@wu2016google, p. 19]. It is a share of a rating gap, not a count of errors. The authors were clear about the limits: the test used "relatively simplistic and isolated sentences", and the announcement noted that GNMT "can still make significant errors that a human translator would never make, like dropping words and mistranslating proper names or rare terms" [@wu2016google, p. 19; @le2016neural].

## Why recurrence was slow

One problem remained, and it shaped every design in this chapter. A recurrent network must finish word 1 before it can start word 2, because each step needs the previous hidden state. In the words of the paper that would replace it, "This inherently sequential nature precludes parallelization within training examples, which becomes critical at longer sequence lengths" [@vaswani2017attention, p. 2]. A GPU does thousands of things at once, but it cannot do step 2 before step 1.

<!-- D-009: code/checks/seq2seq.py, Example 8 -->

Engineers worked around it. They trained on many sentences at a time, and they put different layers on different GPUs, so that layer 2 could start on a word as soon as layer 1 had finished it: Sutskever and colleagues ran one layer per GPU [@sutskever2014sequence, p. 5]. For a 30-word sentence and four layers, that turns 120 steps done one after another into 33. GNMT's authors chose their architecture for parallelism: only the first encoder layer read in both directions, because more bidirectional layers "would reduce parallelism", and attention connected only the bottom decoder layer to the top encoder layer, since connecting the top layers "would have removed all parallelism in the decoder network" [@wu2016google, pp. 6-7]. Still, within one layer, 30 words meant 30 steps.

Writing the output is word by word for every model, including the transformers that came next: each new word depends on the ones already written. The difference lies in training, where the whole target sentence is known in advance. By 2017, recurrent networks with LSTM or GRU units had been "firmly established as state of the art approaches in sequence modeling and transduction problems such as language modeling and machine translation", and "in all but a few cases ... such attention mechanisms are used in conjunction with a recurrent network" [@vaswani2017attention, p. 2]. The next question was whether the recurrence was needed at all, and [the chapter on attention](#attention) takes it up.

## What to carry forward

- Recurrent networks became strong language models around 2010, beating smoothed n-grams on the same data, but they were slow to train and their memory was shorter than hoped.
- Statistical machine translation in the 2000s counted phrase pairs in parallel text and combined many scores with learned weights. BLEU, an overlap count against human translations, became the standard score.
- An encoder-decoder (sequence to sequence) network reads a sentence into a vector and writes the output from it. In 2014 it first beat a phrase-based baseline, with large LSTMs and the source reversed.
- The fixed vector is a bottleneck. Small models degraded on long sentences; large ones less so.
- Attention keeps a vector per source word and, before each output word, takes a weighted average of them, with weights from a softmax over learned match scores. It is trained by backpropagation like everything else, and its weights can be drawn as an alignment.
- Within about two years (2014-2016), attentional encoder-decoders replaced phrase-based systems in research and began to replace them in products.
- Recurrence forces one step after another within a sentence, which limits how much a GPU can do at once. That is the problem the transformer set out to solve.

[The destination chapter](#destination) promised this chapter a limit: a model's parameters contain nothing from after the text it was trained on. A second limit is just as basic: a model produces text and nothing else. In March 2023, announcing plugins for ChatGPT, OpenAI named both: what a language model knows comes from its training data alone, may be out of date, and is the same for every use; and without help, all it can do is produce text [@openai2023chatgptplugins].

The two answers in this chapter use one channel, the context window. Retrieval, as [the chapter on adapting](#adapting) described it, finds text the model was never trained on and places it in the window, where the model reads it like any other input. **Tools** let the program around the model do things, such as search the web or run a calculation, when the model's output asks; the result comes back as more text. Neither changes the weights. The chapter builds retrieval from the keyword search of the 2000s, through learned vectors, to retrieval-augmented generation; then it turns to tools, the loop that makes a model into an agent, a shared standard for connecting tools, and the ways all of this goes wrong.

## What the weights cannot know

Large language models are static. Each was pretrained once, at one point in time, so it cannot discuss fast-changing events, such as something that happened last week, and it cannot be asked about private data that was never in its training text, such as a company's documents [@jurafsky2026ir, p. 2]. In 2020, the paper that named retrieval-augmented generation, met later in this chapter, described a pretrained model's knowledge as a knowledge base held implicitly in its parameters, one whose memory the model cannot easily extend or correct [@lewis2020retrieval, p. 1].

The date after which a model has seen little or no text is its knowledge cutoff ([the chapter on prompting](#prompting)), and published cutoffs lag behind releases. OpenAI's GPT-4 report of 2023 said the model generally lacked knowledge of events after September 2021, when the vast majority of its pretraining data ends [@openai2023gpt4, p. 10]. Meta's card for Llama 3.1 gives a cutoff of December 2023 and a release date of 23 July 2024 [@meta2024llama31card]. <!-- D-009: code/checks/tools.py, Example 10 --> That is about seven months before anyone could use it, and people go on using a model for months or years.

The cutoff is not a sharp edge. The GPT-4 report's wording is "the vast majority" of the data, and the report adds that the training data include a small amount of more recent data [@openai2023gpt4, p. 10]. As of 2026-11, Anthropic lists two dates for each current model, a "reliable knowledge cutoff", "the date through which the model's knowledge is most extensive and reliable", and a broader "training data cutoff", both June 2026 [@anthropic2026models]; OpenAI lists a single "knowledge cutoff" per model, between April and June 2026 for the four models on its page [@openai2026models]. A 2024 study of open-weight models whose training data is public or described found that the **effective cutoff**, the date a model's knowledge actually reflects, is not the same as the reported cutoff and often differs from it drastically, because new web crawls contain much old text and the removal of duplicates is imperfect [@cheng2024dated, pp. 1, 5]. Knowledge thins out toward the cutoff rather than stopping there.

[The chapter on prompting](#prompting) explained that no prompt adds facts the weights lack, and [the chapter on adapting](#adapting) found fine-tuning a poor way to add them. What works is to put the facts into the input, which needs a way to find them. The second limit needs no study. As of 2026-11, Anthropic's documentation names two jobs that call for tools: fetching fresh or outside data, since anything beyond the training data or particular to one system has to be fetched by a tool, and taking actions with side effects, which a model can put into words but only a tool can carry out [@anthropic2026howtooluse].

## Finding text: retrieval before neural networks

**Information retrieval** (IR) is the field concerned with finding media of every kind to meet users' information needs. In its basic task, a user poses a **query** (here a search request, not the attention vector of [the attention chapter](#attention)) and the system returns a ranked list of documents from a collection, where a **document** is "whatever unit of text the system indexes and retrieves" [@jurafsky2026ir, p. 3]. A document search built in the 2000s did this: turned each document and the query into vectors of word counts and ranked by cosine, as in [the embeddings chapter](#embeddings).

A small example shows where raw counts go wrong. OpenAI's retrieval guide (as of 2026-11) uses the query "When did we go to the moon?" and three sentences: "The first lunar landing occurred in July of 1969.", "The first man on the moon was Neil Armstrong.", and "When I ate the moon cake, it was delicious." [@openai2026retrieval]. Call them D1, D2, and D3. <!-- D-009: code/checks/tools.py, Example 1 --> Counting lowercased words and comparing by cosine gives D1 0.126, D2 0.342, D3 0.378. The moon cake wins: it shares "when", "the", and "moon" with the question. The lunar landing shares only "the".

The first repair is to count rare words for more. In 1972, Karen Spärck Jones argued that a term should be weighted by how often it occurs in the collection, so that a match on a rarer, more specific term counts for more than a match on a common one, though common ones still count [@sparckjones1972statistical, pp. 1, 6]. <!-- D-009: code/checks/tools.py, Example 2 --> In her example, in a collection of 200 documents, a term that occurs 90 times gets weight 2 and one that occurs three times gets weight 7 [@sparckjones1972statistical, p. 6]: a base-2 logarithm in whole steps. The idea became the **inverse document frequency** (idf) [@jurafsky2026ir, p. 6]. Combined with a word's **term frequency** (tf) in a document, it gives **tf-idf**, in a current textbook's standard form [@jurafsky2026ir, pp. 5-6]:

$$\text{tf-idf}(t, d) = \text{tf}_{t,d} \times \log_{10} \frac{N}{\text{df}_t}$$

where $t$ is a word, $d$ a document, $N$ the number of documents in the collection, and $\text{df}_t$ the number of documents that contain $t$. The term frequency is damped, $\text{tf}_{t,d} = 1 + \log_{10}$ of the count of $t$ in $d$ (0 if $t$ is absent), because 100 occurrences of a word in a document do not make that word 100 times as likely to be relevant [@jurafsky2026ir, p. 6]. A word found in every document gets weight 0.

<!-- D-009: code/checks/tools.py, Example 1 --> On the three sentences, "the" occurs in all three and gets weight 0; "moon" occurs in two (0.176); "when" in one (0.477). The tf-idf cosines are D1 0.000, D2 0.061, D3 0.426. Weighting made things worse: the lunar landing now shares no weighted word with the question, and the cake's "when" counts heavily. Nothing in the counts says that "lunar" is about the moon.

On real collections, the standard keyword method is **BM25**. A 2009 survey called it the best-known form of a model developed in stages over about 30 years [@robertson2009probabilistic, p. 334]; its original form, a little more complicated than the one below, was published in 1994 [@robertson2009probabilistic, pp. 361, 388]. BM25 adds two ideas to tf-idf. **Saturation** (of a word's count, not the saturated units of [the deep-turn chapter](#deep-turn)): however often a word occurs, its contribution to a document's score cannot rise past a limit, the saturation point [@robertson2009probabilistic, p. 356]. **Length normalization**: long documents contain more of every word, partly because some authors use more words than others to say the same thing [@robertson2009probabilistic, p. 359], so their counts are discounted. In its standard form, the weight of query word $i$ in a document is [@robertson2009probabilistic, p. 360]

$$w_i = \frac{tf}{k_1 \left( (1 - b) + b \, \frac{dl}{avdl} \right) + tf} \times w_i^{\text{RSJ}}$$

where $tf$ is how often the word occurs in the document, $dl$ the document's length and $avdl$ the average length in the collection, $k_1$ and $b$ two settings chosen by the user, and $w_i^{\text{RSJ}}$ a version of the inverse document frequency [@robertson2009probabilistic, p. 349]. A document's score is the sum of these weights over the query's words.

![How much a word's count adds to a document's score, computed for this chapter. (a) A raw count grows without limit; the logarithm used in tf-idf grows slowly, with no ceiling; BM25's term-frequency part (k1 = 1.2, average document length) approaches 1. (b) BM25 with b = 0.75: the same three occurrences count for more in a short document than in a long one.](../../figures/tools-bm25.svg){#fig:tools-bm25}

<!-- D-009: code/checks/tools.py, Example 3 --> The figure uses $b = 0.75$, inside the range $0.5 < b < 0.8$, and $k_1 = 1.2$, at the lower edge of $1.2 < k_1 < 2$; experiments suggest that values in these ranges generally work reasonably well in many situations [@robertson2009probabilistic, pp. 360-361], and a 2026 textbook passes on a 2008 suggestion of exactly $b = 0.75$ [@jurafsky2026ir, p. 9]. In a document of average length, a word that appears once contributes 0.455 times its idf; three times, 0.714; ten times, 0.893; a hundred times, 0.988. The ceiling is 1, where tf-idf's logarithm would give 3 and a raw count 100. Three occurrences count 0.800 in a document half the average length and 0.588 in one twice the average. The theory does not say how to choose the settings [@robertson2009probabilistic, p. 360], and one 2020 team tuned them to $b = 0.4$ and $k_1 = 0.9$ [@karpukhin2020dense, p. 6773].

Keyword search is fast because of the **inverted index**, which lists, for each query term, the documents that contain it [@jurafsky2026ir, p. 9]; a document with none of the query's words is never touched. Since each document's vector has an entry for every word in the vocabulary, almost all zero, this is called **sparse retrieval** [@jurafsky2026ir, p. 3]. As late as 2020, tf-idf and BM25 were the usual way to find passages that answer questions [@karpukhin2020dense, p. 6769].

## Learned vectors for retrieval

The weakness of keywords is the one [the embeddings chapter](#embeddings) took up with latent semantic analysis: different words for the same thing. A 2020 paper on retrieval for question answering gave an example. "Who is the bad guy in lord of the rings?" is answered by "Sala Baker is best known for portraying the villain Sauron in the Lord of the Rings trilogy"; a keyword system would have trouble finding that sentence, and a dense one would have a better chance of pairing "bad guy" with "villain" [@karpukhin2020dense, p. 6769]. Latent semantic analysis had mixed results in 1990, as that chapter reported. The dense retrieval below answers the same problem.

Word vectors are the obvious first try. <!-- D-009: code/checks/tools.py, Example 1 --> Represent each sentence by the average of its GloVe vectors and compare by cosine: D1 0.790, D2 0.896, D3 0.902. The cake still wins, because small words like "the" and "when" dominate the averages. Drop 16 such words (a **stop list**, in IR's term: high-frequency words, often function words such as "the", "a", and "to" [@jurafsky2026ir, p. 9]), and only "go" and "moon" are left of the question. Now D2 scores 0.795, D1 0.651, D3 0.621: both moon landings beat the cake. The vectors know that "moon" is closer to "lunar" (cosine 0.683) than to "cake" (0.315). But the result rests on a hand-picked word list, and by 2019 averaged GloVe vectors were a baseline that sentence-level methods were compared against [@reimers2019sentence, p. 3982]. OpenAI's guide reports scores from its trained embedding model of 65%, 43%, and 28% for the three sentences: the lunar landing first, though it shares no word with the question except "the" [@openai2026retrieval]. Those are OpenAI's numbers, from a model this book cannot run.

The trained retriever came in 2020, with **dense passage retrieval** (DPR). It used two BERT models from [the pretraining chapter](#pretraining), one to turn a question into a vector and one to turn a passage into a vector, 768 numbers each, and scored a passage by the dot product of the two [@karpukhin2020dense, p. 6771]. Because the question never meets a passage inside a network, all the passage vectors can be computed once, in advance. Training pushed the dot product up for each question and its correct passage, and down for the other passages in the same batch [@karpukhin2020dense, p. 6770]. The collection was English Wikipedia of 20 December 2018, cut into 21,015,324 passages of 100 words, each with its article's title in front [@karpukhin2020dense, p. 6772].

The measure was **top-20 accuracy**: the share of questions for which at least one of the 20 highest-scoring passages contains the answer [@karpukhin2020dense, p. 6773]. <!-- D-009: code/checks/tools.py, Example 6 -->

| Data set (as named in the paper) | BM25 | DPR | Difference |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Natural Questions | 59.1 | 78.4 | +19.3 |
| TriviaQA | 66.9 | 79.4 | +12.5 |
| WQ | 55.0 | 73.2 | +18.2 |
| TREC | 70.9 | 79.8 | +8.9 |
| SQuAD | 68.8 | 63.2 | -5.6 |

The abstract's "9%-19% absolute" gain [@karpukhin2020dense, p. 6769] covers the first four. On the fifth, BM25 won, and the authors said why: SQuAD's annotators had written their questions after seeing the passage, so questions and passages share many words [@karpukhin2020dense, p. 6773]. The two methods fail in opposite ways. Keywords cannot capture differences of wording or relations of meaning well, while dense vectors may not have enough capacity to represent important phrases that appear rarely [@karpukhin2020dense, p. 6780]; in Anthropic's 2024 example, a search for the error code "TS-999" might find passages about error codes in general and miss the one that matches "TS-999" exactly [@anthropic2024contextual]. Learned vectors find paraphrases that keywords miss; keywords find exact names and codes that vectors blur. Many systems use both [@anthropic2024contextual]; DPR's authors already tried adding the two scores, which helped on some data sets and not on others [@karpukhin2020dense, p. 6773]. Combining the two rankings is called **hybrid search**.

Why two separate encoders? One BERT reading question and passage together, a **cross-encoder**, is more accurate, but nothing can be computed in advance. <!-- D-009: code/checks/tools.py, Example 8 --> A 2019 paper worked out that finding the most similar pair among 10,000 sentences that way takes 49,995,000 passes through BERT, about 65 hours on a GPU of the time [@reimers2019sentence, p. 3982]. Separate encoders, a **bi-encoder**, need 10,000 passes and a table of cosines, about 5 seconds [@reimers2019sentence, p. 3983], at some cost in accuracy [@jurafsky2026ir, p. 14]. Some systems use both: a cheap first pass, keywords or a vector search, keeps the top few candidates, and a cross-encoder, the **reranker**, reorders only those [@jurafsky2026ir, p. 14].

The 2019 paper's Sentence-BERT also gave the tool its current form: a network trained so that whole sentences come out as vectors that can be compared by cosine, with similar sentences close together [@reimers2019sentence, p. 3982]. That is what an **embedding model** means today: text in, one vector out, trained so that closeness means relevance. Embedding models are compared on benchmarks such as MTEB (2022), whose authors found that no text embedding method was best on every task [@muennighoff2023mteb, p. 2014]; [the chapter on measuring models](#evaluation) explains how to read such a leaderboard, a public ranking of methods by score. As of 2026-11, Anthropic does not offer an embedding model of its own and points to another company's, most of whose current models produce 1,024 numbers per text by default, scaled to length 1 so that cosine similarity and dot-product similarity give the same result [@anthropic2026embeddings]; OpenAI's produce 1,536 or 3,072 [@openai2026embeddings].

## Searching millions of vectors

Finding the passages whose vectors are closest to the question's is **nearest neighbor search**, the k-nearest-neighbors classifier without the vote. <!-- D-009: code/checks/tools.py, Example 5 --> Comparing a question with every passage in DPR's index would take 21,015,324 times 768, about 16.1 billion multiplications and additions. So systems use **approximate nearest neighbor** search [@jurafsky2026ir, p. 16], which finds most of the nearest vectors quickly and may miss a few. One such method, used by the RAG system below, is **HNSW** (hierarchical navigable small world graphs, 2016, published in a journal in 2020), which builds up, as vectors are added, a multi-layer structure of graphs, with few vectors in the top layers and all of them at the bottom; starting the search at the top lets its cost scale logarithmically [@malkov2020efficient, p. 1]. An analogy, not from the paper: motorways to reach the right region, then smaller roads, then the street. DPR and RAG both used **FAISS**, an open-source library for searching and clustering dense vectors by similarity [@karpukhin2020dense, p. 6771], first described in 2017; its authors built a graph connecting a billion vectors in under 12 hours on four Maxwell Titan X GPUs [@johnson2021billion, p. 1]. Products that store vectors and search them this way are sold as a **vector database** or **vector store**.

<!-- D-009: code/checks/tools.py, Example 5 --> The memory is vectors times numbers per vector times bytes per number. DPR's index of about 16.1 billion numbers takes 64.56 GB in 32-bit numbers, 32.28 GB in 16-bit ones, 2.02 GB at one bit each. The 16-bit index of 2018 Wikipedia is twice the 16.1 GB of Llama 3 8B's weights in [the chapter on generation](#generation). A company's million passages at 1,024 numbers each take 4.10 GB in 32-bit numbers and 1.02 GB in 8-bit ones.

## Retrieval-augmented generation

The idea of letting a model look things up in a text collection had several near-simultaneous versions around 2019-2020. One mixed a language model's next-word probabilities with those of a k-nearest-neighbors search over earlier text [@khandelwal2020generalization, p. 1]. In February 2020, REALM put a retriever inside pretraining. It filled in masked words, as BERT did, after retrieving passages from Wikipedia, and was trained by backpropagating through a retrieval step over millions of documents [@guu2020realm, p. 1], on the principle that a retrieval should earn a reward when it lowers the language model's perplexity and a penalty when it tells the model nothing useful [@guu2020realm, p. 2]. REALM picked its answer out of the retrieved text; it did not write one [@lewis2020retrieval, p. 1].

The name **retrieval-augmented generation** (RAG) comes from a paper posted in May 2020 by Patrick Lewis and colleagues, which combined a learned retriever with a model that writes its answer [@lewis2020retrieval, p. 1]. Which of these systems counts as the first depends on what is counted; [the credit appendix](#credit-tools) sets out the candidates. The paper's terms come from statistics. The model's weights are its **parametric memory**; the index of passages, kept and searched like the data of a nearest-neighbor method, is its **non-parametric memory** [@lewis2020retrieval, p. 3], where knowledge can be corrected or added to directly, and where a person can look at what was retrieved and make sense of it [@lewis2020retrieval, p. 1]. The retriever was DPR's, over the same 21 million passages; the writer was BART, a 400-million-parameter encoder-decoder transformer, which read the question and a retrieved passage simply placed one after the other [@lewis2020retrieval, pp. 3-4]. The question encoder and the writer were fine-tuned together; the passage vectors were left as they were [@lewis2020retrieval, pp. 4, 18].

The authors contrasted two kinds of model: **closed-book** models answer from their parameters alone, **open-book** ones look things up [@lewis2020retrieval, p. 5]. On Natural Questions, a closed-book T5 with 11 billion parameters scored 34.5, REALM 40.4, and RAG 44.5 [@lewis2020retrieval, p. 6]. From reading outputs, the authors judged that RAG models "hallucinate less" than BART alone [@lewis2020retrieval, p. 6]: less, not never. One printed example shows the model using its passages. Asked to write a quiz-show clue whose answer is "Hemingway", it retrieved five passages, including one about *A Farewell to Arms* and one about *The Sun Also Rises*, and wrote: "The Sun Also Rises" is a novel by this author of "A Farewell to Arms". It leaned on the passage about *The Sun Also Rises* while writing that title, and on the other while writing *A Farewell to Arms* [@lewis2020retrieval, p. 7].

The paper's most useful experiment concerns the cutoff. The authors built a second index from Wikipedia of December 2016 and asked "Who is {position}?" about 82 world leaders who had changed between 2016 and 2018 [@lewis2020retrieval, pp. 7-8]. <!-- D-009: code/checks/tools.py, Example 7 --> With the 2016 index, RAG named 70% of the 2016 leaders; with the 2018 index, 68% of the 2018 leaders (about 57 and 56 of 82, counts inferred from the percentages). With the indexes swapped, 12% and 4%. Simply replacing the non-parametric memory, the authors concluded, was enough to update what RAG knew of the world [@lewis2020retrieval, p. 8]. No weight changed.

Today "RAG" usually means something simpler than the 2020 system. In its most basic form, as a 2026 textbook gives it, a retriever returns the top passages, a prompt is built from the question and those passages, and a language model is called with that prompt [@jurafsky2026ir, p. 17]. Nothing is trained jointly; the language model is whatever model the system uses. Anthropic's 2024 description is the same: relevant text is fetched from a knowledge base and added to the user's prompt [@anthropic2024contextual]. The figure traces this version. When the word appears, it is worth asking which is meant. Book 2 builds the simple one.

Retrieval is not always worth it. In September 2024, Anthropic advised that a collection under 200,000 tokens, about 500 pages, could simply go into the prompt whole [@anthropic2024contextual]; but more text is not always used well, as a later section shows, and the balance depends on the model. Nor must a system search on every message: in what is called agent-based RAG, the system itself chooses whether to retrieve on a given turn, and from which collection [@jurafsky2026ir, p. 18]. That decision is a tool call.

## Cutting documents into chunks

A retriever returns pieces of documents. A piece is a **chunk**, and cutting documents into them is **chunking**. The research systems used small pieces: 100 words in DPR and RAG [@karpukhin2020dense, p. 6772; @lewis2020retrieval, p. 4]. One reason is that the encoder has a fixed input length (512 tokens for BERT [@jurafsky2026ir, p. 13]); another, that the prompt must hold several pieces: in 2023, with a 4,000-token window, one team could fit at most five passages safely [@gao2023enabling, p. 6469].

In products the pieces are larger and overlap. As of 2026-11, OpenAI's file search cuts each file, by default, into chunks of 800 tokens, each sharing 400 tokens with the next [@openai2026retrieval]. **Overlap** means consecutive chunks share some text, so that a sentence cut at one chunk's edge appears whole in the next. <!-- D-009: code/checks/tools.py, Example 9 --> A 5,000-token document becomes 12 chunks holding 9,400 tokens, 1.88 times the document.

A chunk loses its surroundings. In Anthropic's 2024 example, the chunk "The company's revenue grew by 3% over the previous quarter." does not say which company or quarter, so it is hard to match to a question about one company's second quarter of 2023 [@anthropic2024contextual]. Its remedy has a model write a sentence of context for each chunk and prepend it, much as DPR put each article's title in front of its passages. In the company's own experiments, not peer reviewed, this together with BM25 and a reranker cut the share of relevant chunks missing from the top 20 results from 5.7% to 1.9% [@anthropic2024contextual]. No source read for this chapter gives a rule for chunk size; the same post says chunks are usually a few hundred tokens at most, and that their size, boundaries, and overlap can affect how well retrieval works [@anthropic2024contextual].

## Grounding and citations

An answer built from retrieved text can say where it came from. It is **grounded**: based on facts from a curated collection, with the passage or document it came from available to the user [@jurafsky2026ir, p. 2]. A **citation** in an answer points from a statement to a passage that supports it, which gives **verifiability**: a reader can check the statement against its source.

OpenAI's WebGPT of December 2021 joined both halves of this chapter. GPT-3 was fine-tuned to answer questions by searching and moving around the web in a browser that showed only text, and it had to collect references along the way to support its answers [@nakano2021webgpt, p. 1]. The browser understood a few text commands, such as search, click a link, and quote a passage as a reference; any other text the model wrote counted as an invalid action [@nakano2021webgpt, p. 3]. People preferred its answers to those of the humans who had demonstrated the task 56% of the time [@nakano2021webgpt, p. 1]. Its authors named the limit: their training procedure gives a model a reason to cherry-pick references that it expects the people judging it to find convincing [@nakano2021webgpt, p. 11].

Later measurements are sobering. In 2023, one benchmark checked citations automatically, measuring **citation recall** (is each statement supported by the passages it cites?) and **citation precision** (are any citations irrelevant?), using a model trained to judge whether one text supports another [@gao2023enabling, p. 6468]. On one data set, even the best models' answers lacked full support from their citations half the time [@gao2023enabling, p. 6465]. Another 2023 study had people check answers that four generative search engines gave between late February and late March 2023: on average, only 51.5% of the sentences were fully supported by their citations, and only 74.5% of the citations supported the sentence they were attached to [@liu2023evaluating, pp. 7001, 7004]. The answers people found most useful tended to have the least accurate citations [@liu2023evaluating, p. 7002]. And the model must use what it is given: in a third 2023 study, models used a relevant passage best at the start or end of a long input, an effect [the chapter on prompting](#prompting) describes, and 50 retrieved documents instead of 20 improved answers by only about 1 to 1.5 points [@liu2024lost, pp. 158-159]. Those were 2023 systems. The lesson that survives the date: a citation lets a reader check a claim. It does not check the claim for them.

## Tools: text the program acts on

A **tool** is a program that the system around the model can run on its behalf: a search engine, a calculator, a database, a function that sends an email. The model cannot run anything. It can write text that asks for a tool to be run, in a form the program recognizes.

The mechanism is plainest in Toolformer, from February 2023, a model that learned to decide when to call an API, which one, with what arguments, and how best to use what came back [@schick2023toolformer, p. 1] (an API is a defined way for one program to ask another for something). A call was ordinary text between markers. In one example, a sentence about 400 of 1,400 participants passing a test continues with a marker, the word Calculator, the expression 400 divided by 1,400, and an arrow. At the arrow, generation stops, the program calls the API, inserts its response and a closing marker, and generation resumes [@schick2023toolformer, p. 4]. The program pastes in 0.29, and the model continues "29%". It is the stop of [the chapter on generation](#generation), used to hand control to a program. The authors noted that the model could not feed one tool's output to another, and that its decision to make a call often depended on the exact wording of the input [@schick2023toolformer, p. 11].

Deployed assistants followed within months. In March 2023, ChatGPT gained plugins, outside tools whose descriptions were listed in the prompt the model was shown [@openai2023chatgptplugins]. In June 2023, OpenAI let developers describe functions to models fine-tuned both to notice when a function should be called and to reply with JSON that fits the function's signature [@openai2023functioncalling]; JSON is the text format of [the chapter on prompting](#prompting). One of its examples was a function that gets the current weather, given a location and a unit, Celsius or Fahrenheit. Anthropic made tool use generally available in May 2024 [@anthropic2024tooluse]. Meta trained Llama 3 (2024) to use a search engine for questions about recent events beyond its knowledge cutoff, among other tools [@grattafiori2024llama, p. 24].

What the model sees of a tool is a **tool definition**: a name, a sentence or two of description, and a description of its arguments; in Anthropic's example, a required location and an optional unit that must be Celsius or Fahrenheit [@anthropic2026definetools]. These are text in the context window. As of 2026-11, OpenAI says so directly: functions "are injected into the system message in a syntax the model has been trained on. This means callable function definitions count against the model's context limit and are billed as input tokens" [@openai2026functioncalling]. Anthropic turns the definitions into a special system prompt [@anthropic2026definetools], as of 2026-11, at a fixed cost of 286 tokens for its current models in the default setting, plus the tokens of every definition [@anthropic2026tooluse]. This is the system prompt of [the chapter on instructions](#instructions). The model never sees a tool's code: it receives only the definition it was given and the result sent back [@anthropic2026howtooluse].

The exchange is a loop, drawn in the figure. Anthropic's documentation (as of 2026-11) gives the contract: "The model never runs anything on its own. It emits a structured request, your code (or Anthropic's servers) runs the operation, and the result flows back into the conversation" [@anthropic2026howtooluse]. In steps: the program sends the user's message with the tool definitions; the model's reply ends with the stop reason "tool use", a way of stopping beyond those in [the chapter on generation](#generation): the model has written a request and waits for its result, and the reply contains one or more **tool calls**, each a tool name with its arguments filled in; the program runs each and sends back everything so far plus the **tool results**; and it repeats until the model stops for any other reason [@anthropic2026howtooluse]. Each call is a round trip between model and program [@anthropic2026howtooluse]. OpenAI describes the same steps [@openai2026functioncalling].

When does the model ask? By default, as of 2026-11, Anthropic's models decide each turn, asking for a tool when the request matches what the tool's description says it can do and the answer is not already in the context [@anthropic2026tooluse]. As far as the mechanism goes, the decision is more next-token prediction: the model was trained on conversations in which writing a call was the likely next text. That is this book's inference from how calls are represented, not a vendor's statement. As of 2026-11, both vendors offer a setting that guarantees a call's arguments match the definition's format [@openai2026functioncalling; @anthropic2026tooluse], the structured output of [the chapter on prompting](#prompting). The shape of a call can be enforced. Whether it is the right call cannot.

## The agent loop

If the model may ask for a tool, read the result, and ask for another, for as many steps as it needs, the system is what current writing usually calls an **agent**: a model choosing actions in a loop, a program carrying them out (a looser sense than the reinforcement-learning agent of [the chapter on following instructions](#instructions)). The word is used loosely; [the frontier chapter](#frontier) surveys what agents do as of the book's date.

The pattern was named in October 2022: **ReAct**, for reasoning and acting. A large model was prompted, with a few examples, to alternate reasoning with actions for the task, so that solving it became a series of thought-action-observation steps [@yao2023react, pp. 1, 3]. A **thought** is free text the model writes for itself, an **action** a command, and an **observation** the result the program pastes in. For questions about Wikipedia, the actions were three: search for an article (which returned its first five sentences), look up a string in it, and finish with an answer [@yao2023react, p. 4]. One of the worked examples placed in the prompt asks for "the elevation range for the area that the eastern sector of the Colorado orogeny extends into"; its steps search for "Colorado orogeny", look up "eastern sector", learn that the sector extends into the High Plains, and search for "High Plains" [@yao2023react, pp. 16-17].

ReAct was not a straight win. On a set of multi-step questions (HotpotQA), it scored 27.4 against 29.4 for chain of thought, the step-by-step prompting of [the chapter on prompting](#prompting), which looks nothing up; on a fact-checking set (Fever), 60.9 against 56.3. The best results combined the two: 35.1 and 64.6 [@yao2023react, pp. 5-6]. <!-- D-009: code/checks/tools.py, Example 10 --> The authors also counted how the loop fails. In hand-labeled HotpotQA attempts, ReAct's failures were 47% reasoning errors, including getting stuck repeating the same thoughts and actions; 23% searches that returned nothing useful; 29% answers that were right but did not match the label's wording; and none from hallucination (the column sums to 99 through rounding). Chain of thought's largest failure, 56%, was hallucination [@yao2023react, p. 6]. Looking things up traded invented facts for loops and bad searches. Runs were capped at seven steps on HotpotQA [@yao2023react, p. 5], and products cap their loops the same way: as of 2026-11, tools that run on Anthropic's servers have an iteration limit, after which the model's turn is paused [@anthropic2026howtooluse].

An interactive statistics package has the same shape: type a command, read the output, decide what to type next. In an agent, the model does the typing and reading and the program does the running. (The analogy is this book's.) Book 2 builds the smallest such loop by hand, and then a full harness.

## A shared plug: the Model Context Protocol

Each system first wired its tools its own way. OpenAI's plugin announcement of March 2023 expected open standards to emerge that would unify how applications offer an interface to AI [@openai2023chatgptplugins]. On 25 November 2024, Anthropic published one as open source, the **Model Context Protocol** (MCP), a standard for connecting AI assistants to the systems that hold data, because each new data source otherwise needed its own custom integration [@anthropic2024mcp].

MCP names three parts: the **host**, the application the user works in; a **client**, its connector; and a **server**, a service that offers capabilities. Their messages follow an existing standard format, and the design "takes some inspiration from the Language Server Protocol", which did the same for programming tools [@mcp2024specification; @mcp2026specification]. In the version of 28 July 2026, current as of 2026-11, a server can offer resources (context and data), prompts (templated messages), and tools (functions for the model to execute); a client asks a server for its list of tools, then asks it to run one [@mcp2026specification]. MCP changes the plumbing between program and tools, not the model's side, which still reads definitions and writes calls as text. As of 2026-11, both OpenAI's and Anthropic's platforms can connect to MCP servers [@openai2026functioncalling; @anthropic2026tooluse], and the specification is still changing.

On security, the specification states that "Tools represent arbitrary code execution and must be treated with appropriate caution", that descriptions of what a tool does are to be treated as untrusted unless they come from a trusted server, and that "there SHOULD always be a human in the loop with the ability to deny tool invocations" [@mcp2026specification]. A **human in the loop** is a person who approves an action before the program carries it out. The protocol itself, the specification says, has no way to enforce these principles [@mcp2026specification]; the applications built on it must.

## How it goes wrong

Each part of the system adds a way to fail.

**Retrieval can miss.** It may find nothing relevant (ReAct's bad searches), a passage without its context (the revenue example), or the right passage, which the model then ignores or misstates (the citation studies). It can even stop mattering: Lewis and colleagues saw their retriever "collapse" on a story-writing task, returning the same documents whatever the input, after which the writer learned to ignore them [@lewis2020retrieval, p. 19].

**Tools can be called wrongly.** The program runs any well-formed call, and a well-formed call can be wrong. As of 2026-11, Anthropic warns that if a user asks "What's the weather?" without saying where, a model may ask, but it might also guess values the user never supplied; "This behavior is not guaranteed" [@anthropic2026tooluse]. Toolformer's sensitivity to wording and ReAct's loops are older forms of the problem. This is why vendors and MCP recommend that a person confirm any action with real consequences [@openai2023functioncalling; @mcp2026specification].

**Text can carry instructions.** [The chapter on prompting](#prompting) described prompt injection, text that turns a model against the intent of whoever built the application, and its indirect form, in which the attacker never talks to the model but places instructions in a web page, document, or email that the system will later retrieve and paste into the context. Retrieval and tools are what open that route. The February 2023 paper that demonstrated the indirect form argued that applications built around a model "blur the line between data and instructions", and that attackers with no direct interface to the model can strike remotely by planting prompts in data the system is likely to retrieve [@greshake2023not, p. 1]. Its authors showed it against real systems, including Bing's GPT-4-based chat, whose sidebar could read the user's open page: hidden text on a page, which the user never saw, could carry instructions that reached the model and changed its behavior [@greshake2023not, p. 4]. With tools attached, injected text can decide whether the system calls other APIs, and how [@greshake2023not, p. 1]. OpenAI's announcement of June 2023 acknowledged, citing a proof-of-concept exploit, that untrusted text in a tool's output could direct the model to take actions no one using the system had intended, and advised using only trusted tools and asking the user to confirm actions with real-world effects, such as sending an email, posting online, or buying something [@openai2023functioncalling].

The reason is the mechanism this chapter has described throughout. Everything the model reads, the system's instructions, a retrieved page, a tool's result, arrives as tokens in one context window. In 2023, the same paper said these attacks had no effective defense yet [@greshake2023not, p. 1]. As of 2026-11, none of the sources read for this chapter claims a complete defense.

## What to carry forward

- A model's knowledge thins out around its knowledge cutoff. Published cutoffs lag releases by months, and the effective cutoff can differ from the reported one. A model alone also cannot act; it only writes text.
- Retrieval puts missing text into the context window. Its keyword form predates neural networks: tf-idf (idf from Spärck Jones, 1972) and BM25, which adds saturation and length normalization, served by an inverted index.
- Dense retrieval (DPR, 2020) encodes questions and passages separately and ranks by dot product. It beat BM25 on four of five 2020 test sets and lost on the fifth. Keywords catch exact names and codes, vectors catch paraphrases; many systems combine them.
- An embedding model turns a text into one vector, trained so that closeness means relevance. Approximate nearest-neighbor methods (HNSW, FAISS) search millions of vectors quickly, at a memory cost of vectors times numbers times bytes.
- Retrieval-augmented generation was named in 2020 by Lewis and colleagues, after REALM and others. Swapping its index updated its knowledge without training. Today "RAG" mostly means pasting retrieved passages into an ordinary model's prompt.
- Documents are cut into chunks, often overlapping, which can lose their context. Citations make answers checkable, not correct: in 2023 studies, about half of answers or sentences lacked full support.
- A tool call is text. Tool definitions are tokens in the context; the model writes a request, the program decides whether to run it, and the result comes back as text. Repeated, this is the agent loop (ReAct, 2022), which traded some hallucination for loops and bad searches.
- MCP (2024) standardizes how programs connect to tools; it does not change what the model sees, and it cannot enforce safety itself.
- Retrieved text and tool results can carry instructions (indirect prompt injection). As of 2026-11, no source read claims a complete defense; having a person confirm consequential actions is the standard advice.

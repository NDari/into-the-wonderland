[The destination chapter](#destination) promised this chapter a limit: a model's parameters contain nothing from after the text it was trained on. A second limit is just as basic: a model produces text and nothing else. OpenAI put both in a few sentences in March 2023: "The only information they can learn from is their training data. This information can be out-of-date and is one-size fits all across applications. Furthermore, the only thing language models can do out-of-the-box is emit text" [@openai2023chatgptplugins].

The two answers in this chapter use one channel, the context window. Retrieval, as [the chapter on adapting](#adapting) described it, finds text the model was never trained on and places it in the window, where the model reads it like any other input. **Tools** let the program around the model do things, such as search the web or run a calculation, when the model's output asks; the result comes back as more text. Neither changes the weights. The chapter builds retrieval from the keyword search the reader may have done in the 2000s, through learned vectors, to retrieval-augmented generation; then it turns to tools, the loop that makes a model into an agent, a shared standard for connecting tools, and the ways all of this goes wrong.

## What the weights cannot know

A 2026 textbook draft states the problem plainly: large language models "are static; they were pretrained once, at a particular time", so they cannot discuss "something that happened last week", nor private data that was never in their training text, such as a company's documents [@jurafsky2026ir, p. 2]. Patrick Lewis and colleagues called a pretrained model's knowledge in 2020 "a parameterized implicit knowledge base" that "cannot easily expand or revise" its memory [@lewis2020retrieval, p. 1].

The date after which a model has seen little or no text is its knowledge cutoff ([the chapter on prompting](#prompting)), and published cutoffs lag behind releases. OpenAI's GPT-4 report of 2023 said the model "generally lacks knowledge of events that have occurred after the vast majority of its pre-training data cuts off in September 2021" [@openai2023gpt4, p. 10]. Meta's card for Llama 3.1 gives a cutoff of December 2023 and a release date of 23 July 2024 [@meta2024llama31card]. <!-- D-009: code/checks/tools.py, Example 10 --> That is about seven months before anyone could use it, and people go on using a model for months or years.

The cutoff is not a sharp edge. GPT-4's report says "the vast majority" of the data, and adds that the training data "contain a small amount of more recent data" [@openai2023gpt4, p. 10]. As of 2026-11, Anthropic lists two dates for each current model, a "reliable knowledge cutoff", "the date through which the model's knowledge is most extensive and reliable", and a broader "training data cutoff", both June 2026 [@anthropic2026models]; OpenAI lists a single "knowledge cutoff" per model, between April and June 2026 for the four models on its page [@openai2026models]. In 2024, Jeffrey Cheng and colleagues studied open-weight models whose training data is public or described and found that the **effective cutoff**, the date a model's knowledge actually reflects, is "distinct from the LLM's reported cutoff" and often "drastically" different, because new web crawls contain much old text and the removal of duplicates is imperfect [@cheng2024dated, pp. 1, 5]. Knowledge thins out toward the cutoff rather than stopping there.

[The chapter on prompting](#prompting) explained that no prompt adds facts the weights lack, and [the chapter on adapting](#adapting) found fine-tuning a poor way to add them. What works is to put the facts into the input, which needs a way to find them. The second limit needs no study. As of 2026-11, Anthropic's documentation lists two jobs that need tools: "Fresh or external data", since "Anything outside the training data or specific to your system needs a tool to fetch it", and "Actions with side effects", which "The model can describe ... but only a tool can perform" [@anthropic2026howtooluse].

## Finding text: retrieval before neural networks

**Information retrieval** (IR) is "the name of the field encompassing the retrieval of all manner of media based on user information needs". In its basic task, a user poses a **query** (here a search request, not the attention vector of [the attention chapter](#attention)) and the system "returns an ordered set of documents from some collection", where a **document** is "whatever unit of text the system indexes and retrieves" [@jurafsky2026ir, p. 3]. A reader who built a document search in the 2000s did this: turned each document and the query into vectors of word counts and ranked by cosine, as in [the embeddings chapter](#embeddings).

A small example shows where raw counts go wrong. OpenAI's retrieval guide (as of 2026-11) uses the query "When did we go to the moon?" and three sentences: "The first lunar landing occurred in July of 1969.", "The first man on the moon was Neil Armstrong.", and "When I ate the moon cake, it was delicious." [@openai2026retrieval]. Call them D1, D2, and D3. <!-- D-009: code/checks/tools.py, Example 1 --> Counting lowercased words and comparing by cosine gives D1 0.126, D2 0.342, D3 0.378. The moon cake wins: it shares "when", "the", and "moon" with the question. The lunar landing shares only "the".

The first repair is to count rare words for more. Karen Spärck Jones argued in 1972 that terms should be weighted by how often they occur in the collection, so that, in the words of the abstract in the paper's 2004 reprint, "matches on less frequent, more specific, terms are of greater value than matches on frequent terms" [@sparckjones1972statistical, p. 1]. <!-- D-009: code/checks/tools.py, Example 2 --> In her example, in a collection of 200 documents, a term that occurs 90 times gets weight 2 and one that occurs three times gets weight 7 [@sparckjones1972statistical, p. 6]: a base-2 logarithm in whole steps. The idea became the **inverse document frequency** (idf) [@jurafsky2026ir, p. 6]. Combined with a word's **term frequency** (tf) in a document, it gives **tf-idf**, in a current textbook's standard form [@jurafsky2026ir, pp. 5-6]:

$$\text{tf-idf}(t, d) = \text{tf}_{t,d} \times \log_{10} \frac{N}{\text{df}_t}$$

where $t$ is a word, $d$ a document, $N$ the number of documents in the collection, and $\text{df}_t$ the number of documents that contain $t$. The term frequency is damped, $\text{tf}_{t,d} = 1 + \log_{10}$ of the count of $t$ in $d$ (0 if $t$ is absent), because "a word appearing 100 times in a document doesn't make that word 100 times more likely to be relevant" [@jurafsky2026ir, p. 6]. A word found in every document gets weight 0.

<!-- D-009: code/checks/tools.py, Example 1 --> On the three sentences, "the" occurs in all three and gets weight 0; "moon" occurs in two (0.176); "when" in one (0.477). The tf-idf cosines are D1 0.000, D2 0.061, D3 0.426. Weighting made things worse: the lunar landing now shares no weighted word with the question, and the cake's "when" counts heavily. Nothing in the counts says that "lunar" is about the moon.

On real collections, the standard keyword method is **BM25**, which Stephen Robertson and Hugo Zaragoza called in 2009 "the best-known instantiation" of a model developed "over a period of about 30 years" [@robertson2009probabilistic, p. 334]; its original form is a 1994 paper by Robertson and Walker [@robertson2009probabilistic, pp. 361, 388]. BM25 adds two ideas to tf-idf. **Saturation** (of a word's count, not the saturated units of [the deep-turn chapter](#deep-turn)): "any one term's contribution to the document score cannot exceed a saturation point" however often it occurs [@robertson2009probabilistic, p. 356]. **Length normalization**: long documents contain more of every word, partly because "Some authors are simply more verbose than others" [@robertson2009probabilistic, p. 359], so their counts are discounted. In the survey's standard form, the weight of query word $i$ in a document is [@robertson2009probabilistic, p. 360]

$$w_i = \frac{tf}{k_1 \left( (1 - b) + b \, \frac{dl}{avdl} \right) + tf} \times w_i^{\text{RSJ}}$$

where $tf$ is how often the word occurs in the document, $dl$ the document's length and $avdl$ the average length in the collection, $k_1$ and $b$ two settings chosen by the user, and $w_i^{\text{RSJ}}$ a version of the inverse document frequency [@robertson2009probabilistic, p. 349]. A document's score is the sum of these weights over the query's words.

![How much a word's count adds to a document's score, computed for this chapter. (a) A raw count grows without limit; the logarithm used in tf-idf grows slowly, with no ceiling; BM25's term-frequency part (k1 = 1.2, average document length) approaches 1. (b) BM25 with b = 0.75: the same three occurrences count for more in a short document than in a long one.](../../figures/tools-bm25.svg){#fig:tools-bm25}

<!-- D-009: code/checks/tools.py, Example 3 --> The figure uses $b = 0.75$, inside the range $0.5 < b < 0.8$, and $k_1 = 1.2$, at the lower edge of $1.2 < k_1 < 2$; the survey calls values in these ranges "reasonably good in many circumstances" [@robertson2009probabilistic, pp. 360-361], and a 2026 textbook reports that Manning and colleagues suggest exactly $b = 0.75$ [@jurafsky2026ir, p. 9]. In a document of average length, a word that appears once contributes 0.455 times its idf; three times, 0.714; ten times, 0.893; a hundred times, 0.988. The ceiling is 1, where tf-idf's logarithm would give 3 and a raw count 100. Three occurrences count 0.800 in a document half the average length and 0.588 in one twice the average. The theory does not fix the settings: "the model provides no guidance on how these should be set" [@robertson2009probabilistic, p. 360], and one 2020 team tuned them to $b = 0.4$ and $k_1 = 0.9$ [@karpukhin2020dense, p. 6773].

Keyword search is fast because of the **inverted index**, which, "given a query term, gives a list of documents that contain the term" [@jurafsky2026ir, p. 9]; a document with none of the query's words is never touched. Since each document's vector has an entry for every word in the vocabulary, almost all zero, this is called **sparse retrieval** [@jurafsky2026ir, p. 3]. As late as 2020, tf-idf and BM25 were "the de facto method" for finding passages that answer questions [@karpukhin2020dense, p. 6769].

## Learned vectors for retrieval

The weakness of keywords is the one [the embeddings chapter](#embeddings) took up with latent semantic analysis: different words for the same thing. Vladimir Karpukhin and colleagues gave an example in 2020. "Who is the bad guy in lord of the rings?" is answered by "Sala Baker is best known for portraying the villain Sauron in the Lord of the Rings trilogy"; a keyword system "would have difficulty retrieving such a context, while a dense retrieval system would be able to better match "bad guy" with "villain"" [@karpukhin2020dense, p. 6769]. Latent semantic analysis had mixed results in 1990, as that chapter reported; the link is the problem, not a line of descent.

Word vectors are the obvious first try. <!-- D-009: code/checks/tools.py, Example 1 --> Represent each sentence by the average of its GloVe vectors and compare by cosine: D1 0.790, D2 0.896, D3 0.902. The cake still wins, because small words like "the" and "when" dominate the averages. Drop 16 such words, a **stop list** in the IR term for "high-frequency terms (often function words like the, a, to)" [@jurafsky2026ir, p. 9], and only "go" and "moon" are left of the question. Now D2 scores 0.795, D1 0.651, D3 0.621: both moon landings beat the cake. The vectors know that "moon" is closer to "lunar" (cosine 0.683) than to "cake" (0.315). But the result rests on a hand-picked word list, and by 2019 averaged GloVe vectors were a baseline that sentence-level methods were compared against [@reimers2019sentence, p. 3982]. OpenAI's guide reports scores from its trained embedding model of 65%, 43%, and 28% for the three sentences: the lunar landing first, though it shares no word with the question except "the" [@openai2026retrieval]. Those are OpenAI's numbers, from a model this book cannot run.

The trained retriever came from Karpukhin and colleagues' **dense passage retrieval** (DPR) of 2020. It used two BERT models from [the pretraining chapter](#pretraining), one to turn a question into a vector and one to turn a passage into a vector, 768 numbers each, and scored a passage by the dot product of the two [@karpukhin2020dense, p. 6771]. Because the question never meets a passage inside a network, all the passage vectors can be computed once, in advance. Training pushed the dot product up for each question and its correct passage, and down for the other passages in the same batch [@karpukhin2020dense, p. 6770]. The collection was English Wikipedia of 20 December 2018, cut into 21,015,324 passages of 100 words, each with its article's title in front [@karpukhin2020dense, p. 6772].

The measure was **top-20 accuracy**: the share of questions for which at least one of the 20 highest-scoring passages contains the answer [@karpukhin2020dense, p. 6773]. <!-- D-009: code/checks/tools.py, Example 6 -->

| Data set (as named in the paper) | BM25 | DPR | Difference |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Natural Questions | 59.1 | 78.4 | +19.3 |
| TriviaQA | 66.9 | 79.4 | +12.5 |
| WQ | 55.0 | 73.2 | +18.2 |
| TREC | 70.9 | 79.8 | +8.9 |
| SQuAD | 68.8 | 63.2 | -5.6 |

The abstract's "9%-19% absolute" gain [@karpukhin2020dense, p. 6769] covers the first four. On the fifth, BM25 won, and the authors said why: in SQuAD "the annotators wrote questions after seeing the passage", so questions and passages share many words [@karpukhin2020dense, p. 6773]. The two methods fail in opposite ways. Keywords cannot "capture lexical variations or semantic relationships well", while dense vectors may "lack sufficient capacity to represent salient phrases which appear rarely" [@karpukhin2020dense, p. 6780]; in Anthropic's 2024 example, a search for the error code "TS-999" may find passages about error codes in general and "miss the exact "TS-999" match" [@anthropic2024contextual]. Learned vectors find paraphrases that keywords miss; keywords find exact names and codes that vectors blur. Many systems use both [@anthropic2024contextual]; DPR's authors already tried adding the two scores, which helped on some data sets and not on others [@karpukhin2020dense, p. 6773]. Combining the two rankings is called **hybrid search**.

Why two separate encoders? One BERT reading question and passage together, a **cross-encoder**, is more accurate, but nothing can be computed in advance. <!-- D-009: code/checks/tools.py, Example 8 --> Nils Reimers and Iryna Gurevych worked out in 2019 that finding the most similar pair among 10,000 sentences that way takes 49,995,000 passes through BERT, "about 65 hours" on a GPU of the time [@reimers2019sentence, p. 3982]. Separate encoders, a **bi-encoder**, need 10,000 passes and a table of cosines, about 5 seconds [@reimers2019sentence, p. 3983], at some cost in accuracy [@jurafsky2026ir, p. 14]. Some systems use both: a cheap first pass, keywords or a vector search, keeps the top few candidates, and a cross-encoder, the **reranker**, reorders only those [@jurafsky2026ir, p. 14].

Reimers and Gurevych's Sentence-BERT also gave the tool its current form: a network trained so that whole sentences come out as vectors that "can be compared using cosine-similarity", similar sentences close together [@reimers2019sentence, p. 3982]. That is what an **embedding model** means today: text in, one vector out, trained so that closeness means relevance. Embedding models are compared on benchmarks such as MTEB (2022), whose authors found that "no particular text embedding method dominates across all tasks" [@muennighoff2023mteb, p. 2014]; [the chapter on measuring models](#evaluation) explains how to read such a leaderboard, a public ranking of methods by score. As of 2026-11, Anthropic "does not offer its own embedding model" and points to another company's, most of whose current models produce 1,024 numbers per text by default, scaled so that "Cosine similarity is equivalent to dot-product similarity" [@anthropic2026embeddings]; OpenAI's produce 1,536 or 3,072 [@openai2026embeddings].

## Searching millions of vectors

Finding the passages whose vectors are closest to the question's is **nearest neighbor search**, the reader's k-nearest-neighbors classifier without the vote. <!-- D-009: code/checks/tools.py, Example 5 --> Comparing a question with every passage in DPR's index would take 21,015,324 times 768, about 16.1 billion multiplications and additions. So systems use **approximate nearest neighbor** search [@jurafsky2026ir, p. 16], which finds most of the nearest vectors quickly and may miss a few. One such method, used by the RAG system below, is Yury Malkov and Dmitry Yashunin's **HNSW** (hierarchical navigable small world graphs, 2016, published in a journal in 2020), which "incrementally builds a multi-layer structure" of graphs, with few vectors in the top layers and all of them at the bottom; searching from the top down "allows a logarithmic complexity scaling" [@malkov2020efficient, p. 1]. An analogy, not from the paper: motorways to reach the right region, then smaller roads, then the street. DPR and RAG both used **FAISS**, an open-source library "for similarity search and clustering of dense vectors" [@karpukhin2020dense, p. 6771], described by Facebook AI Research in 2017; it built a graph "connecting 1 billion vectors in less than 12 hours on 4 Maxwell Titan X GPUs" [@johnson2021billion, p. 1]. Products that store vectors and search them this way are sold as a **vector database** or **vector store**.

<!-- D-009: code/checks/tools.py, Example 5 --> The memory is vectors times numbers per vector times bytes per number. DPR's index of about 16.1 billion numbers takes 64.56 GB in 32-bit numbers, 32.28 GB in 16-bit ones, 2.02 GB at one bit each. The 16-bit index of 2018 Wikipedia is twice the 16.1 GB of Llama 3 8B's weights in [the chapter on generation](#generation). A company's million passages at 1,024 numbers each take 4.10 GB in 32-bit numbers and 1.02 GB in 8-bit ones.

## Retrieval-augmented generation

The idea of letting a model look things up in a text collection had several near-simultaneous versions around 2019-2020. One mixed a language model's next-word probabilities with those of a k-nearest-neighbors search over earlier text [@khandelwal2020generalization, p. 1]. In February 2020, Kelvin Guu and colleagues at Google Research put a retriever inside pretraining. Their REALM filled in masked words, as BERT did, after retrieving passages from Wikipedia, and was trained by "backpropagating through a retrieval step that considers millions of documents" [@guu2020realm, p. 1], on the principle that "a retrieval that improves the language model's perplexity is helpful and should be rewarded" [@guu2020realm, p. 2]. REALM picked its answer out of the retrieved text; it did not write one [@lewis2020retrieval, p. 1].

The name **retrieval-augmented generation** (RAG) comes from a paper posted in May 2020 by Lewis and colleagues at Facebook AI Research, with University College London and New York University, which combined a learned retriever with a model that writes its answer [@lewis2020retrieval, p. 1]. Their terms will be familiar to a statistician. The model's weights are its **parametric memory**; the index of passages, kept and searched like the data of a nearest-neighbor method, is its **non-parametric memory** [@lewis2020retrieval, p. 3], in which "knowledge can be directly revised and expanded, and accessed knowledge can be inspected and interpreted" [@lewis2020retrieval, p. 1]. The retriever was DPR's, over the same 21 million passages; the writer was BART, a 400-million-parameter encoder-decoder transformer, which read the question and a retrieved passage simply placed one after the other [@lewis2020retrieval, pp. 3-4]. The question encoder and the writer were fine-tuned together; the passage vectors were left as they were [@lewis2020retrieval, pp. 4, 18].

The authors used a vivid pair of terms: **closed-book** models answer from their parameters alone, **open-book** ones look things up [@lewis2020retrieval, p. 5]. On Natural Questions, a closed-book T5 with 11 billion parameters scored 34.5, REALM 40.4, and RAG 44.5 [@lewis2020retrieval, p. 6]. From reading outputs, the authors judged that RAG models "hallucinate less" than BART alone [@lewis2020retrieval, p. 6]: less, not never. One printed example shows the model using its passages. Asked to write a quiz-show clue whose answer is "Hemingway", it retrieved five passages, including one about *A Farewell to Arms* and one about *The Sun Also Rises*, and wrote: "The Sun Also Rises" is a novel by this author of "A Farewell to Arms". It leaned on the passage about *The Sun Also Rises* while writing that title, and on the other while writing *A Farewell to Arms* [@lewis2020retrieval, p. 7].

The paper's most useful experiment concerns the cutoff. The authors built a second index from Wikipedia of December 2016 and asked "Who is {position}?" about 82 world leaders who had changed between 2016 and 2018 [@lewis2020retrieval, pp. 7-8]. <!-- D-009: code/checks/tools.py, Example 7 --> With the 2016 index, RAG named 70% of the 2016 leaders; with the 2018 index, 68% of the 2018 leaders (about 57 and 56 of 82, counts inferred from the percentages). With the indexes swapped, 12% and 4%. "This shows we can update RAG's world knowledge by simply replacing its non-parametric memory" [@lewis2020retrieval, p. 8]. No weight changed.

Keyword scores (tf-idf) keep the moon cake; averaged word vectors with stop words removed, and OpenAI's trained model (their published figures, not computed here), keep the two moon landings. Today "RAG" usually means something simpler than the 2020 system. The textbook's "most basic RAG algorithm" calls a retriever for the top passages, creates "a prompt that includes q and the retrieved passages", and calls a language model with it [@jurafsky2026ir, p. 17]. Nothing is trained jointly; the language model is whatever model the system uses. Anthropic's 2024 description is the same: RAG "retrieves relevant information from a knowledge base and appends it to the user's prompt" [@anthropic2024contextual]. The figure traces this version. When the word appears, it is worth asking which is meant. Book 2 builds the simple one.

Retrieval is not always worth it. In September 2024, Anthropic advised that a collection under "200,000 tokens (about 500 pages of material)" could simply go into the prompt whole [@anthropic2024contextual]; but more text is not always used well, as a later section shows, and the balance depends on the model. Nor must a system search on every message: in what the textbook calls agent-based RAG, "the system decides when to call a retrieval agent and for which collection" [@jurafsky2026ir, p. 18]. That decision is a tool call.

## Cutting documents into chunks

A retriever returns pieces of documents. A piece is a **chunk**, and cutting documents into them is **chunking**. The research systems used small pieces: 100 words in DPR and RAG [@karpukhin2020dense, p. 6772; @lewis2020retrieval, p. 4]. One reason is that the encoder has a fixed input length (512 tokens for BERT [@jurafsky2026ir, p. 13]); another, that the prompt must hold several pieces: in 2023, with a 4,000-token window, one team could "at most safely fit k = 5 passages" [@gao2023enabling, p. 6469].

In products the pieces are larger and overlap. As of 2026-11, OpenAI's file search splits each file "into 800-token chunks, with 400-token overlap between consecutive chunks" by default [@openai2026retrieval]. **Overlap** means consecutive chunks share some text, so that a sentence cut at one chunk's edge appears whole in the next. <!-- D-009: code/checks/tools.py, Example 9 --> A 5,000-token document becomes 12 chunks holding 9,400 tokens, 1.88 times the document.

A chunk loses its surroundings. In Anthropic's 2024 example, the chunk "The company's revenue grew by 3% over the previous quarter." does not say which company or quarter, so it is hard to match to a question about one company's second quarter of 2023 [@anthropic2024contextual]. Its remedy has a model write a sentence of context for each chunk and prepend it, much as DPR put each article's title in front of its passages. In the company's own experiments, not peer reviewed, this together with BM25 and a reranker cut the share of relevant chunks missing from the top 20 results from 5.7% to 1.9% [@anthropic2024contextual]. No source read for this chapter gives a rule for chunk size; the same post says chunks are "usually no more than a few hundred tokens" and that size, boundaries, and overlap "can affect retrieval performance" [@anthropic2024contextual].

## Grounding and citations

An answer built from retrieved text can say where it came from. It is **grounded**: based on "facts from some curated dataset", with "the context of the passage or document it came from" available to the user [@jurafsky2026ir, p. 2]. A **citation** in an answer points from a statement to a passage that supports it, which gives **verifiability**: a reader can check the statement against its source.

OpenAI's WebGPT of December 2021 joined both halves of this chapter. GPT-3 was fine-tuned to answer questions in "a text-based web-browsing environment", and "models must collect references while browsing in support of their answers" [@nakano2021webgpt, p. 1]. The browser understood a few text commands, such as search, click a link, and quote a passage as a reference. "If a model generates any other text, it is considered to be an invalid action" [@nakano2021webgpt, p. 3]. People preferred its answers to those of the humans who had demonstrated the task 56% of the time [@nakano2021webgpt, p. 1]. Its authors named the limit: training "incentivizes models to cherry-pick references that they expect labelers to find convincing" [@nakano2021webgpt, p. 11].

Later measurements are sobering. In 2023, Tianyu Gao and colleagues checked citations automatically, measuring **citation recall** (is each statement supported by the passages it cites?) and **citation precision** (are any citations irrelevant?), using a model trained to judge whether one text supports another [@gao2023enabling, p. 6468]. On one data set, "even the best models lack complete citation support 50% of the time" [@gao2023enabling, p. 6465]. Nelson Liu and colleagues had people check answers that four generative search engines gave between late February and late March 2023: "a mere 51.5% of generated sentences are fully supported by citations and only 74.5% of citations support their associated sentence" [@liu2023evaluating, pp. 7001, 7004]. The answers people found most useful tended to have the least accurate citations [@liu2023evaluating, p. 7002]. And the model must use what it is given: in another 2023 study, models used a relevant passage best at the start or end of a long input, an effect [the chapter on prompting](#prompting) describes, and 50 retrieved documents instead of 20 improved answers by only about 1 to 1.5 points [@liu2024lost, pp. 158-159]. Those were 2023 systems. The lesson that survives the date: a citation lets a reader check a claim. It does not check the claim for them.

## Tools: text the program acts on

A **tool** is a program that the system around the model can run on its behalf: a search engine, a calculator, a database, a function that sends an email. The model cannot run anything. It can write text that asks for a tool to be run, in a form the program recognizes.

The mechanism is plainest in Toolformer, from Timo Schick and colleagues at Meta AI Research in February 2023, a model that learned "which APIs to call, when to call them, what arguments to pass, and how to best incorporate the results" [@schick2023toolformer, p. 1] (an API is a defined way for one program to ask another for something). A call was ordinary text between markers. In one example, a sentence about 400 of 1,400 participants passing a test continues with a marker, the word Calculator, the expression 400 divided by 1,400, and an arrow. At the arrow, "we interrupt the decoding process, call the appropriate API to get a response, and continue the decoding process after inserting both the response" and a closing marker [@schick2023toolformer, p. 4]. The program pastes in 0.29, and the model continues "29%". It is the stop of [the chapter on generation](#generation), used to hand control to a program. The authors noted that the model could not feed one tool's output to another, and that its decision to make a call was "sensitive to the exact wording" of the input [@schick2023toolformer, p. 11].

Deployed assistants followed within months. In March 2023, ChatGPT gained "plugins", outside tools whose descriptions were listed "in the prompt shown to the language model" [@openai2023chatgptplugins]. In June 2023, OpenAI let developers "describe functions" to models "fine-tuned to both detect when a function needs to be called ... and to respond with JSON that adheres to the function signature" [@openai2023functioncalling]; JSON is the text format of [the chapter on prompting](#prompting). One of its examples was a function that gets the current weather, given a location and a unit, Celsius or Fahrenheit. Anthropic made tool use generally available in May 2024 [@anthropic2024tooluse]. Meta trained Llama 3 (2024) to use a search engine "to answer questions about recent events that go beyond its knowledge cutoff", among other tools [@grattafiori2024llama, p. 24].

What the model sees of a tool is a **tool definition**: a name, a sentence or two of description, and a description of its arguments; in Anthropic's example, a required location and an optional unit that must be Celsius or Fahrenheit [@anthropic2026definetools]. These are text in the context window. As of 2026-11, OpenAI says so directly: functions "are injected into the system message in a syntax the model has been trained on. This means callable function definitions count against the model's context limit and are billed as input tokens" [@openai2026functioncalling]. Anthropic builds "a special system prompt from the tool definitions" [@anthropic2026definetools], as of 2026-11, at a fixed cost of 286 tokens for its current models in the default setting, plus the tokens of every definition [@anthropic2026tooluse]. This is the system prompt of [the chapter on instructions](#instructions). The model never sees a tool's code: it "only receives the schema you provided and the result you returned" [@anthropic2026howtooluse].

The exchange is a loop, drawn in the figure. Anthropic's documentation (as of 2026-11) gives the contract: "The model never runs anything on its own. It emits a structured request, your code (or Anthropic's servers) runs the operation, and the result flows back into the conversation" [@anthropic2026howtooluse]. In steps: the program sends the user's message with the tool definitions; the model's reply ends with the stop reason "tool use", a way of stopping beyond those in [the chapter on generation](#generation): the model has written a request and waits for its result, and the reply contains one or more **tool calls**, each a tool name with its arguments filled in; the program runs each and sends back everything so far plus the **tool results**; and it repeats until the model stops for any other reason [@anthropic2026howtooluse]. "The model asks, you execute, you report back, the model continues" [@anthropic2026howtooluse]. OpenAI describes the same steps [@openai2026functioncalling].

When does the model ask? By default, as of 2026-11, Anthropic's models decide each turn, asking for a tool "when the request maps to that tool's described capability and the answer isn't already in context" [@anthropic2026tooluse]. As far as the mechanism goes, the decision is more next-token prediction: the model was trained on conversations in which writing a call was the likely next text. That is this book's inference from how calls are represented, not a vendor's statement. As of 2026-11, both vendors offer a setting that guarantees a call's arguments match the definition's format [@openai2026functioncalling; @anthropic2026tooluse], the structured output of [the chapter on prompting](#prompting). The shape of a call can be enforced. Whether it is the right call cannot.

## The agent loop

If the model may ask for a tool, read the result, and ask for another, for as many steps as it needs, the system is what current writing usually calls an **agent**: a model choosing actions in a loop, a program carrying them out (a looser sense than the reinforcement-learning agent of [the chapter on following instructions](#instructions)). The word is used loosely; [the frontier chapter](#frontier) surveys what agents do as of the book's date.

Shunyu Yao and colleagues named the pattern in October 2022: **ReAct**, for reasoning and acting. They prompted a large model, with a few examples, to write "both reasoning traces and task-specific actions in an interleaved manner", in "multiple thought-action-observation steps" [@yao2023react, pp. 1, 3]. A **thought** is free text the model writes for itself, an **action** a command, and an **observation** the result the program pastes in. For questions about Wikipedia, the actions were three: search for an article (which returned its first five sentences), look up a string in it, and finish with an answer [@yao2023react, p. 4]. One of the worked examples the authors placed in the prompt asks for "the elevation range for the area that the eastern sector of the Colorado orogeny extends into"; its steps search for "Colorado orogeny", look up "eastern sector", learn that it "extends into the High Plains", and search for "High Plains" [@yao2023react, pp. 16-17].

ReAct was not a straight win. On a set of multi-step questions (HotpotQA), it scored 27.4 against 29.4 for chain of thought, the step-by-step prompting of [the chapter on prompting](#prompting), which looks nothing up; on a fact-checking set (Fever), 60.9 against 56.3. The best results combined the two: 35.1 and 64.6 [@yao2023react, pp. 5-6]. <!-- D-009: code/checks/tools.py, Example 10 --> The authors also counted how the loop fails. In hand-labeled HotpotQA attempts, ReAct's failures were 47% reasoning errors, including getting stuck repeating the same thoughts and actions; 23% searches that returned nothing useful; 29% answers that were right but did not match the label's wording; and none from hallucination (the column sums to 99 through rounding). Chain of thought's largest failure, 56%, was hallucination [@yao2023react, p. 6]. Looking things up traded invented facts for loops and bad searches. Runs were capped at seven steps on HotpotQA [@yao2023react, p. 5], and products cap their loops the same way: as of 2026-11, tools that run on Anthropic's servers have "an iteration limit", after which the model's turn is paused [@anthropic2026howtooluse].

A reader who has used an interactive statistics package knows the shape: type a command, read the output, decide what to type next. In an agent, the model does the typing and reading and the program does the running. (The analogy is this book's.) Book 2 builds the smallest such loop by hand, and then a full harness.

## A shared plug: the Model Context Protocol

Each system first wired its tools its own way. OpenAI's plugin announcement of March 2023 expected "that open standards will emerge to unify the ways in which applications expose an AI-facing interface" [@openai2023chatgptplugins]. On 25 November 2024, Anthropic published one as open source, the **Model Context Protocol** (MCP), "a new standard for connecting AI assistants to the systems where data lives", because "Every new data source requires its own custom implementation" [@anthropic2024mcp].

MCP names three parts: the **host**, the application the user works in; a **client**, its connector; and a **server**, a service that offers capabilities. Their messages follow an existing standard format, and the design "takes some inspiration from the Language Server Protocol", which did the same for programming tools [@mcp2024specification; @mcp2026specification]. In the version of 28 July 2026, current as of 2026-11, a server can offer resources ("Context and data"), prompts ("Templated messages"), and tools ("Functions for the AI model to execute"); a client asks a server for its list of tools, then asks it to run one [@mcp2026specification]. MCP changes the plumbing between program and tools, not the model's side, which still reads definitions and writes calls as text. As of 2026-11, both OpenAI's and Anthropic's platforms can connect to MCP servers [@openai2026functioncalling; @anthropic2026tooluse], and the specification is still changing.

It is frank about risk: "Tools represent arbitrary code execution and must be treated with appropriate caution", tool descriptions "should be considered untrusted, unless obtained from a trusted server", and "there SHOULD always be a human in the loop with the ability to deny tool invocations" [@mcp2026specification]. A **human in the loop** is a person who approves an action before the program carries it out. The protocol itself, the specification says, "cannot enforce these security principles" [@mcp2026specification]; the applications built on it must.

## How it goes wrong

Each part of the system adds a way to fail.

**Retrieval can miss.** It may find nothing relevant (ReAct's bad searches), a passage without its context (the revenue example), or the right passage, which the model then ignores or misstates (the citation studies). It can even stop mattering: Lewis and colleagues saw their retriever "collapse" on a story-writing task and "retrieve the same documents regardless of the input", after which the writer learned to ignore them [@lewis2020retrieval, p. 19].

**Tools can be called wrongly.** The program runs any well-formed call, and a well-formed call can be wrong. As of 2026-11, Anthropic warns that if a user asks "What's the weather?" without saying where, a model may ask, but "might guess values you didn't supply"; "This behavior is not guaranteed" [@anthropic2026tooluse]. Toolformer's sensitivity to wording and ReAct's loops are older forms of the problem. This is why vendors and MCP recommend that a person confirm any action with real consequences [@openai2023functioncalling; @mcp2026specification].

**Text can carry instructions.** [The chapter on prompting](#prompting) described prompt injection, text that turns a model against the intent of whoever built the application, and its indirect form, in which the attacker never talks to the model but places instructions in a web page, document, or email that the system will later retrieve and paste into the context. Retrieval and tools are what open that route. In Kai Greshake and colleagues' words of February 2023, "LLM-Integrated Applications blur the line between data and instructions", and attackers can strike "remotely (without a direct interface) ... by strategically injecting prompts into data likely to be retrieved" [@greshake2023not, p. 1]. They showed it against real systems, including Bing's GPT-4-based chat, whose sidebar could read the user's open page: "any prompts/instructions written on a page (while being invisible to the user) can be effectively injected and affect the model" [@greshake2023not, p. 4]. With tools attached, injected text can "control how and if other APIs are called" [@greshake2023not, p. 1]. OpenAI's announcement of June 2023 acknowledged that "untrusted data from a tool's output can instruct the model to perform unintended actions", and advised trusted tools only and asking the user before "sending an email, posting online, or making a purchase" [@openai2023functioncalling].

The reason is the mechanism this chapter has described throughout. Everything the model reads, the system's instructions, a retrieved page, a tool's result, arrives as tokens in one context window. In 2023, Greshake and colleagues wrote that "effective mitigations of these emerging threats are currently lacking" [@greshake2023not, p. 1]. As of 2026-11, none of the sources read for this chapter claims a complete defense.

## What to carry forward

- A model's knowledge thins out around its knowledge cutoff. Published cutoffs lag releases by months, and the effective cutoff can differ from the reported one. A model alone also cannot act; it only writes text.
- Retrieval puts missing text into the context window. Its keyword form is the reader's own: tf-idf (idf from Spärck Jones, 1972) and BM25, which adds saturation and length normalization, served by an inverted index.
- Dense retrieval (DPR, 2020) encodes questions and passages separately and ranks by dot product. It beat BM25 on four of five 2020 test sets and lost on the fifth. Keywords catch exact names and codes, vectors catch paraphrases; many systems combine them.
- An embedding model turns a text into one vector, trained so that closeness means relevance. Approximate nearest-neighbor methods (HNSW, FAISS) search millions of vectors quickly, at a memory cost of vectors times numbers times bytes.
- Retrieval-augmented generation was named in 2020 by Lewis and colleagues, after REALM and others. Swapping its index updated its knowledge without training. Today "RAG" mostly means pasting retrieved passages into an ordinary model's prompt.
- Documents are cut into chunks, often overlapping, which can lose their context. Citations make answers checkable, not correct: in 2023 studies, about half of answers or sentences lacked full support.
- A tool call is text. Tool definitions are tokens in the context; the model writes a request, the program decides whether to run it, and the result comes back as text. Repeated, this is the agent loop (ReAct, 2022), which traded some hallucination for loops and bad searches.
- MCP (2024) standardizes how programs connect to tools; it does not change what the model sees, and it cannot enforce safety itself.
- Retrieved text and tool results can carry instructions (indirect prompt injection). As of 2026-11, no source read claims a complete defense; having a person confirm consequential actions is the standard advice.

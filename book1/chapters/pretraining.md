# Pretraining: learn language first, tasks later (2015-2019) {#pretraining}

<!-- D-002: intuition only; no new formulas. D-003: no code. D-009: numbers from code/checks/pretraining.py. D-014: figure from figures/pretraining.py. D-016: the vision bridge is kept to about a page. -->

The working rule of the 2000s was simple: to build a classifier for a task, collect labeled examples of that task. A 2010 survey of the field put the assumption behind it plainly: "the training and test data are drawn from the same feature space and the same distribution. When the distribution changes, most statistical models need to be rebuilt from scratch using newly collected training data" [@pan2010survey, p. 1]. Labels were the expensive part. A sentiment classifier needed thousands of reviews marked positive or negative; a question-answering system needed thousands of questions with their answers.

This chapter is about how that rule stopped holding for language, between 2015 and 2019. The new recipe had two stages. First, train one large network on a huge amount of unlabeled text, with a task that the text supplies for free, such as predicting the next word. Then adapt that network to each real task with a small set of labeled examples. [The chapter on the deep learning turn](#deep-turn) closed its account of pretraining with a textbook's observation, as of 2016, that unsupervised pretraining had been largely abandoned except in natural language processing. This is where that exception led.

## The idea comes from vision

The move was first made with images, and with labels. By 2014, networks trained on ImageNet's million labeled photographs ([the chapter on the deep learning turn](#deep-turn)) were being reused for other problems. Jeff Donahue and colleagues took such a network, froze its weights, and fed its internal activations to an ordinary linear SVM; with only 30 training images per class it reached 86.9% accuracy on a standard set of 101 object categories [@donahue2014decaf, pp. 1, 5]. Another group, feeding "a feature representation of size 4096" from such a network to a linear SVM on a range of tasks, found it beat "the highly tuned state-of-the-art systems in all the visual classification tasks" they tried, and opened their paper with a researcher leaving a coffee room thinking: "Pity I have neither the time, GPU programming skills nor large amount of labelled data to train my own network" [@razavian2014cnn, p. 806].

Two ways of reusing a network came out of this work. Jason Yosinski and colleagues described them: copy the first layers of a trained network into a new one, then either "backpropagate the errors from the new task into the base (copied) features to fine-tune them to the new task, or the transferred feature layers can be left frozen" [@yosinski2014transferable, p. 2]. This is **transfer learning**: "what has been learned in one setting ... is exploited to improve generalization in another setting" [@goodfellow2016deep, p. 534]. The first network is **pretrained**; adjusting its weights on the new task is **fine-tuning**, in a wider sense than in [the chapter on the deep learning turn](#deep-turn), where it meant adjusting all the weights together after layer-by-layer pretraining. Which to choose was a standard trade-off: "If the target dataset is small and the number of parameters is large, fine-tuning may result in overfitting, so the features are often left frozen" [@yosinski2014transferable, p. 2].

Language lagged behind. As one 2017 paper summarized, "Computer vision has benefited from initializing multiple deep layers with weights pretrained on large supervised training sets like ImageNet. Natural language processing (NLP) typically sees initialization of only the lowest layer of deep models with pretrained word vectors" [@mccann2017learned, p. 1]. Those word vectors were the static embeddings of [the embeddings chapter](#embeddings): one vector per word, reused as the first layer of a task-specific model, with everything above it trained from scratch. And there was no ImageNet for language: no single labeled data set big and general enough to pretrain on. The answer turned out to need no labels at all. Large amounts of text, the T5 authors later noted, are "available en masse thanks to the Internet" [@raffel2020exploring, p. 2].

## Pretraining a whole network on text

In 2015, Andrew Dai and Quoc Le proposed using unlabeled text to prepare a whole recurrent network, not just its first layer. Their first method was "to predict what comes next in a sequence, which is a language model in NLP"; the second was a sequence autoencoder, the encoder-decoder of [the chapter on sequences](#seq2seq) trained to reproduce its own input. Either "can be used as a "pretraining" algorithm for a later supervised sequence learning algorithm" [@dai2015semi, p. 1]. Their quotation marks around "pretraining" probably signal a borrowed word, from the 2006 sense of [the deep learning chapter](#deep-turn).

It worked, and it did something else besides. On a set of 25,000 labeled movie reviews, an LSTM trained only on the labels reached a test error of 13.50%; pretrained first as a sequence autoencoder, 7.24% [@dai2015semi, pp. 3-4]. Without pretraining, they wrote, training was unstable: with more hidden units or longer backpropagation through time it "breaks down very quickly"; pretraining "can significantly stabilize the training of LSTMs" [@dai2015semi, pp. 1, 4]. The limit was that the unlabeled text mostly came from the task's own domain: for a second movie-review task they pretrained on 7.9 million movie reviews from Amazon [@dai2015semi, p. 5]. Later authors noted that the method required "millions of in-domain documents to achieve good performance" [@howard2018universal, p. 328].

The 2015-2019 papers called this kind of first stage "unsupervised" and the whole recipe "semi-supervised" [@dai2015semi, p. 1; @radford2018improving, p. 2]. The term used in [the destination chapter](#destination), self-supervised learning, became the common name later. Yann LeCun and Ishan Misra, arguing for it in 2021, wrote that it "is more accepted than the previously used term "unsupervised learning." Unsupervised learning is an ill-defined and misleading term that suggests that the learning uses no supervision at all" [@lecun2021selfsupervised]: the text supplies the answers.

## Vectors that depend on the sentence

The first change that spread widely gave each word a different vector in each sentence. In early 2018, Matthew Peters and colleagues described ELMo, "a new type of deep contextualized word representation" in which "each token is assigned a representation that is a function of the entire input sentence" [@peters2018deep, p. 2227]. They trained two LSTM language models on about 30 million sentences, one reading left to right and one right to left, and used the internal states of both as each word's vector [@peters2018deep, pp. 2228-2230]. Such a vector is a contextual embedding, the term [the attention chapter](#attention) introduced. ELMo was not the first to produce one: a 2017 system called CoVe took them from a translation model's encoder, and the ELMo paper names other predecessors [@mccann2017learned, p. 1; @peters2018deep, p. 2228]. ELMo improved every task it was tried on.

ELMo's authors showed what changed, with the same kind of nearest-neighbor table as [the embeddings chapter](#embeddings). For the word "play", the static GloVe vector's nearest neighbors were "playing, game, games, played, players, plays, player, Play, football, multiplayer": mostly sport. With ELMo's vectors, "play" in a sentence about baseball ("Chico Ruiz made a spectacular play on Alusik 's grounder") had as its nearest neighbor another sports sentence, while "play" in a sentence about the theater ("Olivia De Havilland signed to do a Broadway play") had as its nearest neighbor a sentence about actors in "a successful play" [@peters2018deep, p. 2233]. The one-vector-per-word weakness, named by Deerwester in 1990, was fixed first with LSTMs. [The previous chapters](#attention) showed how transformers do the same; transformers made it standard.

Added to existing models for six tasks, from question answering to identifying named entities, ELMo set a new best result on every one [@peters2018deep, p. 2230]. <!-- D-009: code/checks/pretraining.py, Example 5 --> The gains are reported as **relative error reduction**: the share of the remaining error removed. On a question-answering benchmark, the F1 score rose from 81.1 to 85.8: 4.7 points, but 24.9% of the 18.9 points that were still missing [@peters2018deep, p. 2230]. ELMo also made labels go further: one model with ELMo trained on 1% of its training set did about as well as the model without it trained on 10% [@peters2018deep, p. 2234].

## Fine-tune the whole network

ELMo was used as a set of extra features: its language models were usually adapted to a task's own text, then frozen, and a separate task model was trained on top. Around the same time, in January 2018, Jeremy Howard and Sebastian Ruder argued for the other way, the one vision had shown: fine-tune the whole pretrained network. Their method, ULMFiT, had three stages: pretrain an LSTM language model on 103 million words of Wikipedia, fine-tune it as a language model on the task's own text, and then fine-tune it with a small classifier on the task's labels [@howard2018universal, p. 330]. They described language modeling as "the ideal source task and a counterpart of ImageNet for NLP", because, unlike translation or other labeled tasks, "it provides data in near-unlimited quantities for most domains and languages" [@howard2018universal, p. 329].

The difficulty was that fine-tuning could destroy what pretraining had learned, which they called "catastrophic forgetting" [@howard2018universal, p. 328]. Their remedies were small, careful changes to the training loop: lower learning rates for lower layers, a learning-rate schedule that rises briefly and then decays (the warmup of [the toolkit chapter](#toolkit)), and unfreezing the layers one at a time from the top [@howard2018universal, pp. 330-332]. On six text classification tasks they reduced the error "by 18-24% on the majority of datasets" [@howard2018universal, p. 328]. <!-- D-009: code/checks/pretraining.py, Example 5 --> Against the previous best on each data set, the reductions were 22.0%, 23.7%, and 18.2% on three of them, and between 2% and 8% on the other three. Their most striking claim was about labels: on a movie-review task, with only 100 labeled examples, ULMFiT matched a model trained from scratch on 10 times as many labeled examples, or on 100 times as many if it was also allowed to fine-tune its language model on 50,000 unlabeled reviews from the task [@howard2018universal, pp. 329, 334].

## GPT: a transformer, pretrained

In June 2018, Alec Radford and colleagues at OpenAI put the transformer of [the attention chapter](#attention) into this recipe. Their paper argued that "large gains on these tasks can be realized by generative pre-training of a language model on a diverse corpus of unlabeled text, followed by discriminative fine-tuning on each specific task" [@radford2018improving, p. 1]. The model was the decoder-only transformer of [the anatomy chapter](#anatomy), 12 blocks of width 768, trained to predict the next token on the BooksCorpus, "over 7,000 unique unpublished books", which, the authors noted, "contains long stretches of contiguous text" [@radford2018improving, pp. 4-5]. OpenAI's announcement gave the cost: "an expensive pre-training step - 1 month on 8 GPUs. Luckily, this only has to be done once and we're releasing our model so others can avoid it" [@openai2018language]. GPT-2's paper later listed the size of this first GPT as 117 million parameters [@radford2019language, p. 4].

To fine-tune, GPT added only a final output layer and a few special tokens. Every task was turned into one sequence of tokens: for deciding whether one sentence follows from another, the two sentences were joined with a delimiter token between them [@radford2018improving, pp. 3-4]. The same pretrained model, with a few epochs of fine-tuning ("3 epochs of training was sufficient for most cases"), improved the state of the art "in 9 out of the 12 tasks studied" [@radford2018improving, pp. 1, 5]. It was not best everywhere: on one of the smallest data sets, 2,490 examples of textual entailment, it scored 56% against 61.7% for an earlier model [@radford2018improving, p. 5].

The paper also measured what pretraining was worth. <!-- D-009: code/checks/pretraining.py, Example 6 --> The same transformer trained only on the task data, with no pretraining, averaged 59.9 on their set of tasks against 74.7 with it: "the lack of pre-training hurts performance across all the tasks" [@radford2018improving, p. 8]. That is 14.8 points, which the paper reports as "a 14.8% decrease"; relative to 74.7 it is 19.8%. Replacing the transformer with an LSTM, with pretraining, cost 5.6 points [@radford2018improving, p. 8].

One more observation pointed ahead. The authors tried using the pretrained model with no fine-tuning at all. For sentiment, they appended the word "very" to each review and compared the probabilities the model gave to the next word being "positive" or "negative" [@radford2018improving, p. 8]. Its performance on such tasks "steadily increases over training" [@radford2018improving, p. 8]. Asking a language model for the next word as a way of doing a task is the seed of prompting, which [the chapter on scale](#scale) takes up.

## BERT: reading in both directions

In October 2018, Jacob Devlin and colleagues at Google posted BERT. Their criticism of GPT was its direction: "standard language models are unidirectional ... in OpenAI GPT, the authors use a left-to-right architecture, where every token can only attend to previous tokens in the self-attention layers of the Transformer" [@devlin2019bert, p. 4171]. For a task like question answering, they argued, "it is crucial to incorporate context from both directions". ELMo had both directions, but as two separate models joined at the end, "a shallow concatenation" [@devlin2019bert, p. 4172]. BERT wanted one model that used both sides in every layer.

That creates a problem. A model that predicts each word while seeing the words on both sides can, through its layers, look at the word it is predicting: "bidirectional conditioning would allow each word to indirectly "see itself"" [@devlin2019bert, p. 4174]. The solution, a **masked language model**, was an old idea, the cloze test of filling in blanks: "we simply mask some percentage of the input tokens at random, and then predict those masked tokens. We refer to this procedure as a "masked LM" (MLM)" [@devlin2019bert, p. 4174]. Google's announcement called it a technique that "has been around for a very long time" [@devlin2018open].

BERT chose 15% of the token positions in each sequence for prediction. Of those, 80% were replaced by a special [MASK] token ([the chapter on tokens](#tokens)), 10% by a random token, and 10% left unchanged, so that the model could not rely on seeing [MASK] at fine-tuning time, where it never appears [@devlin2019bert, p. 4174]. On a 512-token input that is, on average, about 77 positions predicted, against 512 for a next-token model, which is why the authors expected masked training to converge more slowly [@devlin2019bert, p. 4183]. The precise 80/10/10 recipe mattered little: across six variants, one benchmark's accuracy after fine-tuning ranged over less than one point [@devlin2019bert, p. 4186]. BERT also had a second training task, **next-sentence prediction**: given two passages, guess whether the second really follows the first, which is the case half the time [@devlin2019bert, p. 4174].

BERT-base matched GPT's size, 12 layers of width 768, so that the two could be compared; BERT-large had 24 layers of width 1,024 and 340 million parameters [@devlin2019bert, p. 4173]. Both were trained on the BooksCorpus and English Wikipedia, 3.3 billion words in all, for a million steps [@devlin2019bert, pp. 4175, 4183]. <!-- D-009: code/checks/pretraining.py, Examples 3 and 4 --> Each pretraining run "took 4 days to complete", on 16 of Google's TPU chips for BERT-base and 64 for BERT-large [@devlin2019bert, p. 4183]. Fine-tuning, by contrast, was "relatively inexpensive. All of the results in the paper can be replicated in at most 1 hour on a single Cloud TPU, or a few hours on a GPU" [@devlin2019bert, p. 4175]. Counted in chip-hours (derived from the papers' figures, taking a Cloud TPU to be four chips), BERT-large's pretraining cost at least 1,500 times as much as any one fine-tuning. And fine-tuning added almost nothing to the model: one output layer, "K x H" new weights for K labels and width H [@devlin2019bert, p. 4175], 2,304 numbers for a three-way classification on BERT-base, against 110 million.

The results moved the field. BERT reported new best results on eleven tasks, including "pushing the GLUE score to 80.5% (7.7% point absolute improvement)" [@devlin2019bert, p. 4171], where GLUE is a benchmark that averages nine English sentence-understanding tasks [@wang2019glue, p. 3; @devlin2019bert, p. 4176]. The authors' own ablations, experiments that remove one part and measure the drop, backed both of their design choices. <!-- D-009: code/checks/pretraining.py, Example 6 --> A left-to-right version, compared with the same model also trained without next-sentence prediction, did worse on every task, by 10.1 points on question answering and 9.0 on one paraphrase task; removing next-sentence prediction cost 3.5 points on one task and 0.6 or less on the others [@devlin2019bert, p. 4178].

Those findings did not all last. In 2019, Yinhan Liu and colleagues found that "BERT was significantly undertrained", and that removing next-sentence prediction "matches or slightly improves downstream task performance", when the inputs were long passages from a single document [@liu2019roberta, pp. 1, 5]. Their RoBERTa, the same architecture trained longer on ten times as much text, matched or beat every model published after BERT [@liu2019roberta, pp. 1, 3]. Another 2019 paper suggested that next-sentence prediction had been too easy: a random second passage is usually on a different topic, so the model could solve the task by noticing the change of topic [@lan2020albert, pp. 4-5]. Whether the second task helps depends on how it is set up.

## Two families

By 2019 there were two main kinds of pretrained transformer, besides encoder-decoders such as T5 below: decoder-only models trained to predict the next token, like GPT, and encoder-only models trained to fill in blanks, like BERT. The difference is the mask of [the anatomy chapter](#anatomy), and it decides what each can do. BERT's authors noted that the left-to-right kind "is referred to as a "Transformer decoder" since it can be used for text generation" [@devlin2019bert, p. 4174]. A BERT-style model, on the other hand, is "applicable for classification or span prediction tasks but not for generative tasks like translation or abstractive summarization" [@raffel2020exploring, p. 10]: it can fill in a blank, but it does not write a text one token after another.

On understanding tasks after fine-tuning, both-sides models did better in 2018 and 2019, and their authors' ablations showed it. OpenAI stayed with left-to-right models anyway. The GPT-3 paper explained why in 2020: they focused on such models "because it is straightforward to both sample and compute likelihoods with this model class", accepting "the cost of potentially worse performance on tasks which empirically benefit from bidirectionality", and conjectured "that a large bidirectional model would be stronger at fine-tuning than GPT-3" [@brown2020language, p. 33]. As of a 2026-08 textbook draft, the left-to-right decoder "is the architecture used to create large language models like GPT, Claude, Llama, and Mistral", while models of the BERT family "are not generative models" and are typically fine-tuned as classifiers [@jurafsky2026masked, pp. 1-2].

## T5: every task as text

The most systematic comparison came from Colin Raffel and colleagues, who described transfer learning in one sentence: "a model is first pre-trained on a data-rich task before being fine-tuned on a downstream task" [@raffel2020exploring, p. 1]. Their T5 treated "every text processing problem as a "text-to-text" problem, i.e. taking text as input and producing new text as output" [@raffel2020exploring, p. 2]. To translate, the model is given "translate English to German: That is good." and trained to output "Das ist gut."; to classify, it outputs the label as a word [@raffel2020exploring, pp. 7-8]. One model, one objective, any task. They pretrained on a new collection of filtered web text, C4, of "about 750 GB" [@raffel2020exploring, p. 7]; [the chapter on training](#training) describes how such collections are built.

Their comparisons, at about the size of BERT-base and as of 2019, favored filling in blanks over predicting the next token: "using a denoising objective always results in better downstream task performance compared to a language modeling objective" [@raffel2020exploring, pp. 19-20]. Their final version hid short spans of text, about 15% of the tokens, and trained the model to output only the missing pieces [@raffel2020exploring, p. 36]. <!-- D-009: code/checks/pretraining.py, Example 7 --> On 512 tokens, that makes a target of about 103 tokens, instead of the whole 512-token input that a model reconstructing the entire text would write out, which made pretraining "more computationally efficient" [@raffel2020exploring, p. 41]. They also confirmed the pattern every paper in this chapter had seen: pretraining helped on almost every benchmark, except translation from English to French, a task with so much labeled data that "gains from pre-training tend to be marginal" [@raffel2020exploring, p. 14].

## Who started it?

Reusing what a network learned from raw text was not new in 2018. A 2016 textbook credits word-level pretraining in language to Collobert and Weston in 2008 and to work in 2010 and 2011 [@goodfellow2016deep, p. 533], and [the embeddings chapter](#embeddings) told that story. Howard and Ruder wrote that "Dai and Le (2015) first proposed fine-tuning a language model" [@howard2018universal, p. 328], and OpenAI's announcement said GPT "builds on" Dai and Le's approach and "extends ULMFiT" [@openai2018language]. A current textbook (as of its 2026-08 draft) calls ELMo "perhaps the moment when it became clear to the community that language models could be used as a general solution for NLP problems" [@jurafsky2026transformers, p. 31]; a 2021 report saw "a sociological inflection point around the introduction of BERT", after which "using BERT has become the norm" [@bommasani2021opportunities, p. 5]. A fair summary: the ideas were in place by 2015; what changed in 2018 was that pretraining worked across many tasks at once, first with LSTMs (ELMo, ULMFiT) and then with transformers (GPT, BERT).

## Where it helps, and what it costs

Pretraining did not remove the need for labels. Every model in this chapter was fine-tuned on labeled examples for each task. As the GPT-2 paper put it in 2019, "These methods still require supervised training in order to perform a task" [@radford2019language, p. 2]. What changed was how many labels were needed. Pretraining helped most where labeled data was scarce: ULMFiT's authors found it "most useful for small and medium-sized datasets" [@howard2018universal, p. 334], ELMo's gains were "largest for smaller training sets" [@peters2018deep, p. 2234], and T5's only exception was a task with abundant labels [@raffel2020exploring, p. 14]. That is the old reasoning about overfitting, applied at a new scale.

The economics also changed. Pretraining was expensive and done once; fine-tuning was cheap and done for every task. The model trained once could be shared. OpenAI released GPT "so others can avoid" the month of pretraining [@openai2018language], and Google's announcement of BERT said that "anyone in the world can train their own state-of-the-art question answering system ... in about 30 minutes on a single Cloud TPU" [@devlin2018open]. The next step, taken in 2019 and 2020, asked whether a model made large enough would need the fine-tuning at all. That is [the chapter on scale](#scale).

## What to carry forward

- The 2000s rule was one model per task, trained from scratch on that task's labels. Vision broke it first, around 2014, by reusing networks trained on ImageNet.
- Pretraining trains one network on a large amount of text with a task the text supplies itself, such as predicting the next word. Fine-tuning then adapts it to a real task with far fewer labeled examples than training from scratch would need.
- Dai and Le (2015) pretrained whole LSTMs; ELMo (2018) gave words vectors that depend on their sentence; ULMFiT (2018) showed how to fine-tune a whole language model without destroying it.
- GPT (2018) pretrained a decoder-only transformer to predict the next token. BERT (2018) pretrained an encoder to fill in masked words using both sides.
- Both-sides models fine-tuned better on understanding tasks in 2018 and 2019; left-to-right models can generate text and score whole texts, and became the basis of large language models.
- Pretraining is expensive and done once; fine-tuning is cheap and done per task. Pretraining helps most when labels are scarce.
- The first stage was called "unsupervised" at the time; "self-supervised" became the common name later.

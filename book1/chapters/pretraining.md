# Pretraining: learn language first, tasks later (2015-2019) {#pretraining}

The working rule of the 2000s was simple: to build a classifier for a task, collect labeled examples of that task. The assumption behind it, as a 2010 survey of the field put it, was that training and test data come from the same feature space and the same distribution; many methods worked well only under that assumption, and when the distribution shifted, most statistical models had to be trained again from the start on newly collected data [@pan2010survey, p. 1]. Labels were the expensive part. A sentiment classifier needed thousands of reviews marked positive or negative; a question-answering system needed thousands of questions with their answers.

This chapter is about how that rule stopped holding for language, between 2015 and 2019. The new recipe had two stages. First, train one large network on a huge amount of unlabeled text, with a task that the text supplies for free, such as predicting the next word. Then adapt that network to each real task with a small set of labeled examples. [The chapter on the deep learning turn](#deep-turn) closed its account of pretraining with a textbook's observation, as of 2016, that unsupervised pretraining had been largely abandoned except in natural language processing. This is where that exception led.

## The idea comes from vision

The move was first made with images, and with labels. By 2014, networks trained on ImageNet's million labeled photographs ([the chapter on the deep learning turn](#deep-turn)) were being reused for other problems. One 2014 study took such a network, froze its weights, and fed its internal activations to an ordinary linear SVM; with only 30 training images per class it reached 86.9% accuracy on a standard set of 101 object categories [@donahue2014decaf, pp. 1, 5]. Another group fed 4,096 numbers taken from one layer of such a network to a linear SVM, on tasks from scene recognition to fine-grained recognition, and reported results consistently better than carefully tuned state-of-the-art systems on every visual classification task they tried. Their paper opens on a researcher leaving a coffee room, regretting having neither the time, the GPU programming skills, nor enough labeled data to train a network of their own [@razavian2014cnn, p. 806].

Two ways of reusing a network came out of this work. A third 2014 study described them: copy the first layers of a trained network into a new one, then either fine-tune the copied features, by backpropagating the new task's errors into them, or leave them frozen, unchanged while the new task is trained [@yosinski2014transferable, p. 2]. This is **transfer learning**: using what was learned in one setting to improve generalization in another [@goodfellow2016deep, p. 534]. The first network is **pretrained**; adjusting its weights on the new task is **fine-tuning**, in a wider sense than in [the chapter on the deep learning turn](#deep-turn), where it meant adjusting all the weights together after layer-by-layer pretraining. Which to choose was a standard trade-off: with a small target data set and many parameters, fine-tuning may overfit, so the features were often left frozen [@yosinski2014transferable, p. 2].

Language lagged behind. As of 2017, vision had benefited from starting several deep layers with weights pretrained on large labeled sets such as ImageNet, while deep models for language typically started only their lowest layer from pretrained word vectors [@mccann2017learned, p. 1]. Those word vectors were the static embeddings of [the embeddings chapter](#embeddings): one vector per word, reused as the first layer of a task-specific model, with everything above it trained from scratch. And there was no ImageNet for language: no single labeled data set big and general enough to pretrain on. The answer turned out to need no labels at all. Unlabeled text was available in bulk, thanks to the Internet [@raffel2020exploring, p. 2].

## Pretraining a whole network on text

In 2015, Andrew Dai and Quoc Le proposed using unlabeled text to prepare a whole recurrent network, not just its first layer. The first trained it as a language model, predicting each next word; the second was a sequence autoencoder, the encoder-decoder of [the chapter on sequences](#seq2seq) trained to reproduce its own input. Either, they wrote, could serve as a "pretraining" step: a later supervised model would start its training from the parameters it had learned [@dai2015semi, p. 1]. Their quotation marks around "pretraining" probably signal a borrowed word, from the 2006 sense of [the deep learning chapter](#deep-turn). Whether the recipe began here, earlier, or in 2018 depends on what is counted, and [the credit appendix](#credit-pretraining) sets out the accounts.

It worked, and it did something else besides. On a set of 25,000 labeled movie reviews, an LSTM trained only on the labels reached a test error of 13.50%; pretrained first as a sequence autoencoder, 7.24% [@dai2015semi, pp. 3-4]. Without pretraining, training was unstable: with more hidden units or longer backpropagation through time, it quickly broke down, while even a simple pretraining step could make the training of LSTMs much more stable [@dai2015semi, pp. 1, 4]. The limit was that the unlabeled text mostly came from the task's own domain: for a second movie-review task they pretrained on 7.9 million movie reviews from Amazon [@dai2015semi, p. 5]. The method, later authors pointed out, worked well only with millions of documents from the task's own domain [@howard2018universal, p. 328].

The 2015-2019 papers called this kind of first stage "unsupervised" and the whole recipe "semi-supervised" [@dai2015semi, p. 1; @radford2018improving, p. 2]. The term used in [the destination chapter](#destination), self-supervised learning, became the common name later. In 2021, Yann LeCun and a coauthor, arguing for the term, wrote that it was more accepted than "unsupervised learning", which they called ill-defined and misleading because it suggests that no supervision is used at all [@lecun2021selfsupervised]: the text supplies the answers.

## Vectors that depend on the sentence

The first change that spread widely gave each word a different vector in each sentence. In early 2018, a system called ELMo assigned each token a representation computed from the entire sentence it appeared in [@peters2018deep, p. 2227]. Its authors trained two LSTM language models on about 30 million sentences, one reading left to right and one right to left, and used the internal states of both as each word's vector [@peters2018deep, pp. 2228-2230]. Such a vector is a contextual embedding, the term [the attention chapter](#attention) introduced. In 2017, a system called CoVe had taken such vectors from the encoder of a translation model [@mccann2017learned, p. 1]; [the credit appendix](#credit-pretraining) sets out who else had produced them. ELMo improved every task it was tried on.

ELMo's authors showed what changed, with the same kind of nearest-neighbor table as [the embeddings chapter](#embeddings). For the word "play", the static GloVe vector's nearest neighbors were "playing, game, games, played, players, plays, player, Play, football, multiplayer": mostly sport. With ELMo's vectors, "play" in a sentence about baseball ("Chico Ruiz made a spectacular play on Alusik 's grounder") had as its nearest neighbor another sports sentence, while "play" in a sentence about the theater ("Olivia De Havilland signed to do a Broadway play") had as its nearest neighbor a sentence about actors in "a successful play" [@peters2018deep, p. 2233]. The one-vector-per-word weakness, named in 1990 ([the embeddings chapter](#embeddings)), was fixed first with LSTMs. [The previous chapters](#attention) showed how transformers do the same; transformers made it standard.

Added to existing models for six tasks, from question answering to identifying named entities, ELMo set a new best result on every one [@peters2018deep, p. 2230]. <!-- D-009: code/checks/pretraining.py, Example 5 --> The gains are reported as **relative error reduction**: the share of the remaining error removed. On a question-answering benchmark, the F1 score rose from 81.1 to 85.8: 4.7 points, but 24.9% of the 18.9 points that were still missing [@peters2018deep, p. 2230]. ELMo also made labels go further: one model with ELMo trained on 1% of its training set did about as well as the model without it trained on 10% [@peters2018deep, p. 2234].

## Fine-tune the whole network

ELMo was used as a set of extra features: its language models were usually adapted to a task's own text, then frozen, and a separate task model was trained on top. Around the same time, in January 2018, ULMFiT took the other way, the one vision had shown: fine-tune the whole pretrained network. It had three stages: pretrain an LSTM language model on 103 million words of Wikipedia, fine-tune it as a language model on the task's own text, and then fine-tune it with a small classifier on the task's labels [@howard2018universal, p. 330]. Its authors argued that language modeling could serve as language's ImageNet, the ideal task to pretrain on, because, unlike translation or other labeled tasks, it offers almost unlimited data in most domains and languages [@howard2018universal, p. 329].

The difficulty was that fine-tuning could destroy what pretraining had learned, which they called "catastrophic forgetting" [@howard2018universal, p. 328]. Their remedies were small, careful changes to the training loop: lower learning rates for lower layers, a learning-rate schedule that rises briefly and then decays (the warmup of [the toolkit chapter](#toolkit)), and unfreezing the layers one at a time from the top [@howard2018universal, pp. 330-332]. On six text classification tasks they reduced the error "by 18-24% on the majority of datasets" [@howard2018universal, p. 328]. <!-- D-009: code/checks/pretraining.py, Example 5 --> Against the previous best on each data set, the reductions were 22.0%, 23.7%, and 18.2% on three of them, and between 2% and 8% on the other three. Their most striking claim was about labels: on a movie-review task, with only 100 labeled examples, ULMFiT matched a model trained from scratch on 10 times as many labeled examples, or on 100 times as many if it was also allowed to fine-tune its language model on 50,000 unlabeled reviews from the task [@howard2018universal, pp. 329, 334].

## GPT: a transformer, pretrained

In June 2018, OpenAI put the transformer of [the attention chapter](#attention) into this recipe. Its paper argued that large gains could come from two stages: "generative pre-training" of a language model on varied unlabeled text, then "discriminative fine-tuning" for each task [@radford2018improving, p. 1]. The model was the decoder-only transformer of [the anatomy chapter](#anatomy), 12 blocks of width 768, trained to predict the next token on the BooksCorpus, more than 7,000 unpublished books, which, the authors noted, hold long unbroken passages of text [@radford2018improving, pp. 4-5]. OpenAI's announcement called pretraining the expensive step, a month on 8 GPUs, needed only once, and said the model was being released so that others could skip it [@openai2018language]. GPT-2's paper later listed the size of this first GPT as 117 million parameters [@radford2019language, p. 4].

To fine-tune, GPT added only a final output layer and a few special tokens. Every task was turned into one sequence of tokens: for deciding whether one sentence follows from another, the two sentences were joined with a delimiter token between them [@radford2018improving, pp. 3-4]. The same pretrained model, with a few epochs of fine-tuning (three were enough in most cases), improved the state of the art in 9 of the 12 tasks studied [@radford2018improving, pp. 1, 5]. It was not best everywhere: on one of the smallest data sets, 2,490 examples of textual entailment, it scored 56% against 61.7% for an earlier model [@radford2018improving, p. 5].

The paper also measured what pretraining was worth. <!-- D-009: code/checks/pretraining.py, Example 6 --> The same transformer trained only on the task data, with no pretraining, averaged 59.9 on their set of tasks against 74.7 with it, and did worse on every task [@radford2018improving, p. 8]. That is 14.8 points, which the paper reports as "a 14.8% decrease"; relative to 74.7 it is 19.8%. Replacing the transformer with an LSTM, with pretraining, cost 5.6 points [@radford2018improving, p. 8].

One more observation pointed ahead. The authors tried using the pretrained model with no fine-tuning at all. For sentiment, they appended the word "very" to each review and compared the probabilities the model gave to the next word being "positive" or "negative" [@radford2018improving, p. 8]. Its performance on such tasks rose steadily over the course of pretraining [@radford2018improving, p. 8]. Asking a language model for the next word as a way of doing a task is the seed of prompting, which [the chapter on scale](#scale) takes up.

## BERT: reading in both directions

In October 2018, a team at Google posted BERT. Their objection to GPT was its direction: standard language models read one way only; in GPT, for example, every token could attend only to the tokens before it, and for tasks such as question answering, they argued, context from both directions is crucial [@devlin2019bert, p. 4171]. ELMo had both directions, but as two separate models joined at the end, "a shallow concatenation" [@devlin2019bert, p. 4172]. BERT wanted one model that used both sides in every layer.

That creates a problem. A model that predicts each word while seeing the words on both sides can, through its layers, look at the word it is predicting: each word could indirectly "see itself" [@devlin2019bert, p. 4174]. The solution was the **masked language model**: hide a random share of the input tokens and train the model to predict them. The authors called the procedure a "masked LM" (MLM), noting that the literature often calls it a cloze task, the test of filling in blanks [@devlin2019bert, p. 4174]. It was an old idea; Google's announcement said it had been around for a very long time [@devlin2018open].

BERT chose 15% of the token positions in each sequence for prediction. Of those, 80% were replaced by a special [MASK] token ([the chapter on tokens](#tokens)), 10% by a random token, and 10% left unchanged, so that the model could not rely on seeing [MASK] at fine-tuning time, where it never appears [@devlin2019bert, p. 4174]. On a 512-token input that is, on average, about 77 positions predicted, against 512 for a next-token model, which is why the authors expected masked training to converge more slowly [@devlin2019bert, p. 4183]. The precise 80/10/10 recipe mattered little: across six variants, one benchmark's accuracy after fine-tuning ranged over less than one point [@devlin2019bert, p. 4186]. BERT also had a second training task, **next-sentence prediction**: given two passages, guess whether the second really follows the first, which is the case half the time [@devlin2019bert, p. 4174].

BERT-base matched GPT's size, 12 layers of width 768, so that the two could be compared; BERT-large had 24 layers of width 1,024 and 340 million parameters [@devlin2019bert, p. 4173]. Both were trained on the BooksCorpus and English Wikipedia, 3.3 billion words in all, for a million steps [@devlin2019bert, pp. 4175, 4183]. <!-- D-009: code/checks/pretraining.py, Examples 3 and 4 --> Each pretraining run took four days, on 16 of Google's TPU chips for BERT-base and 64 for BERT-large [@devlin2019bert, p. 4183]. Fine-tuning, by contrast, cost comparatively little: any result in the paper could be reproduced within an hour on one Cloud TPU, or within a few hours on a GPU [@devlin2019bert, p. 4175]. Counted in chip-hours (derived from the papers' figures, taking a Cloud TPU to be four chips), BERT-large's pretraining cost at least 1,500 times as much as any one fine-tuning. And fine-tuning added almost nothing to the model: one output layer of K x H new weights, for K labels and width H [@devlin2019bert, p. 4175], 2,304 numbers for a three-way classification on BERT-base, against 110 million.

The results moved the field. BERT reported new best results on eleven tasks, including a GLUE score of 80.5%, 7.7 points above the previous best [@devlin2019bert, p. 4171], where GLUE is a benchmark that averages nine English sentence-understanding tasks [@wang2019glue, p. 3; @devlin2019bert, p. 4176]. The authors' own ablations, experiments that remove one part and measure the drop, backed both of their design choices. <!-- D-009: code/checks/pretraining.py, Example 6 --> A left-to-right version, compared with the same model also trained without next-sentence prediction, did worse on every task, by 10.1 points on question answering and 9.0 on one paraphrase task; removing next-sentence prediction cost 3.5 points on one task and 0.6 or less on the others [@devlin2019bert, p. 4178].

Those findings did not all last. In 2019, a replication study found BERT "significantly undertrained", and found that dropping next-sentence prediction did no harm on downstream tasks, and sometimes helped a little, when the inputs were long passages from a single document [@liu2019roberta, pp. 1, 5]. Its model, RoBERTa, the same architecture trained longer on ten times as much text, matched or beat every model published after BERT [@liu2019roberta, pp. 1, 3]. Another 2019 paper suggested that next-sentence prediction had been too easy: a random second passage is usually on a different topic, so the model could solve the task by noticing the change of topic [@lan2020albert, pp. 4-5]. Whether the second task helps depends on how it is set up.

## Two families

By 2019 there were two main kinds of pretrained transformer, besides encoder-decoders such as T5 below: decoder-only models trained to predict the next token, like GPT, and encoder-only models trained to fill in blanks, like BERT. The difference is the mask of [the anatomy chapter](#anatomy), and it decides what each can do. BERT's authors noted that in the literature the left-to-right kind is called a "Transformer decoder", since it can be used to generate text [@devlin2019bert, p. 4174]. A BERT-style model, on the other hand, suits classification or picking out a span of the input, but not tasks that produce new text, such as translation or abstractive summarization [@raffel2020exploring, p. 10]: it can fill in a blank, but it does not write a text one token after another.

On understanding tasks after fine-tuning, both-sides models did better in 2018 and 2019, and their authors' ablations showed it. OpenAI stayed with left-to-right models anyway. The GPT-3 paper gave its reason in 2020: it studied such models because they make it straightforward both to sample text and to compute its likelihood. Its authors granted that this might cost them performance on tasks that benefit from reading both ways, and conjectured that a large bidirectional model would fine-tune better than GPT-3 [@brown2020language, p. 33]. As of a 2026-08 textbook draft, the left-to-right decoder is the architecture behind large language models such as GPT, Claude, Llama, and Mistral, while models of the BERT family are not generative and are often fine-tuned as classifiers [@jurafsky2026masked, pp. 1-2].

## T5: every task as text

The most systematic comparison came in 2019 with T5, whose paper described transfer learning as training a model first on a task with plenty of data and then fine-tuning it on a downstream task [@raffel2020exploring, p. 1]. T5 treated every text-processing problem as a "text-to-text" problem: text goes in, and new text comes out [@raffel2020exploring, p. 2]. To translate, the model is given "translate English to German: That is good." and trained to output "Das ist gut."; to classify, it outputs the label as a word [@raffel2020exploring, pp. 7-8]. One model, one objective, any task. Its authors pretrained on a new collection of filtered web text, C4, of about 750 GB [@raffel2020exploring, p. 7]; [the chapter on training](#training) describes how such collections are built.

Their comparisons, at about the size of BERT-base and as of 2019, favored filling in blanks over predicting the next token: in their tests, a denoising (fill-in-the-blank) objective always gave better results on downstream tasks than a language-modeling one [@raffel2020exploring, pp. 19-20]. Their final version hid short spans of text, about 15% of the tokens, and trained the model to output only the missing pieces [@raffel2020exploring, p. 36]. <!-- D-009: code/checks/pretraining.py, Example 7 --> On 512 tokens, that makes a target of about 103 tokens, instead of the whole 512-token input that a model reconstructing the entire text would write out; short targets, the authors suggested, make pretraining more computationally efficient [@raffel2020exploring, p. 41]. They also confirmed the pattern every paper in this chapter had seen: pretraining helped on almost every benchmark, except translation from English to French, a data set so large that pretraining tends to add little [@raffel2020exploring, p. 14].

## Where it helps, and what it costs

Pretraining did not remove the need for labels. Every model in this chapter was fine-tuned on labeled examples for each task. As the GPT-2 paper observed in 2019, these methods still had to be trained on labeled examples before they could perform a task [@radford2019language, p. 2]. What changed was how many labels were needed. Pretraining helped most where labeled data was scarce: ULMFiT's authors found that it helped most on small and medium-sized data sets [@howard2018universal, p. 334], ELMo's improvements were largest when the training set was smaller [@peters2018deep, p. 2234], and T5's only exception was a task with abundant labels [@raffel2020exploring, p. 14]. That is the old reasoning about overfitting, applied at a new scale.

The economics also changed. Pretraining was expensive and done once; fine-tuning was cheap and done for every task. The model trained once could be shared. OpenAI released GPT so that others could skip the month of pretraining [@openai2018language], and Google's announcement of BERT said its release would let anyone train a state-of-the-art question-answering system in about 30 minutes on one Cloud TPU [@devlin2018open]. The next step, taken in 2019 and 2020, asked whether a model made large enough would need the fine-tuning at all. That is [the chapter on scale](#scale).

## What to carry forward

- The 2000s rule was one model per task, trained from scratch on that task's labels. Vision broke it first, around 2014, by reusing networks trained on ImageNet.
- Pretraining trains one network on a large amount of text with a task the text supplies itself, such as predicting the next word. Fine-tuning then adapts it to a real task with far fewer labeled examples than training from scratch would need.
- Dai and Le (2015) pretrained whole LSTMs; ELMo (2018) gave words vectors that depend on their sentence; ULMFiT (2018) showed how to fine-tune a whole language model without destroying it.
- GPT (2018) pretrained a decoder-only transformer to predict the next token. BERT (2018) pretrained an encoder to fill in masked words using both sides.
- Both-sides models fine-tuned better on understanding tasks in 2018 and 2019; left-to-right models can generate text and score whole texts, and became the basis of large language models.
- Pretraining is expensive and done once; fine-tuning is cheap and done per task. Pretraining helps most when labels are scarce.
- The first stage was called "unsupervised" at the time; "self-supervised" became the common name later.

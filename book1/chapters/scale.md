# Scale (2019-2022) {#scale}

<!-- D-002: intuition only; two formulas (C = 6ND, a power law), every symbol explained. D-003: no code. D-009: numbers from code/checks/scale.py. D-014: figures from figures/scale.py. -->

[The previous chapter](#pretraining) ended with a recipe: pretrain one large model on unlabeled text, then fine-tune it for each task with a small set of labeled examples. This chapter is about what happened when the first half of that recipe was simply made bigger. Between 2019 and 2022, the largest language models grew from about a billion parameters to several hundred billion, and three things were learned along the way. A large enough model could do some tasks with no fine-tuning at all, given only a description or a few examples in its input. The loss of a language model fell along a strikingly regular curve as models, data, and computation grew. And the first rule for how to spend a computing budget had to be revised in an important way.

Three quantities run through the chapter. **Parameters** are the learned numbers of [the anatomy chapter](#anatomy). **Training tokens** are how many tokens of text the model reads during training. **Compute** is the total arithmetic done in training, counted in **FLOPs**, floating-point operations: each multiplication or addition counts as one.

## Bigger has helped before

The reader's own field knew that more data helps. In 2007, a Google team published a paper titled "Large Language Models in Machine Translation", about n-gram models of [the prediction chapter](#prediction), trained on "up to 2 trillion tokens" [@brants2007large, p. 858]. Its findings will look familiar by the end of this chapter. "As a general rule, more data tends to yield better language models" [@brants2007large, p. 858]. Translation quality improved with every doubling of the training data, by about 0.6 BLEU points per doubling at first and 0.15 at the largest sizes [@brants2007large, p. 866], and when the authors plotted their models' size against the amount of data on logarithmic axes, "The plots are very close to straight lines in the log/log space" [@brants2007large, pp. 864, 866].

Neural networks added computation to the list. In 2018, OpenAI estimated that "since 2012, the amount of compute used in the largest AI training runs has been increasing exponentially with a 3.5 month-doubling time", more than 300,000 times in all [@amodei2018ai]. <!-- D-009: code/checks/scale.py, Example 7 --> The post's chart gives a doubling every 3.43 months, a factor of about 11 per year; the post itself rounds this to "roughly a factor of 10 each year" [@amodei2018ai]. That run of years began with AlexNet in [the chapter on the deep learning turn](#deep-turn), trained on two GPUs.

## GPT-2: tasks without task training

In February 2019, OpenAI described GPT-2, the decoder-only model of [the anatomy chapter](#anatomy) at four sizes, the largest with 1.5 billion parameters [@radford2019language, p. 4]. It was trained on WebText, built from about 45 million links posted on the social media site Reddit; the version used in the paper, without links after December 2017 and after removing duplicates and cleaning, held "slightly over 8 million documents for a total of 40 GB of text" [@radford2019language, p. 3]. The paper does not say how many tokens the models were trained on.

The paper's surprise was what the model could do without fine-tuning. "We demonstrate language models can perform down-stream tasks in a zero-shot setting - without any parameter or architecture modification" [@radford2019language, p. 2]. Given a document and a question, GPT-2 could write an answer; given a few English-French sentence pairs and a new English sentence, it could attempt a French translation [@radford2019language, p. 6]. The results were uneven. On a question-answering test about a passage, it reached 55 F1, "matching or exceeding the performance of 3 out of 4 baseline systems without using the 127,000+ training examples" [@radford2019language, p. 1]. On English-to-French translation it scored 5 BLEU [@radford2019language, p. 6], far below the systems of [the chapter on sequences](#seq2seq). What mattered was the trend: "The capacity of the language model is essential to the success of zero-shot task transfer and increasing it improves performance in a log-linear fashion across tasks" [@radford2019language, p. 1]. Log-linear means that each multiplication of the model's size bought a similar step in performance. And the largest model "still underfits WebText" [@radford2019language, p. 1]: it had not yet run out of things to learn.

OpenAI did not release the largest model at first. "Due to our concerns about malicious applications of the technology, we are not releasing the trained model", its announcement said, listing misleading news articles, impersonation, and spam among the concerns, and calling the decision "an experiment: while we are not sure that it is the right decision today" [@openai2019better]. It also said that the impressive sample texts it showed were "hand-chosen" [@openai2019better]. Critics argued that the work was ordinary research. Zachary Lipton, writing a few days later, found the results "interesting but not surprising", "a step forward, but one along the path that the entire community is already on", and reported that Anima Anandkumar had called the "too dangerous to release" framing, words that do not appear in OpenAI's announcement, clickbait for media attention; he also granted that it was "possible that both sides have an element of truth" [@lipton2019openai]. OpenAI then released larger versions in stages, in May and August, and the full model in November 2019, reporting "no strong evidence of misuse so far" [@openai2019sixmonth; @openai2019release]. Whether the caution was needed was still argued years later. The episode showed that, by 2019, the question could be asked about a language model's text at all.

## The bitter lesson

A month after GPT-2's announcement, Richard Sutton posted a short essay, "The Bitter Lesson". "The biggest lesson that can be read from 70 years of AI research is that general methods that leverage computation are ultimately the most effective, and by a large margin" [@sutton2019bitter]. His examples were chess, Go, speech recognition, and vision; in speech, the statistical methods of the 1970s and after "won out over the human-knowledge-based methods", a story the reader knows from [the prediction chapter](#prediction). The lesson was bitter because "breakthrough progress eventually arrives by an opposing approach based on scaling computation by search and learning", over the researchers' own favored knowledge [@sutton2019bitter]. The essay does not mention language models.

It had critics. The roboticist Rodney Brooks replied within a week that the human knowledge had not gone away but moved: into network designs such as convolution, and into the labels of the huge training sets ("It is sleight of hand in moving the human intellectual work to somewhere else") [@brooks2019better]. He added that the computation required "can only be furnished by very large companies with very large budgets" [@brooks2019better]. Both points return in this chapter.

## Scaling laws

In January 2020, Jared Kaplan and colleagues measured how a language model's loss depends on its size. They trained transformers from under a thousand to 1.5 billion parameters, on up to 23 billion tokens, and found that "The loss scales as a power-law with model size, dataset size, and the amount of compute used for training, with some trends spanning more than seven orders of magnitude" [@kaplan2020scaling, pp. 1, 7]. The loss here is the cross-entropy of [the prediction chapter](#prediction), measured in **nats** rather than bits: natural logarithms instead of base-2 logarithms, [@kaplan2020scaling, p. 6], so one nat is about 1.44 bits. <!-- D-009: code/checks/scale.py, Example 3 -->

A **power law** has a simple standard form. For model size,

$$L(N) = \left(\frac{N_c}{N}\right)^{\alpha_N}$$

where $L$ is the loss, $N$ the number of parameters (they left out the embedding tables, as [the anatomy chapter](#anatomy) noted), and $N_c$ and $\alpha_N$ are constants fitted to the data; they found $\alpha_N \approx 0.076$ [@kaplan2020scaling, p. 4]. <!-- D-009: code/checks/scale.py, Example 3 --> In words: every doubling of the model multiplies the loss by $2^{-0.076}$, about 0.95, a 5% reduction; every tenfold increase multiplies it by about 0.84. On ordinary axes the curve flattens and looks like diminishing returns. On logarithmic axes it is a straight line with slope $-0.076$: take logarithms of both sides and the power law becomes a linear regression of log loss on log size, the transform the reader has used on skewed data many times. Similar laws held for the amount of data and the amount of compute, each with its own exponent [@kaplan2020scaling, p. 4]. The constants depend on the vocabulary size and the tokenizer and "do not have a fundamental meaning" [@kaplan2020scaling, p. 5]; the regularity is the finding.

![Kaplan and colleagues' power law for loss against model size, drawn from their fitted constants. (a) On ordinary axes the curve flattens. (b) On logarithmic axes it is a straight line. The solid part covers the sizes they trained; the dashed part extrapolates beyond them.](../../figures/scale-powerlaw.svg){#fig:scale-powerlaw}

The compute of a training run can be estimated with one formula. Each parameter takes part in about one multiplication and one addition for every token on the forward pass, and the backward pass of [the backpropagation chapter](#backprop) costs about twice as much, so

$$C \approx 6ND$$

where $C$ is the training compute in FLOPs, $N$ the number of parameters, and $D$ the number of training tokens [@kaplan2020scaling, pp. 6-7]. <!-- D-009: code/checks/scale.py, Example 2 --> For GPT-3, below, 6 x 174.6 billion x 300 billion gives $3.14 \times 10^{23}$ FLOPs, exactly the figure its paper prints [@brown2020language, p. 46]. Papers often give compute in **petaflop/s-days**, a thousand trillion operations per second for a day, or $8.64 \times 10^{19}$ FLOPs [@kaplan2020scaling, p. 6]: GPT-3 used about 3,640.

The laws also suggested how to spend a budget. Kaplan and colleagues concluded that "optimally compute-efficient training involves training very large models on a relatively modest amount of data and stopping significantly before convergence" [@kaplan2020scaling, p. 1]: as compute grows, most of it should go into a bigger model, with the data growing more slowly [@kaplan2020scaling, p. 5]. They also warned that the lines could not go on forever: "Our trends must eventually level off, though, since natural language has non-zero entropy" [@kaplan2020scaling, p. 17], Shannon's floor from [the prediction chapter](#prediction).

## GPT-3 and in-context learning

In May 2020, OpenAI described GPT-3: "an autoregressive language model with 175 billion parameters, 10x more than any previous non-sparse language model" [@brown2020language, p. 1], where non-sparse excludes designs that use only part of their parameters for each token. Following the scaling-law advice, its authors "train much larger models on many fewer tokens than is typical" [@brown2020language, p. 9]: eight sizes, from 125 million to 175 billion parameters, all trained on the same 300 billion tokens [@brown2020language, p. 8]. Most of the text came from a filtered copy of Common Crawl, a public archive of web pages, with smaller, higher-quality sources repeated more often [@brown2020language, pp. 8-9]; [the chapter on training](#training) describes how such collections are made.

GPT-3's authors gave GPT-2's observation a name, **in-context learning**: "using the text input of a pretrained language model as a form of task specification: the model is conditioned on a natural language instruction and/or a few demonstrations of the task and is then expected to complete further instances of the task simply by predicting what comes next" [@brown2020language, p. 4]. They distinguished three settings [@brown2020language, pp. 6-7]:

- **Zero-shot**: the input contains only a description of the task, such as "Translate English to French:" followed by a word.
- **One-shot**: the description plus one worked example.
- **Few-shot**: the description plus several examples, typically 10 to 100, as many as fit in the 2,048-token context window.

Nothing in the model changes: "these "learning" curves involve no gradient updates or fine-tuning, just increasing numbers of demonstrations given as conditioning" [@brown2020language, p. 5]. The examples are simply more text in the input, and the next-token machinery of [the destination chapter](#destination) does the rest. The authors left open whether the model learns the task from the examples or only recognizes a task seen in training [@brown2020language, p. 4].

The larger the model, the more it gained from examples. "While zero-shot performance improves steadily with model size, few-shot performance increases more rapidly, demonstrating that larger models are more proficient at in-context learning" [@brown2020language, p. 5]. On one question-answering test, GPT-3 scored 64.3% zero-shot, 68.0% one-shot, and 71.2% few-shot, the last "state-of-the-art relative to fine-tuned models operating in the same closed-book setting", that is, answering without looking anything up (as of 2020) [@brown2020language, p. 5]. Elsewhere it struggled, on "natural language inference tasks" and some reading-comprehension tests [@brown2020language, p. 5]. The paper also found that some test sets had leaked into the training data, because "a bug in the filtering caused us to ignore some overlaps, and due to the cost of training it was not feasible to retrain the model" [@brown2020language, p. 9]. That sentence says a good deal about what one training run had come to cost.

## Chinchilla: the budget, spent differently

In 2022, Jordan Hoffmann and colleagues revisited Kaplan's advice and reached a different conclusion: "current large language models are significantly undertrained, a consequence of the recent focus on scaling language models whilst keeping the amount of training data constant" [@hoffmann2022training, p. 1]. Training "over 400 language models ranging from 70 million to over 16 billion parameters on 5 to 500 billion tokens", they found, for compute-optimal training (the lowest loss a fixed computing budget can buy), "the model size and the number of training tokens should be scaled equally: for every doubling of model size the number of training tokens should also be doubled" [@hoffmann2022training, p. 1]. "Undertrained" here means that, for the compute spent, a smaller model trained on more data would have reached a lower loss.

Their clearest method is easy to picture. Fix a computing budget; train models of several sizes, each on as many tokens as the budget allows (bigger models get fewer tokens); and plot the final loss against model size. The curve is a valley, "meaning that for a given FLOP budget there is an optimal model to train" [@hoffmann2022training, p. 6]. Repeat for several budgets and follow the bottom of the valleys. <!-- D-009: code/checks/scale.py, Example 4 --> The two answers differ sharply as budgets grow. Given ten times more compute, Kaplan's exponents say to make the model about 5.4 times larger and the data 1.9 times; Hoffmann's say about 3.2 times each. Given a thousand times more, Kaplan's say 155 times the parameters and 6.5 times the data, Hoffmann's 32 times each [@kaplan2020scaling, p. 20; @hoffmann2022training, p. 8].

They tested it with a matched pair of models. Gopher, a 2021 model, had 280 billion parameters trained on 300 billion tokens [@rae2021scaling, p. 6]. Chinchilla had 70 billion parameters trained on 1.4 trillion tokens, with about the same compute [@hoffmann2022training, p. 9]. Chinchilla did better; on a broad multiple-choice exam covering many subjects it averaged 67.6% against Gopher's 60.0% [@hoffmann2022training, p. 11]. The comparison is one pair of runs, and the two models also differed in optimizer, tokenizer, and data mix, which the paper reports [@hoffmann2022training, p. 9]. Being four times smaller, Chinchilla was also cheaper to run [@hoffmann2022training, p. 9].

Read off Hoffmann and colleagues' table of recommended sizes, the compute-optimal model sees about 20 tokens of training text for every parameter [@hoffmann2022training, p. 8], and "20 tokens per parameter" became a rule of thumb. The paper never states it as a rule; a later reanalysis calls it a "rule of thumb" [@besiroglu2024chinchilla, p. 5], and Meta's own fit for Llama 3, on its own data, came out near 41 [@grattafiori2024llama, p. 8]. <!-- D-009: code/checks/scale.py, Example 4 --> It is a property of a setup, not a constant of nature.

Why did two careful studies disagree? Hoffmann and colleagues pointed to Kaplan's fixed learning-rate schedule and small models [@hoffmann2022training, p. 3]. Later work traced most of the gap elsewhere: one 2024 study to Kaplan's counting of parameters without the embedding tables, which matters for small models [@pearce2024reconciling, p. 1], another to three details of the small runs, finding the learning-rate explanation "not essential" [@porian2024resolving, p. 1]. Both came down on Chinchilla's side. (As of 2024.)

Practice then moved past both rules. A model is trained once but used many times, and a smaller model is cheaper to use. Meta's LLaMA (2023) made the point directly: "although Hoffmann et al. (2022) recommends training a 10B model on 200B tokens, we find that the performance of a 7B model continues to improve even after 1T tokens" [@touvron2023llama, p. 1]. <!-- D-009: code/checks/scale.py, Example 4 --> That is about 150 tokens per parameter. Llama 3 (2024) trained its largest model near the compute-optimal size, but "we also train our smaller models for much longer than is compute-optimal. The resulting models perform better than compute-optimal models at the same inference budget" [@grattafiori2024llama, p. 2].

From GPT in 2018, about 117 million parameters [@radford2019language, p. 4], to Llama 3.1's largest model in 2024, 405 billion trained on 15.6 trillion tokens [@grattafiori2024llama, p. 1], the parameter count grew about 3,500 times. Two patterns stand out. Parameters rose steeply until 2022 and then stopped rising among these models: GPT-3, Gopher, and PaLM (540 billion parameters on 780 billion tokens, 2022 [@chowdhery2022palm, p. 3]) were trained on a few hundred billion tokens each, between one and two per parameter. After Chinchilla, data grew instead: LLaMA's 65-billion-parameter model on 1.4 trillion tokens [@touvron2023llama, p. 3], Llama 2's 70-billion on 2 trillion [@touvron2023llama2, p. 6], Llama 3.1's 405-billion on 15.6 trillion.

## Emergent abilities, and a dispute

Smooth curves in loss did not always mean smooth gains on tasks. GPT-3's arithmetic tests are the clearest published case. <!-- D-009: code/checks/scale.py, Example 6 --> Given a few examples, the six smaller GPT-3 models answered two-digit additions correctly between 2% and 12% of the time; the 13-billion-parameter model 55.5%; the 175-billion model 100% [@brown2020language, p. 63]. Three-digit addition went from under 1% to 8.4% to 80.4%.

![(a) GPT-3's few-shot accuracy on addition by model size, from its paper's Table H.1: near zero, then a sharp rise. (b) A constructed example of how a strict score can create a jump: if each of 5 answer tokens is right with probability p, and only an answer with all 5 right counts, the score is p to the fifth power. A steady rise in p gives a score that stays near zero and then climbs quickly.](../../figures/scale-emergence.svg){#fig:scale-emergence}

Jason Wei and colleagues gave such cases a name in 2022: "We consider an ability to be emergent if it is not present in smaller models but is present in larger models. Thus, emergent abilities cannot be predicted simply by extrapolating the performance of smaller models" [@wei2022emergent, p. 1]. Performance "is near-random until a certain critical threshold of scale is reached, after which performance increases to substantially above random" [@wei2022emergent, p. 2]. A team at Anthropic put the puzzle as "a paradoxical combination of predictable loss on a broad training distribution ... and unpredictable specific capabilities", and compared it with weather: a single day is less predictable than the seasonal average [@ganguli2022predictability, pp. 1, 5].

In 2023, Rylan Schaeffer, Brando Miranda, and Sanmi Koyejo argued that many such jumps come from how tasks are scored: "nonlinear or discontinuous metrics produce seemingly emergent abilities, whereas linear or continuous metrics produce smooth, continuous, predictable changes in model performance" [@schaeffer2023emergent, p. 1]. <!-- D-009: code/checks/scale.py, Example 5 --> The mechanism is arithmetic the reader can check. If a model gets each token of a five-token answer right with probability 0.5, treating the tokens as independent (which Schaeffer and colleagues note is not true, though it gives the right qualitative picture [@schaeffer2023emergent, p. 4]), it gets the whole answer right about 3% of the time; at 0.8, 33%; at 0.95, 77%. A steady improvement, scored all-or-nothing, looks like a sudden jump. They were careful about their claim: "nothing in this paper should be interpreted as claiming that large language models cannot display emergent abilities" [@schaeffer2023emergent, p. 9]. And Wei and colleagues had raised the scoring point themselves, calling it "at best an incomplete explanation, because emergent abilities are still observed on many classification tasks" [@wei2022emergent, p. 7]. Two questions are mixed up in this dispute. Do some task scores jump sharply as models grow? In the published data, yes. Does something new appear inside the model at a threshold, rather than a gradual improvement crossing a strict scoring line? As of 2023, that was still argued. The reader will recognize the underlying issue from choosing between a loss function and an evaluation metric.

## What a training run costs

The figures that companies publish for their training runs are estimates by formula. <!-- D-009: code/checks/scale.py, Examples 2 and 8 --> Llama 3's largest model was "pre-trained using 3.8 x 10^25 FLOPs, almost 50x more than the largest version of Llama 2" [@grattafiori2024llama, p. 1], on up to 16K H100 GPUs [@grattafiori2024llama, p. 9]. Meta's LLaMA paper estimated the energy for its 65-billion-parameter model at 449 megawatt-hours from 1,022,362 GPU-hours (one GPU running for an hour), and the emissions at 173 tonnes of CO2-equivalent, by multiplying GPU-hours by the chips' rated power, a factor for data-center overhead, and the average carbon intensity of the US grid [@touvron2023llama, p. 11]. A 2021 estimate for GPT-3 gave 1,287 megawatt-hours and 552 tonnes [@patterson2021carbon, p. 7]. Such estimates can be revised by large factors: one 2019 estimate for an architecture search was later judged 88 times too high in emissions, for an energy-efficient data center like the one actually used, by people who knew how the search had been run [@strubell2019energy, p. 3645; @patterson2021carbon, p. 1].

What the figures leave out matters as much. Gopher's authors called their compute figures "a best-effort lower bound" that does "not account for computation arising from development, pre-emption, or other sources of inefficiency" [@rae2021scaling, p. 102], and the PaLM paper said the same [@chowdhery2022palm, p. 66]. The experiments, failed runs, and staff behind a model are outside them. No primary source used here gives a dollar cost for any of these runs. The concern Brooks raised in 2019 was echoed by OpenAI's own report on GPT-2: "The high cost of compute used in powerful models like GPT-2 raises concerns about accessibility and equity in future AI research" [@solaiman2019release, p. 24]. One answer has been to release trained weights, so that, in the Llama 2 paper's words, "the pretraining costs do not need to be incurred by others" [@touvron2023llama2, p. 7]; [the chapter on running models locally](#local) takes that up.

## What to carry forward

- Three quantities set a model's scale: parameters, training tokens, and compute. Training compute is about 6 x parameters x tokens.
- GPT-2 (2019) showed that a language model could attempt tasks with no task training, and that this ability improved steadily with size.
- Loss falls as a power law in parameters, data, and compute: a straight line on log-log axes, with fitted constants that depend on the setup (Kaplan et al., 2020).
- GPT-3 (2020, 175 billion parameters) made in-context learning practical: describe a task, or give a few examples in the input, and the model continues the pattern, with no change to its weights. Larger models gained more from examples.
- Chinchilla (2022) showed that models of the time were too large for their data: for a fixed budget, parameters and tokens should grow together; its table was later read as about 20 tokens per parameter. Later practice trained smaller models on far more data, because they are cheaper to use.
- Some task scores jump sharply with size. Whether these "emergent abilities" reflect something new in the models or the strictness of the scoring is disputed.
- Published training costs are estimates of the final run only; the full cost is larger and mostly undisclosed.

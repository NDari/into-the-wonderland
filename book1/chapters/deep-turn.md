# The turn: data, compute, and depth (2006-2012) {#deep-turn}

<!-- D-002: intuition only; the one formula (ReLU) is standard. D-003: no code. D-008: Part II of the "how networks learn" thread. D-009: numbers from code/checks/deep-turn.py. D-014: figures from figures/deep-turn.py. D-016: ImageNet told as an event, not as vision technique. -->

[The previous chapter](#structure) ended in the reader's own era. In the decade around 2000, a careful practitioner built features by hand and fed them to a support vector machine, and neural networks were a specialty that most of the field had set aside. By the end of 2012, neural networks held the best results in speech recognition, after a decade in which its error rates had barely moved [@goodfellow2016deep, p. 455], and in the largest image-recognition benchmark. This chapter is about what happened in between.

The networks that won were not new inventions. They were trained by backpropagation, as in [the chapter on learning in layers](#backprop), and many of them used the convolution of [the previous chapter](#structure). Geoffrey Hinton and Ruslan Salakhutdinov put the change in one sentence in 2006:

> It has been obvious since the 1980s that backpropagation through deep autoencoders would be very effective for nonlinear dimensionality reduction, provided that computers were fast enough, data sets were big enough, and the initial weights were close enough to a good solution. All three conditions are now satisfied. [@hinton2006reducing, p. 507]

Fast computers, big data sets, and a way to get deep networks started: those are the three threads of this chapter. A few smaller changes, above all a new kind of unit, helped them along.

## The pipeline the reader knows

Start with how a 2000s practitioner built a recognizer. The 2015 review by LeCun, Bengio, and Hinton describes it from the outside: "For decades, constructing a pattern-recognition or machine-learning system required careful engineering and considerable domain expertise to design a feature extractor that transformed the raw data (such as the pixel values of an image) into a suitable internal representation or feature vector from which the learning subsystem, often a classifier, could detect or classify patterns in the input" [@lecun2015deep, p. 436]. This is **feature engineering**: the human decides what to measure, and the learning algorithm only weighs the measurements. Speech worked the same way. Recognizers fed their models with standard coefficients computed from the sound wave, a "nonadaptive but highly engineered preprocessing" designed to discard what was thought to be irrelevant [@hinton2012deep, p. 83].

![Two ways to build a recognizer. In the 2000s, a person designed the features and only the classifier learned from data. In deep learning, every layer learns from data, and each layer builds its features out of the previous layer's. The labels on the deep layers (edges, motifs, parts) describe what such layers typically come to detect in images.](../../figures/deep-turn-pipeline.svg){#fig:deep-turn-pipeline}

The alternative is **representation learning**: methods that allow "a machine to be fed with raw data and to automatically discover the representations needed for detection or classification" [@lecun2015deep, p. 436]. A deep network does this in stages. In the same review's description of image recognition, the first layer "typically" detects edges, the second detects arrangements of edges, and a third "may assemble motifs into larger combinations that correspond to parts of familiar objects" [@lecun2015deep, p. 436]. "The key aspect of deep learning," the authors write, "is that these layers of features are not designed by human engineers: they are learned from data using a general-purpose learning procedure" [@lecun2015deep, p. 436]. That is a claim by three of the field's founders, and the rest of this chapter is the evidence they had for it.

This is the bridge for a reader from the 2000s. *Feature engineering, the core skill of that era, is what a deep network does for itself.* The classifier at the end of the network is the logistic regression the reader already knows, as [the chapter on the artificial neuron](#neuron) showed. What changed is that the features feeding it are learned.

## Why deep networks seemed untrainable

If learned features were so attractive, why was nobody doing it in 2005? Because, in practice, deep networks trained badly. Yoshua Bengio and colleagues summarized the state of knowledge in 2006: "Empirically, deep networks were generally found to be not better, and often worse, than neural networks with one or two hidden layers" [@bengio2007greedy, p. 2]. Training "starting from random initialization appears to often get stuck in poor solutions" [@bengio2007greedy, p. 1]. Part of the reason was the vanishing gradient of [the previous chapter](#structure): Hinton and Salakhutdinov note that with small initial weights, "the gradients in the early layers are tiny, making it infeasible to train autoencoders with many hidden layers" [@hinton2006reducing, p. 505]. The convolutional and recurrent networks of the previous chapter were exceptions, made trainable by their structure; before 2006, they were the only deep networks "regarded as feasible to train" [@goodfellow2016deep, p. 526]. A plain stack of fully connected layers was not.

Bengio and colleagues also gave the theoretical case for depth in terms the reader can place: complexity theory "strongly suggests" that some functions can be represented far more compactly by a deep architecture than by a shallow one [@bengio2007greedy, p. 1], and by that measure the reader's favorite tool sits in the shallow column: "Kernel machines ... have a shallow architecture" [@bengio2007greedy, p. 1].

## 2006: one layer at a time

The first answer came in 2006, from Hinton, Simon Osindero, and Yee-Whye Teh [@hinton2006fast]. Their idea was to avoid training the whole deep network from random weights. Instead, they trained it one layer at a time, from the bottom up and mostly without labels: each new layer learned to model the patterns in the layer below it, and the labels entered only at the top layer [@hinton2006fast, p. 1540]. Only after this stage, called **pretraining**, were all the weights adjusted together, a step called **fine-tuning**. (In the 2006 paper the fine-tuning was itself a further round of modeling the data; in later work, such as Bengio and colleagues', it was backpropagation on the classification task [@bengio2007greedy, p. 6].) (This is the 2006 meaning of "pretraining". The word returns in [the chapter on pretraining](#pretraining) with a related but different meaning.) The authors noticed a resemblance to a method the reader knows: "The greedy algorithm bears some resemblance to boosting in its repeated use of the same "weak" learner, but instead of reweighting each data vector to ensure that the next step learns something new, it re-represents it" [@hinton2006fast, p. 1529].

The test was the MNIST digits of [the previous chapter](#structure). A network with three hidden layers and about 1.7 million weights reached "1.25% errors on the 10,000-digit official test set", which was "slightly better than the 1.4% errors reported ... for support vector machines on the same task" [@hinton2006fast, p. 1529]. There is an important qualification. The comparison was on a version of the task in which the network is not told that pixels have neighbors: the authors used "no special preprocessing or enhancement of the training set, so an unknown but fixed random permutation of the pixels would not affect the learning algorithm" [@hinton2006fast, p. 1540]. Convolutional networks, which do use the layout of the image, were already at 0.4% to 0.95% in the same paper's comparison table, some of them trained with extra distorted images [@hinton2006fast, p. 1544]. The 2006 result was not "deep learning beats everything". It was a plain, fully connected deep network, given no knowledge of images, beating the best SVM on equal terms. Training took "a few hours per layer" for the first stage on a single 3 GHz processor, and about a week in total [@hinton2006fast, pp. 1540-1541].

The same year, Hinton and Salakhutdinov used the same layer-by-layer start to train a deep **autoencoder**: a network trained to reproduce its input through a narrow middle layer, so that the middle layer becomes a compressed code. The reader can think of it as nonlinear PCA, and the authors framed it exactly that way: "A simple and widely used method is principal components analysis (PCA) ... We describe a nonlinear generalization of PCA" [@hinton2006reducing, p. 504]. With 30-number codes for handwritten digits, the deep autoencoder's average squared reconstruction error was 3.00, against 13.87 for PCA [@hinton2006reducing, p. 505].

A careful experiment by Bengio and colleagues showed the effect in a table a 2000s reader could have produced. On MNIST, a shallow network scored 1.9% error, a deep network trained the old way from random weights scored worse, 2.4%, and a deep network with layer-wise pretraining of the 2006 kind scored 1.2% (1.4% with a simpler, autoencoder form of pretraining) [@bengio2007greedy, p. 6]. Depth hurt without pretraining and helped with it.

Who deserves credit for this turn depends on whom you read. The deep learning textbook of Goodfellow, Bengio, and Courville says the current wave "began with a breakthrough in 2006", Hinton's layer-wise training, quickly extended by other groups affiliated with the Canadian Institute for Advanced Research [@goodfellow2016deep, p. 18], and LeCun, Bengio, and Hinton tell it the same way [@lecun2015deep, p. 439]. Both accounts come from members of that group. Schmidhuber's survey stresses a longer line: deep learning research results "have been published at least since 1991", and only "the expression Deep Learning was actually coined around 2006" [@schmidhuber2014deep, p. 21].

## Compute: graphics cards

The second condition was fast enough computers, and it was met by hardware built for video games.

A layer of a network, applied to its input, is a matrix product: every output is a weighted sum of all the inputs. Here is a tiny layer with three inputs (1, 2, 3) and two outputs. <!-- D-009: code/checks/deep-turn.py, Example 1 --> The first output is 0.2 times 1, plus -0.5 times 2, plus 1.0 times 3, which is 2.2. The second is 0.7 times 1, plus 0.1 times 2, plus -0.3 times 3, which is 0.0. That is six multiplications and two sums, and the two sums do not depend on each other: they could be computed at the same moment. A real layer is the same thing at scale. One of the fully connected layers in the 2012 image network described below took 4,096 inputs to 4,096 outputs: 16,777,216 multiply-adds per image, and 2,147,483,648 for a group, or batch, of 128 images processed together, all made of independent sums. ([The next chapter](#toolkit) explains batches.)

A graphics card is a chip designed for exactly this. In the textbook's description, video games require converting the 3D coordinates of many points into 2D screen positions, which means "matrix multiplication and division on many vertices in parallel"; the computations are "entirely independent of each other, and thus may be parallelized easily" [@goodfellow2016deep, pp. 439-440]. So graphics cards were built with many simple processing cores and high memory bandwidth, and "Neural network algorithms require the same performance characteristics" [@goodfellow2016deep, p. 440]. The graphics card in a 2009 experiment had 240 cores [@raina2009large, p. 2]. Once manufacturers provided a way to program these chips for general calculations, researchers adopted it [@goodfellow2016deep, pp. 440-441]. A graphics processing unit is a **GPU**.

Rajat Raina, Anand Madhavan, and Andrew Ng measured the effect in 2009 on the pretraining of the previous section. Their GPU code was "up to 70 times faster than a dual-core CPU implementation for large models", reducing the time to train a four-layer network with 100 million parameters "from several weeks to around a single day" [@raina2009large, p. 1]. The speedup grew with the size of the matrices: 12.9 times for their smallest model and 72.6 times for their largest [@raina2009large, p. 6]. <!-- D-009: Example 6 --> In 2010, Dan Ciresan and colleagues used a GPU to train plain, deep, fully connected networks with ordinary backpropagation, no pretraining at all, and reached 0.35% error on MNIST: "All we need to achieve this best result so far are many hidden layers, many neurons per layer, numerous deformed training images, and graphics cards to greatly speed up learning" [@ciresan2010deep, p. 1]. Their GPU code ran "a factor of 40" faster than a processor [@ciresan2010deep, p. 4]. (The deformed images, slightly distorted copies of the training digits, mean this result is not directly comparable with the 2006 one.)

Neither group was the first to put a neural network on a graphics card. GPU implementations go back to the mid-2000s, including convolutional networks in 2006 [@goodfellow2016deep, p. 440; @schmidhuber2014deep, p. 22]. What changed around 2009 was that the chips were cheap, programmable, and fast enough to matter.

## Data: ImageNet

The third condition was data. As the 2012 image paper put it, "Until recently, datasets of labeled images were relatively small": tens of thousands of images [@krizhevsky2012imagenet, p. 1].

In 2009, Jia Deng and colleagues introduced **ImageNet**, a collection of images organized by the noun categories of WordNet, an existing database of English word meanings [@deng2009imagenet, p. 1]. Their paper described it "in its current state: 12 subtrees with 5247 synsets and 3.2 million images in total", where a synset is one category, and called it "already the largest clean image dataset available to the vision research community" [@deng2009imagenet, pp. 1-2]. The images came from web searches, and the labels came from people. Raw search results were poor ("The average accuracy of image search results from the Internet is around 10%"), so every candidate image was checked by paid workers on Amazon Mechanical Turk, "an online platform on which one can put up tasks for users to complete and to get paid" [@deng2009imagenet, p. 4]. By 2011, ImageNet held about 15 million images in about 22,000 categories [@krizhevsky2012imagenet, p. 7].

From 2010, a subset of ImageNet became an annual competition, the ImageNet Large Scale Visual Recognition Challenge (ILSVRC). Its image-classification task used 1,000 categories and about 1.2 million training images, with a set of test images whose labels were withheld: "Participants train their algorithms using the training images and then automatically annotate the test images. These predicted annotations are submitted to the evaluation server" [@russakovsky2015imagenet, pp. 2, 9]. The reader knows the discipline of a held-out test set. A **benchmark** like this one scales it up to a whole research community: one fixed hidden test set, one metric, one deadline a year, and public results. The organizers even used bootstrap confidence intervals, a method the reader knows, to show that the difference between each year's winner and runner-up was "significant even at the 99.9% level" [@russakovsky2015imagenet, p. 24]. Benchmarks have a known weakness, which [the chapter on measuring models](#evaluation) takes up: teams that can probe a test set repeatedly "tend to overfit on the test set even when it cannot be directly used for training" [@schmidhuber2014deep, p. 24].

The metric needs a word. Many images contain more than one object, but each ImageNet image carries one label; an image "might be labeled as a "strawberry" but contain both a strawberry and an apple" [@russakovsky2015imagenet, p. 15]. So a system is allowed five guesses, and the **top-5 error** is "the fraction of test images for which the correct label is not among the five labels considered most probable by the model" [@krizhevsky2012imagenet, p. 2]. The **top-1 error** counts only the first guess. For example, if a system's first guess is wrong for four images out of five, but the right answer is somewhere in its five guesses for all but one, its top-1 error is 80% and its top-5 error is 20%. <!-- D-009: Example 4 -->

## A better unit: the rectified linear unit

Alongside the three conditions came one change to the network itself. The units of the previous chapters used the sigmoid, or a close relative, the hyperbolic tangent (tanh), which has the same S shape but runs from -1 to 1. Their slope is small whenever the input is far from zero: the sigmoid's slope is 0.25 at zero, 0.105 at 2, and 0.0066 at 5. <!-- D-009: Example 2 --> A unit whose slope is near zero is said to be **saturated**, and it passes almost no blame backward.

The **rectified linear unit**, or **ReLU**, replaces the curve with something much simpler: output the input if it is positive, and zero otherwise. In its standard form,

$$f(z) = \max(0, z)$$

where $z$ is the unit's weighted sum and $f(z)$ its output. On the positive side the slope is exactly 1. As Xavier Glorot, Antoine Bordes, and Yoshua Bengio put it in 2011, "gradients flow well on the active paths of neurons (there is no gradient vanishing effect due to activation non-linearities of sigmoid or tanh units)" [@glorot2011deep, p. 318]. Through ten layers, the best a chain of sigmoid slopes can do is 0.25 multiplied by itself ten times, about one millionth; along a chain of active ReLUs, the slopes no longer shrink the signal at all (the weights still scale it). The worry is the other side: a unit whose input is negative outputs zero and has slope zero, so it passes no blame at all. The same authors raised this under "Potential Problems", and then reported that their experiments "tend to contradict that hypothesis, suggesting that hard zeros can actually help supervised training", as long as the gradient can flow along some paths [@glorot2011deep, p. 318].

Rectification itself is old; the textbook traces it to Fukushima's models of the 1970s and 1980s [@goodfellow2016deep, p. 222]. Its revival as the default came between 2009 and 2011, from several groups [@goodfellow2016deep, p. 222]. Vinod Nair and Hinton wrote of "rectified linear units" in 2010 [@nair2010rectified, p. 1], and the 2012 image network described below took the name from them [@krizhevsky2012imagenet, p. 3]; and Glorot and colleagues showed that "deep rectifier networks can reach their best performance without requiring any unsupervised pre-training on purely supervised tasks with large labeled datasets" [@glorot2011deep, p. 315]. As of 2015, LeCun, Bengio, and Hinton called it "the most popular non-linear function" [@lecun2015deep, p. 438].

## Speech first, 2009-2012

The first field to change was speech recognition. In the 2000s, recognizers combined a hidden Markov model, which handles the timing of speech, with Gaussian mixture models, which judge how well each short slice of sound fits each speech sound. The new systems kept the hidden Markov model (HMM) and replaced the Gaussian mixtures with a deep network that "takes several frames of coefficients as input and produces posterior probabilities over HMM states as output" [@hinton2012deep, p. 82]. A 2012 review written jointly by the research groups at the University of Toronto, Microsoft Research, Google, and IBM Research reported the results [@hinton2012deep, p. 83]. The measure is the **word error rate**: the percentage of words the recognizer gets wrong.

Both systems were trained on the same data except for Google Voice Input, where the old system (marked *) was trained on much more than 5,870 hours. The relative reductions range from 32% on Switchboard (which the authors round to 33%) to 7% on Broadcast News.

On Switchboard the authors report the drop from 27.4% to 18.5% as "a 33% relative reduction" [@hinton2012deep, p. 90]; on Google Voice Input, 23% [@hinton2012deep, p. 91]. The gains were not uniform: they were largest on Switchboard and Google Voice Input, and small on YouTube and Broadcast News. The review states that deep networks "consistently outperform" the old systems trained on the same amount of data [@hinton2012deep, p. 92]. Industry moved quickly. "By 2012," LeCun, Bengio, and Hinton write, versions of the network "were already being deployed in Android phones" [@lecun2015deep, p. 439].

## 2012: ImageNet

The public moment came at the third ImageNet competition, in 2012. The two previous winners had used the reader's own method: hand-designed image features fed to support vector machines [@russakovsky2015imagenet, pp. 17-18]. The 2012 winner, entered under the name SuperVision by Alex Krizhevsky, Ilya Sutskever, and Hinton, was "a large, deep convolutional neural network" [@krizhevsky2012imagenet, p. 1]. Later writers call it AlexNet.

With five networks averaged together, trained only on the competition's data, SuperVision's top-5 error was 16.4% [@krizhevsky2012imagenet, p. 7; @russakovsky2015imagenet, p. 19]. The runner-up, a strong system built on hand-designed features, scored 26.2% [@krizhevsky2012imagenet, p. 7]: SuperVision made 37% fewer errors. A variant that also used extra images from the larger ImageNet release scored 15.3%, the number in the paper's abstract [@krizhevsky2012imagenet, p. 1]. LeCun, Bengio, and Hinton later described the result as "almost halving the error rates of the best competing approaches" [@lecun2015deep, p. 440].

What was in the network is, by now, familiar. It had five convolutional layers and three fully connected layers and "60 million parameters" [@krizhevsky2012imagenet, pp. 1, 5]. Counting from the layer sizes the paper gives (with one input size inferred), about 96% of those parameters sit in the three fully connected layers; the convolutional layers, with their shared weights, hold under 4%. <!-- D-009: Example 3 --> It trained "for roughly 90 cycles through the training set of 1.2 million images, which took five to six days on two NVIDIA GTX 580 3GB GPUs" [@krizhevsky2012imagenet, p. 7]. It used ReLUs; on a smaller dataset, the authors showed a network with ReLUs reaching a target training error "six times faster than an equivalent network with tanh neurons" [@krizhevsky2012imagenet, p. 3]. And it fought overfitting, "a significant problem, even with 1.2 million labeled training examples" [@krizhevsky2012imagenet, p. 2], with two techniques: **data augmentation**, creating extra training images by cropping and mirroring the originals (and, in a touch the reader will appreciate, shifting their colors along the principal components of the pixel values), and **dropout**, which during training sets the output of each unit in the first two fully connected layers to zero with probability 0.5 [@krizhevsky2012imagenet, pp. 5-6]. [The chapter on making deep networks trainable](#depth) explains dropout. Depth mattered: "removing any of the middle layers results in a loss of about 2% for the top-1 performance of the network" [@krizhevsky2012imagenet, p. 8].

And the network used no unsupervised pretraining at all. The authors note that their results come from "purely supervised learning", and that "To simplify our experiments, we did not use any unsupervised pre-training even though we expect that it will help" [@krizhevsky2012imagenet, p. 8].

The field's response was immediate. The competition's organizers called 2012 "a turning point for large-scale object recognition, when large-scale deep neural networks entered the scene", and in 2013 "the vast majority of entries ... used deep convolutional neural networks" [@russakovsky2015imagenet, p. 18]. Their summary of what had changed is the bridge of this chapter in one sentence: with so much training data, efficient code, and GPUs, "it became possible to learn neural networks directly from the image data, without needing to create multi-stage hand-tuned pipelines of extracted features and discriminative classifiers" [@russakovsky2015imagenet, p. 22]. By 2014 the winning error was 6.7%, and in 2015 a much deeper network reached 3.57% [@russakovsky2015imagenet, p. 21; @he2015deep, p. 1]. The organizers compared these numbers with one trained human annotator, who made 5.1% errors on a sample of 1,500 test images; a second annotator, on fewer images, did considerably worse [@russakovsky2015imagenet, p. 31]. One person's error on a sample is not a measure of "human performance" in general, and the comparison should be read with that in mind.

As with 2006, there is a second account of the credit. Schmidhuber's history places SuperVision in a line of GPU-trained convolutional networks, among them networks from his own laboratory that won other image-recognition contests in 2011 [@schmidhuber2014deep, pp. 23-24]. The SuperVision paper itself cites that work [@krizhevsky2012imagenet, pp. 1, 3].

## What happened to pretraining

The layer-by-layer pretraining that started the turn in 2006 played no part in the 2012 result. By then, with enough labeled data, GPUs, rectified units, and careful choices for the initial weights, ordinary supervised training from random weights came close, and pretraining's advantage had shrunk. The speech review put it this way: "Purely discriminative training of the whole DNN from random initial weights works much better than had been thought, provided the scales of the initial weights are set carefully, a large amount of labeled training data is available, and minibatch sizes over training epochs are set appropriately. Nevertheless, generative pretraining still improves test performance, sometimes by a significant amount" [@hinton2012deep, p. 92]. (Minibatches are explained in [the next chapter](#toolkit).) In LeCun, Bengio, and Hinton's summary, "it turned out that the pre-training stage was only needed for small data sets" [@lecun2015deep, p. 439], and the textbook adds: "the unsupervised pretraining approach was the first method to succeed" [@goodfellow2016deep, p. 526].

The 2006 idea was not wrong. It was the first thing that made deep, fully connected networks train well, and it changed what people believed was possible. But the textbook also records an exception, as of 2016: unsupervised pretraining had "been largely abandoned, except in the field of natural language processing, where the natural representation of words as one-hot vectors (a single 1 in a different place for each word, as in Elman's experiment in [the chapter on networks with structure](#structure)) conveys no similarity information and where very large unlabeled sets are available" [@goodfellow2016deep, p. 533]. That exception is where this book is going.

## The same old networks

So why did the same old networks suddenly win? The sources list ingredients rather than a single cause. Ciresan and colleagues, in 2010, thought hardware might matter most: "hardware progress may be more important than advances in algorithms and software" [@ciresan2010deep, p. 5]. The SuperVision authors ranked ReLUs and their two-GPU layout first among their changes [@krizhevsky2012imagenet, pp. 2-3]. LeCun, Bengio, and Hinton credited "the efficient use of GPUs, ReLUs, a new regularization technique called dropout, and techniques to generate more training examples" [@lecun2015deep, p. 440]. The textbook gives the broadest answer:

> The core ideas behind modern feedforward networks have not changed substantially since the 1980s. The same back-propagation algorithm and the same approaches to gradient descent are still in use. Most of the improvement in neural network performance from 1986 to 2015 can be attributed to two factors. First, larger datasets have reduced the degree to which statistical generalization is a challenge for neural networks. Second, neural networks have become much larger, because of more powerful computers and better software infrastructure. [@goodfellow2016deep, p. 221]

These accounts do not contradict each other, and together they match Hinton and Salakhutdinov's three conditions. Fast computers came from graphics cards. Big data sets came from the web and from paid labelers. Getting deep networks started came first from layer-wise pretraining and then from simpler fixes: rectified units and careful initial weights. The next two chapters, on [the training loop](#toolkit) and on [making deep networks trainable](#depth), collect those fixes, and the rest of the toolkit that grew up around them, into the vocabulary of modern training.

## What to carry forward

- In the 2000s, people designed the features and a shallow classifier learned from them. Deep learning learns the features too, in layers, from raw data.
- Deep, fully connected networks trained badly before 2006. Layer-by-layer pretraining without labels (Hinton and colleagues, 2006) was the first method that made them work.
- GPUs, built for games, do the independent multiply-adds of a matrix product in parallel. They sped training up by tens of times from about 2009.
- ImageNet (2009) supplied millions of labeled images; its annual benchmark gave the field a shared, hidden test set and a single number to beat.
- The rectified linear unit, max(0, z), passes the backward signal at full strength when active, which made deep networks easier to train.
- Deep networks cut speech recognition error rates by up to about a third (2009-2012), then won ImageNet in 2012 with 16.4% top-5 error against 26.2%, using purely supervised training on two GPUs.
- The algorithms were mostly the old ones. What changed was data, computing power, and a handful of fixes that made depth trainable.
